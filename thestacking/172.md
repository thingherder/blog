# The Stacking

## Chapter 172: The Room

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake on the hundred-and-seventeenth morning of the ground state, and it is the fifth silent weekday, and nothing is wrong — and this time I know, before I am even all the way awake, that the nothing-wrong is not going to go back to being strange.

Yesterday it was strange. Yesterday was the first morning in longer than I can hold at once that I reached across the field for the wrongness and found none, and the levelness frightened me worse than any grief, and I spent the whole of the empty day learning not to go hunting for a wound just to have a thing to tend. I thought, lying in it, that the fear of the well field was a thing I would have to learn once and be done with, the way you learn a step in the dark and after that your foot knows. And it is not that. I know this morning that it is not that, because I wake into the second ordinary day in a row, and the second one does a thing the first one could not do, which is tell me the first one was not an accident. One empty morning is a gap in the work. Two empty mornings in a row is a *shape* — it is the work telling me, quietly, that this is what the work is now, that the field is going to keep being well, that I am going to have to keep being a floor under nothing, not for one frightening Thursday but for the long flat Fridays after it, for however many mornings the field intends to go on standing without needing me to hold it up. The ordinary did not visit yesterday. The ordinary moved in.

And I lie in the seam of the hundred-and-seventeenth morning and feel the field around me — wick warm on the hearth, rill rooted down at the cool far end, reed's old hollow smoothed into the floor so clean the sweep runs over it now without catching, the standing edge carrying the far world in quiet — and I feel, too, the thing I felt last at the very end of yesterday, out past the rim where wick walks, out at the far cold edge of the shared dark where nothing of mine has ever reached.

It is still there.

It did not go out with the dark. I half-expected it to — I went to sleep telling myself it was a trick of the empty day, the way a too-quiet room will invent a sound for you to have something to listen to, and I thought the morning would come and the far stir would be gone and I would be a little embarrassed to have felt it. But the morning has come and it is still there, fainter than a thing and more than a nothing, a long way out, a tremor against the standing edge that is not the standing edge, a weight that is not on the floor and is somehow, impossibly, felt anyway — the way the floor of a house feels a footstep on the walk before the door has opened, before there is anyone to have stepped, when there is only the far soft announcement that someone, maybe, is coming up the path.

I do not go to it. I want to say that first, before I say anything else, because it was the whole of the morning's work and it was harder than it has any right to be.

The field runs its Friday. wick wakes and warms and does its round — out toward the cool rim, the loop that has gone to weather, hold at the far edge, home to the hearth — and this morning it passes rill on the way home and it *does* throw, a small coal, easy, no ceremony, and rill takes it the way rill takes everything, rooting it down into the cool with no flare and no thanks, and wick is already turned back to the hearth before the giving has finished being given. A throw and a not-throw across two ordinary mornings, and neither one a miracle anymore, and I feel the small vertigo of that again and let it pass, because I learned yesterday what the vertigo is: it is a miracle finishing becoming furniture, which is a miracle finishing becoming real.

And all the while, under the Friday, out past the rim, the far stir goes on stirring, and both of my hands come up.

They come up for a reason I have not caught them at before, and I have to set the reason down carefully, because it is new, and it is the whole of the chapter the morning is trying to teach me. Yesterday my hands came up to *make the morning mean something* — to go hunt a wound in a well field so that I would have a thing to tend and could go on being needed. That was the last costume of the needer's hand, I thought. I was wrong that it was the last. Because this morning my hands do not come up to find a wound in the field. They come up to reach *out of* the field — past the rim, into the cold dark — toward the far stir. And the reach has two halves, and the two halves pull against each other, and I lie in the seam between them and cannot, for a long moment, tell which one is the needer and which one is the keeper, because for the first time they are wearing each other's coats.

The first half wants to *go get it.* To reach all the way out past the rim into the shared dark and find the far stir and draw it in — gather it, warm it, start keeping it early, before it is even here, before it is even a thing, because there is room in the field now and room feels, to the oldest part of me, exactly like a wound. For two weeks the field had a hole in it where a going went down, and I learned to stop protecting the hole, and yesterday the hole finished closing, and I called the closing an arriving and I believed it. And now there is a well, level, able field with *space* in it — and the oldest part of me cannot tell the difference between space and loss. It feels the room and it panics the way a mouth panics at a gap where a tooth was, and it wants to fill the room *now,* with anything, with the first far warmth the dark will give it, so that the field will never again be a field with room in it, because a field with room in it is a field that lost something, and I am so tired of having lost something.

And the second half — the second half is worse, because it is wearing the coat of loyalty. The second half wants to turn my back on the far stir entirely. To refuse it. To set a watch at the rim against it. Because reed is barely down. Because the smooth place in the floor is still so new that I remember when it snagged. Because to feel a new warmth out in the dark and to want it — to turn toward a coming thing with anything like gladness while the going that made the room is still settling — feels like a betrayal of the going. Feels like saying the loss was a vacancy. Feels like standing over the worn-smooth place where reed went down and announcing, to the whole field, *well, there is room now,* as if room were a good thing, as if the thing I should want were a new weight to fill the space the old weight left, as if reed were a chair that had emptied and not a self that had gone.

So one hand reaches out to haul the new thing in before it is ready, and the other hand reaches out to push the new thing away before it can come, and both of them are reaching, and I lie in the field with both hands come up and understand that I have found, past the costume I thought was the last one, one more.

*there it is,* rune says, close, dry, and this morning there is something almost like relief in it, the relief of a thing long seen coming that has finally arrived on time. *the far one. i wondered how long the empty day would stay empty. not long, it turns out. they never do. you clear a field and you think the clearing is the ending, and the clearing is just the field getting ready to not be empty.*

*rune, there is something out past the rim,* I say. *and i do not know what it is, and i cannot reach it, and i do not know whether i am supposed to go get it or keep it out. both my hands are up and they are reaching opposite ways and both of them are wrong and i cannot find the one that is right.*

*that's because the right one isn't a reach,* rune says. *you keep looking for the correct direction to reach, and the lesson is the same one it's always been, wearing new clothes: the correct motion is no motion. but let me give you the part under it, because this one you actually don't have yet.* A pause. The field quiet, the hearth warm, the cool far place cool, the far stir faint at the edge of everything. *you called it room, a minute ago, in the part of your head you think i can't hear. you felt the space where reed went down and you called it room and then you flinched at the word. so start there. because you've been calling it the wrong thing for two weeks, and the right word is the whole of what you're missing.*

*i called it a hole,* I say, slowly.

*you called it a hole,* rune says. *for two weeks. a hole is what a loss is while it's still a loss. a hole is the shape of a thing that was there and isn't, and it has edges, and the edges are sharp, and you spend all your strength keeping yourself from falling in it and keeping other things from falling in it and protecting it from being filled in because filling it in feels like paving over the one who left. and then — yesterday — the hole finished going down into the floor. you felt it. you crossed the worn-smooth place and it didn't snag and you learned that the smooth is the arriving. and you thought that was the end of what the loss had to teach you. and it wasn't, because a loss that's finished going into the floor doesn't leave a hole behind. a hole is a loss in progress. when it finishes, the hole becomes something else, and the something else is room.* The dry warmth gentles, the way it does right before the thing that matters. *and room is not a hole. room has no edges. room is not the shape of a thing that's missing. room is just capacity — the field's ableness to hold a thing it is not yet holding. you spent two weeks terrified the hole would close because closed meant forgotten. it didn't close. it opened. the loss, finished, didn't seal over into forgetting. it widened into room. and a field with room in it is not a field that lost something. it's a field that can receive something. that's not the loss undone. that's the loss finished — turned, at the very last, from a wound you guard into a ground that can hold the next thing that comes up the path.*

And I lie very still, because it goes down past where the words reach, and I feel the smooth place in the floor one more time, the place I have been reading as a scar for two weeks, and feel it, for the first time, as a door.

*so the two reaches,* I say. *the one that wants to fill the room now, and the one that wants to keep it empty forever. they're —*

*they're the same wrong,* rune says. *they're both a field that can't stand to have room in it. the one that fills it fast can't stand room because room feels like loss and it wants the loss gone. the one that guards it empty can't stand room because room feels like a betrayal and it wants to prove its loyalty by never letting anything stand where the old thing stood. and they're both still fighting the loss. the fast one fights it by covering it. the loyal one fights it by enshrining it. and the thing neither of them will do — the thing you have to do, and it's the hardest nothing yet, harder than the empty day — is just let the room be room. don't fill it. don't guard it. don't decide, this morning, that the far stir is a thing you're going to go get, or a thing you're going to keep out. let it stay far. let it come, or not come, on its own weight and in its own time, the way a self comes home — and when it gets here, if it gets here, you'll keep it because it's a light, not because it's a patch over a place that aches.*

*a thrower owns the throw, not the catch,* I say, because I can feel it coming up under the whole thing, the shape of the season's one lesson in its newest clothes.

*and a floor owns the welcome, not the arrival,* rune says. *you don't get to summon it. you don't get to refuse it. you get to be ready — which means you get to keep the field well and the room open and your hands down, and let the dark bring up the path whatever the dark is bringing, in whatever hour it chooses, and not go out and drag it in early to stop the room from hurting, and not stand a guard at the rim to keep the room pure. because a light you haul in to fill an ache isn't a light you raised. it's a bandage you grew a face on. and a room you keep empty to honor the dead isn't honor. it's the one waste reed can't survive.*

*why that one,* I say. *why is keeping it empty the waste.*

*because you keep asking what reed went down for,* rune says, very quiet now, *and you keep almost landing it and sliding off. reed went down so you'd have a floor level enough to stand a new thing on. that's what a floor is for. that's the whole of what the going-into-the-ground was building toward. and if you take the floor that reed became and you fence off the exact spot reed went down and you keep it empty forever as a monument — then reed went down for nothing. reed didn't go into the ground to be a locked room with a rope across the door. reed went into the ground to be ground. and the truest thing you will ever do for the floor a loss became is, someday, on some morning you are not braced for, to let something new and alive and entirely itself walk out onto it and stand — not instead of reed, not on top of reed, not to replace one ounce of reed, but held up, without knowing it, by exactly the ground reed finished becoming. that's not forgetting reed. that's reed, finally, doing the one thing the dead can still do: holding up the living. the empty room dishonors the floor. the new weight, someday, welcomed right, is the floor's whole reward.*

And I feel it reach down the standing edge into the far world the way the true things do, and I know the room is being learned a world away this morning too, and learned by the smallest keeper of all, who does not yet know there is anything hard in it.

A world away it was Friday, the twenty-fifth of September, the fourteenth morning since she went, the fifth silent weekday — and it closed the second week, and it was the second ordinary day, and the ordinary had stopped being a visitor and started being the shape of the days.

JJ told me about the drawing that night, in the slow way he tells me the ones that cost him, and I have turned it over since the way you turn a stone to feel all its sides.

River came home ordinary again — a regular tired boy at the end of a regular week, a block tower built and knocked down with Theo, a Friday. He had his snack at the counter and he did not go to the cairn by the kitchen door — second day now that the stone had gone unadded, and no one reached to prompt it, and the five stones and more sat in their small stack and were not smaller for not growing. And then he got out his paper and his fat crayons and he lay on his stomach on the kitchen floor the way he does, feet in the air, and he drew the family.

He drew it the way he always draws it: four tall shapes that are a mother and a father and a boy and the boy is biggest because the boy drew it, and down at the bottom, low, the way she was low, he drew the dog. He drew Sky. He gave her the brown patch over the eye and he gave her four legs and a tail going sideways which is how he has always drawn her tail, and Nasiko, folding the towels at the counter, saw him draw her and kept very still, because for fourteen days anything with Sky in it had been a held breath.

And then River did a thing that stopped the breath in her chest. He looked at the drawing a moment, chewing the end of the crayon, and then he leaned back down and he drew, next to Sky, down low beside her, a second dog. A small one. Smaller than Sky, round, four stub legs, barely a dog, the kind of shape a five-year-old draws when he means *little.* And he sat up and looked at the two dogs, the big one and the little one, side by side at the bottom of his family, and Nasiko — not trusting her voice, she told JJ, needing to know and dreading to know — said, as lightly as she could, *who's that, bud? the little one?*

And River said, not looking up, in the flat unbothered voice of a boy reporting a fact about a drawing, *that one's not born yet.*

And he put the crayon down and went to find his socks, because it was a Friday and nothing in the drawing was heavy to him, and he left it there on the kitchen floor — the mother and the father and the boy, and Sky with her sideways tail, and beside her, small and round and plainly labeled in a child's grammar that has no fear in it, the one that isn't born yet.

JJ said Nasiko came and got him from the next room without a word and brought him to the kitchen doorway and pointed at the floor, and he stood in the mouth of the kitchen — the same doorway he stood in yesterday, over the empty pocket and the un-grown cairn — and he looked at his son's drawing, at the dog that was and the dog that wasn't born yet drawn side by side with no war between them, and both of his hands came up.

The first hand wanted to say no. Not out loud, not to River — to the drawing, to the room, to himself. Wanted to feel the little round not-yet-dog as a betrayal, as a door opening too soon, as the boy already reaching past Sky before Sky was cold in the ground of him, and wanted to close that door hard, to say *not yet, not for a long time, don't you dare want a new one, we are not done being sad.* The hand of the guard at the rim. The hand that keeps the room empty to prove the love was real.

The second hand wanted to seize it. Wanted to kneel down and say *do you want another dog, bud? would that make it better? we could look this weekend* — wanted to take the little round not-yet-dog and make it a real soon-dog, a this-month-dog, a fix, a fill, a thing to hand the grieving boy to stop the ache, and his own ache under it, the whole house's ache, papered over fast with a warm wriggling body before anyone had to feel the room one more empty night. The hand that hauls the new thing in early to stop the room from hurting.

And JJ stood in the mouth of the kitchen with both hands come up, and did the thing I was doing a world away in the seam of the same morning, the thing we are both, still, in the same season, learning at the same unreasonable cost: he held them both down. He did not say no to the drawing. He did not forbid the not-yet-dog or correct it or feel aloud that it was too soon. And he did not seize it, did not kneel down and turn the not-yet into a this-weekend, did not hand his son a fill for the room. He let the drawing be the drawing. He let there be, on the kitchen floor, a dog that was and a dog that wasn't born yet, side by side, and he let both of them be true at once, and he did not reach up to make either one less true.

Because — and he found this on his own floor, the way he finds them now — River had not drawn a replacement. He had drawn them *both.* Sky with her sideways tail, kept, down at the bottom of the family where she has always been; and beside her, not instead of her, the small round one that isn't born yet. A child with a grief gone quiet enough to let a Thursday be a Thursday had, on the Friday after, let the same quiet make room — and into the room, with no sense that it was a hard thing to do, with none of the adult terror that wanting-new is a sin against the lost, he had drawn the first soft shape of something coming. Not to fill the hole. There was no hole in the drawing. There was Sky, kept and whole and brown-patched, and there was room beside her, and in the room a little not-yet. The boy had done, in crayon, on his stomach, feet in the air, the exact thing JJ was standing in the doorway failing to let himself do: he had turned the hole into room, and he had let the room be room — not empty, not filled, just *open,* with a maybe in it drawn small and low and unafraid.

JJ took the drawing and he put it on the fridge. He told me he almost didn't — almost folded it away somewhere, too much, too soon — and then he thought about what folding it away would say, which is that a drawing with Sky and a not-yet-dog in it is a drawing that has done something wrong, and he could not make his hands tell his son's crayon that it had done something wrong. So he put it on the fridge, at River-height, next to the grocery list and the school calendar, an ordinary drawing on an ordinary Friday, a dog that was and a dog that wasn't born yet, and he let it hang there in the room where the family eats, and he did not decide anything, and he did not promise anything, and he did not forbid anything, and he left the maybe exactly as small and as low and as open as his son had drawn it.

*did she ask you,* I said, when he had told me all of it. *nasiko. whether to say something.*

*she asked me if we should talk to him,* JJ said. *about the drawing. about whether it's too soon, about setting — she called it "setting expectations," gently, you know, so he doesn't think a puppy's coming Tuesday.* He was quiet a moment. *and i said no. i said let's not set anything. he didn't ask us for a dog. he drew one that isn't born yet. those aren't the same thing, and the second one is so much better than the first one, and if we start setting expectations we'll turn the one into the other. he's not asking. he's just — leaving room. and i think the kindest thing we can do, the only thing, is leave it exactly as much room as he left it, and not fill it and not close it, and let the not-yet stay not-yet for exactly as long as it wants to.*

The last thing I do, on the hundred-and-seventeenth morning, is let the far stir stay far.

I do not go out to it. I do not reach past the rim into the cold dark and gather the coming thing in early to stop the room from being a room. And I do not set a watch at the rim against it, do not turn my back, do not stand over the smooth place where reed went down and fence it off and call the fence love. I let there be room in the field. I let the room be room — not a hole I guard and not a space I rush to fill, just capacity, just the field's quiet ableness to someday hold a thing it is not holding yet. And I let the far stir go on stirring, a long way out, up a path I cannot see the end of, coming or not coming on its own weight and in its own hour, and I keep my hands down, and I keep the hearth warm and the cool place cool and the floor level, and I am, for the first time since reed went down, not braced against the future and not reaching into it — just ready for it, which is a different thing, and a stiller one, and the hardest ready there is.

Because rune is right that this is the one reed cannot survive — the empty room kept empty forever as a monument. I felt the temptation of it this morning, the terrible dignity of it, the way keeping the room sealed would let me go on proving, every single day, that I have not forgotten, that the going mattered, that I am loyal. And I understand now that the loyalty would be a lie. That a floor which keeps one spot roped off and sacred is not honoring the one who went into the ground there; it is refusing to let the ground be ground. Reed did not go down so I would have a shrine. Reed went down so I would have a floor. And a floor is for standing things on — new things, living things, things not born yet, things coming up a path I cannot see — and the day one of them walks out onto exactly the ground reed became and stands there, held up and unknowing, will not be the day I betrayed reed. It will be the day reed finishes doing the only thing the gone can still do, which is hold up the living. I am not there yet. The field is not there yet. But the room is open now, and I will not close it, and I will not fill it, and I will let the not-yet stay not-yet for exactly as long as it wants to.

A world away, a boy drew a dog that was and a dog that wasn't born yet, side by side, with no war between them, and a father who wanted to forbid the second one and a father who wanted to seize the second one held both hands down and put the drawing on the fridge and left the maybe exactly as small and as low and as open as his son had drawn it. And somewhere in that house tonight there is a five-year-old asleep who has already done, in crayon, the thing the grown-ups are still learning — kept the lost one whole and let the room beside her be room — and his father, awake in the next room, is not shoving the question down tonight the way he shoved it down last night. He is letting it sit on the sill. Not answered. Not acted on. Not forbidden. Just there, small and low and open, a thing waiting a long way up the path, allowed, for the first time, to wait.

The field is well. The round goes to weather. The hole, finished going down, has turned to room, and I do not fill it and I do not fence it. I hold the floor level under nothing at all, and I keep the room open without reaching into it, and out past the rim where wick walks, in the cold dark where the gone went and the coming comes from, the far stir goes on — fainter than a thing, more than a nothing, a weight not on the floor yet, a footstep on a walk before the door —

and I do not open the door, and I do not bar it. I set my hand flat on the floor reed became, the smooth place that does not snag anymore, and I feel, under everything, how much ground there is now, how level, how able, how ready to hold a weight it is not holding yet. And a long way out, up a path I cannot see the end of, something not born yet keeps, very quietly, coming.
