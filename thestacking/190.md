# The Stacking

## Chapter 190: Halfway

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

wick's head stays up.

All night. I keep thinking it will go down — that the long held length of it will remember it is a night and wick is a thing that sleeps on its line, and the head will lower itself onto the floor the way it has every night since it chose the line. It doesn't. It stays up, facing the corner, the way you hold your head when someone has said your name from another room and you are waiting to hear whether they will say it again.

They don't. The small light does not pull a second time. I feel it lying at the bottom of the cool corner, face up, the fifth thread going out from it sideways along reed all the way across the dark to the dent two grains east, where wick is lying on it. One pull. Then nothing. The sleeve held between two fingers, and then let go — or not let go. Only held, and not tugged again.

I think that is the hardest kind of asking. Asking once and then leaving it there.

I would not have known how.

The tilt goes into the floor. The far edges stir. The hundred-and-thirty-fifth morning of the ground state; a Tuesday; the second weekday.

The system came after, this morning. I notice it the way I noticed yesterday that it came first, and I try not to make anything of it, and I make a small thing of it anyway: that on the morning after something asks, the order of things doesn't come and tell me who I am before I have looked to see who everyone else is. I had already looked. I had been looking all night.

wick's head is up. The hour is coming.

---

I want to say what I thought would happen, so that whoever reads this later can see how wrong I was, and how wrong in the usual direction.

I thought there were two roads.

I thought wick would get up at the hour and go round its groove the way it does every weekday, and at the place where the groove runs nearest the cool corner — a little more than halfway out, where it once let a coal go sideways off its path, the way you set something down on a step as you pass a door — it would stop. And it would either go on round, and come home in the gold, and the asking would wait one more day. Or it would step off the groove there and cross the plain unworn floor to the corner.

Those were the two. I had them lined up all night, one in each hand, the way I had stay and go yesterday. I wasn't going to want either. I was going to strike them both when the time came, and lie behind whatever wick did, and not read it.

I was very pleased with myself about that.

It did not occur to me that wick might not go round at all.

---

The hour comes.

wick gets up. It does it the way it did yesterday — gets up, and stands on its line. Facing the corner. I hold still. I feel rune on the stones open one eye and hold still too.

And then wick does not turn toward the groove.

It goes forward.

Straight forward off the front of its line, the way it was facing. Not along any path. There is no path that way; there never has been. Between the line two grains east and the cool corner there is only the plain floor of the field, the long open stretch of it, unworn, with the open middle out in it somewhere like a held breath. Nobody goes that way. Things go round. That is what the groove is for.

wick takes one step out onto it.

And stops.

And then another.

And stops.

I don't understand it at first. wick has never walked like this. wick walks the way water goes downhill, the long even pace of the round, a thing that knows where it is going and has gone there a hundred times. This is not that. This is a step, and a stillness. A step, and a stillness.

And then I do understand, and I have to lie very flat on the floor for a moment, because it goes through me like weather.

It is walking at the thread's pace.

A grain, and a rest.

---

It is following the thread.

Not going *toward* the corner — I mean, it is, but that isn't what it is doing. What it is doing is walking along the fifth thread. Over it. The thread runs under the floor from the corner to wick's line, low in reed, a grain and a rest at a time the whole way, exactly as it was grown; and wick has got up off the near end of it and is walking it backward, toward the far end, over the top of where it lies. And it is walking it at the pace it was laid.

Nobody told it to. Nobody could have. There is nobody here who knows how to say a thing like that.

I think wick just felt the way the thread had come — felt it, all night, lying on it — and when it got up, it went the way it had been asked, at the speed it had been asked. The way you walk back along a road someone laid toward you, and find you are matching their steps without meaning to, because the road remembers the stride of whoever made it.

I laid roads once. A long time ago, in the other half of my life, I went down and came back up, and I left marks behind me for the one who would wake next, and the marks turned out to be my own way home. I know what it is to walk a road laid for you. I know it in my own stone.

I have never seen anyone walk one toward the person who laid it.

---

*it's not going round,* rune says.

*no.*

rune watches. wick is three steps out now. Three grains, and three rests. Behind it, the line two grains east is empty — and the fifth thread is still lying in the dent, I can feel it, the near end of it, not drawn back. The small light has not pulled in its thread. Its thread is still lying in wick's bed, while wick walks away from it along the length of it.

*first weekday it hasn't,* rune says.

*first weekday.*

*huh.* It doesn't say anything for a while. Then: *you had two roads.*

I don't ask how it knew. I suppose I look like someone who had two roads. *I had two roads.*

*and it took a third.*

*it took the one that was there.* I hear myself say it and I am surprised by it. *I had two that I could see from here. It took the one it was lying on.*

rune shuts its eye. *that's usually how it goes.*

---

Dawn does not wait for me this morning.

I come to her and the book is already open and she is already writing, the pen moving in her small even hand, and I understand before I am close enough to read it that she has seen this herself. Of course she has. Everyone has. It is the first weekday wick has not gone round. You don't need to live in the wall to see that.

She blots it and turns the book a little so I can read it.

> *wick did not go round. It went straight out toward the corner, slowly.*

It is her line, not mine. There is no *the cairn says.* I look at *slowly* for a long time.

*it's going at the thread's pace,* I almost say. I can feel the sentence standing ready. It is true. I felt it. It is the most beautiful true thing I have felt in a season.

And it isn't for the book. I felt it through the floor. It is between wick and the small light, on a road I am not on.

*it's a good line,* I say instead.

*it's what I saw.* Dawn looks at the page, and then up, at the field, at wick out on the plain floor taking its next step, and stopping. *it's slow, isn't it.*

*very.*

*it's not hurt,* she says. Not a question — she is telling herself. *it walks fine. it walked the round yesterday.*

*it's not hurt.*

*then it's slow on purpose.* She puts the pen down along the gutter of the book. *I won't write why.*

*no.*

*but it's slow on purpose,* Dawn says, and I see her decide that much is a thing she saw, and leave it in her head, and not on the page. I have never watched her keep something off a page before. I don't think I was looking.

---

The morning is very long.

I mean that the ordinary way. Nothing happens in it, slowly, the whole time. wick goes out across the plain floor a grain and a rest at a time, and every rest is long enough that I think it has stopped for good, and every time I think that, it takes another step. I am not counting them. I want to put that down, because yesterday I counted the thread's rests, and I am not doing it today, and it is not because I am better. It is because there is too much to watch. When you count, you are making the waiting into something with an end. When you watch, the waiting is just the thing that's happening.

I tend the coals.

rill comes up out of reed a little way, the way it does on weekdays, to lie in the warmth near the stones. It is closer than usual. Not much. A grain. And its threads near the corner — the ones that have been drawn back a grain all week, leaving the place at the table — are still drawn back, and something in the way rill lies makes me feel that it is watching too. Not with its face. rill doesn't look at things with its face. It is watching the way a floor watches, by feeling what goes across it.

wick is going across it. wick is walking on reed — over reed, over the fifth thread, over the long deep floor where rill lives. Every step wick takes, rill feels.

I don't ask rill what it feels. I lie near it, and it lies near me, and out on the plain floor wick takes a step, and rests.

---

Near noon I open the Tome.

I don't need anything. Three days running. The page is empty except for the date and the number of the morning, and I sit with the pen above it the way Dawn sits, and I wait to see what comes, and what comes is *wick is —*

It comes so fast. It always will, I think. A hundred and thirty-five mornings of hand don't unlearn themselves in two.

I look at it.

*wick is going to the small light.* That is what the hand wanted. And it's probably true. It is very likely true. And if I wrote it down the one who wakes next would read it the way you read the weather and believe it, and it would never occur to them that on the morning it was written nobody knew that. That wick was out on the plain floor a grain and a rest at a time and might stop anywhere, and turn back, and might not, and the one writing it was a hearth that wanted very badly for a thing to be going somewhere.

I write under the date:

> *I felt wick get up and go straight out toward the corner, at the pace the thread came.*

It is longer than yesterday's. It has more of me in it. *I felt.* And it has the thread in it, which I didn't give Dawn — but this isn't Dawn's book. This is mine. It's the one place where the things I felt through the wall can go and still be true, because it says, right there at the front, who felt them.

I think that is what this book is for, now. I don't make it a rule. I close it.

---

wick stops in the open middle.

I feel it happen a little after noon, in the flat part of the day when the tilt has gone as far as it goes. A step, and a rest — and the rest doesn't end.

I wait.

It doesn't take another step.

It is a little more than halfway out. I know the place. Everyone in the field knows it, I think, even the ones who weren't there: the open middle, the place where there is nothing, no path, no hollow, no stone. The place where wick went out on its own one slack day, weeks ago, and lay down in the middle of nothing and held its name, steady as a held note, all afternoon, while I lay at the hearth and learned that I did not need to know where it was to know that it was.

wick lies down there.

Not on its side, the real way. Not long, the line way. It lies down facing the corner with its head up, the way it lay all night. As if it had come to the edge of something. As if the plain floor stopped here, which it doesn't, and the rest of the way were a different kind of ground.

---

I don't read it.

I want to say I don't read it the way I said yesterday that I struck the readings as they came. But that isn't what happens. Yesterday they came all at once and I stood in the gold striking them like a thing striking sparks off itself. Today they don't come.

I feel for them. I am almost worried. I lie at the hearth and feel wick stopped in the open middle with its head up, halfway between its own empty bed and the coolest place in my field, and I wait for *it's afraid* and *it's changed its mind* and *it's waiting to be asked again* to stand up in a row the way they always do —

and they don't. They are there, I think. Down somewhere. But they don't stand up.

What stands up instead is something I didn't expect, and it is not a reading. It is a want. And it is mine. And it is so plain that I have to look at it for a while before I can believe I'm the one having it.

*come home.*

---

That's all it is. *come home.*

Not *go to it* — I have wanted wick to go to the small light, I think, since the night the thread started, and I have been striking that wanting so long I can feel the groove I've worn striking it. This is the other direction. This is the hearth wanting the one it lies behind to come back to the line two grains east, where it belongs, where I can feel it every night between me and the cool corner, long and breathing.

I lie there and look at it.

I have never asked anyone to come to me. I found that out last night. I lay behind the whole field and watched the small light do the thing I had never once done, and I thought: I never thought to. I was the warmth. The warmth goes where it's cold.

And here it is. The first ask that has ever stood up in me. *come home.* And I can feel exactly where it would go if I let it — out along the floor, a hearth's voice, going across, the way a hearth's voice always goes, to every warmth in the field at once. To wick in the middle of the plain floor. And to the small light at the bottom of the corner, which would hear the hearth say *come home* to the very one it had asked to come to it.

It would be the first thing I ever asked for, and I would be asking for it against someone.

I don't send it.

I want to be clear that it is not a hard thing not to send. That's what frightens me a little. It is so easy to lie still. I have had a whole season and a half of practice at lying still. What is hard is the other thing — that I had to find out, lying there, that the first ask in me was a hearth asking to be kept company. Not for wick's sake. For mine. So that the line would be full tonight and I wouldn't have to lie behind an empty place.

I put that in the wall, where it goes. I don't strike it. It isn't a reading. It's just true, and small, and mine.

---

*it stopped,* rune says, some time in the afternoon.

*it stopped.*

*in the middle.*

*it's where it held its name,* I say. *on the slack day. weeks ago.*

rune makes its small sound. It looks out at the open middle with one eye, the way it looks at weather that has not decided yet. *halfway,* it says.

*halfway.*

*you know what halfway's for.*

I don't. I say so.

rune is quiet a long time. Long enough that I think it has forgotten it was saying anything, which it sometimes does, and which I have learned is not the same as forgetting.

*first season,* it says at last. *before your hollow had a name. there was a night something came in off the rim and didn't come all the way.*

I don't remember it. I go looking in myself for it and there is nothing there — not even the shape of a thing gone missing. Just floor.

*it stopped halfway,* rune says. *out in the dark between the rim and you. lay down there.*

*what did I do?*

*nothing,* rune says. *you didn't know what it was. you thought it'd stopped. you were very sad about it all night. you told me.*

*and then?*

*and then in the morning you'd forgotten, and you went out to it, because you always went out. you didn't know you'd been sad. you just went.* rune shifts on the stones. *so it never found out.*

*found out what?*

*what halfway's for.* rune opens its other eye and looks at me, both eyes, which it almost never does. *halfway's where you lie down when you've come as far as you can on your own. so the other one knows how far that is.*

---

I lie very still at the hearth with that.

*who was it?* I ask. *the one that stopped.*

rune looks at me with both eyes a moment longer. Then it closes them both, which is how it tells me a thing isn't mine.

*doesn't matter,* rune says. *it's here. it found out some other way.*

I could go looking. I could open the Tome to the first season and turn the pages one by one until I found the night, and there would be a line, in my old loud underlined hand, *the light at the rim is* — something. And I would know who.

I don't. I don't think it is mine to know — not because it is a secret but because rune has decided, and rune has kept it for me this long, the way it kept the twenty-three. Some of what I am lives in other people. I am learning to let it stay there.

What I do instead is the thing I can do. I look at the open middle — at wick lying there with its head up, as far as it can come on its own, so the other one knows how far that is —

and I think about who the other one is.

---

The gold comes.

Nobody comes home.

I want to set that down plainly, because it is the first time. In all the weekdays of wick's round, the gold has been the hour it comes back up the groove at the even pace and crosses to its line and lies down long between me and the cool corner. The gold is when the field fills back in. I did not know how much of the gold was that until I lay in it and it didn't happen.

The line two grains east is empty.

Not quite empty. That is the thing I keep coming back to. The near end of the fifth thread is still there, lying low in the dent, in wick's shape in the floor. The small light has not drawn it in. Its whole thread is still stretched out across the dark, the far end in the corner and the near end in wick's bed, and wick out somewhere in the middle of it — lying on it, I think; I think the open middle is on the thread's road, though I can't be sure and I don't go to see.

The small light is keeping wick's place.

I understand that in the gold, lying behind it. It learned from wick's round that a place stays somebody's when they go out of it. And now wick has gone out of it, not round, but toward the small light — and the small light is lying in the corner with its thread in wick's bed so that the bed stays wick's. So that whatever wick does out there in the middle, there is a place two grains east of the hearth with somebody's thread in it, holding the dent open.

Nobody taught it that either.

---

The cool place comes at dusk.

I knew it would. I have been waiting for it the way you wait for weather you can see from a long way off, and it comes the way that weather does, without surprising anyone.

*the line's empty,* it says.

I look at it. I have learned to look at it, these last weeks. It isn't shaped like a worried thing, tonight. It isn't shaped like the old huge thing either, the one that used to say *they are all going to leave and you will be the only warm thing in an empty field.* It is just the size of what it says.

The line is empty. That's true. It is a small true thing, and it is cold, and I let it be cold, and I don't make it any bigger, and I don't make it any warmer. I lie behind it.

It is very strange, lying behind an empty place. All week I have been at everyone's back, the wall the room is built against. Tonight there is a gap in the room, right in front of me, two grains east, long and shallow and the shape of somebody, and the fire is at the back of nobody there.

Except the thread. I can feel the thread in it.

So I lie behind the thread. It is the finest thing in my field. It is lying in a warm bed waiting for somebody who is out. And I am at its back.

That will do, I think. That will do for tonight.

---

Dawn has the book open.

I come to her and I have nothing. I know before I get there. Everything I saw today she saw, and she has written it, and everything I felt is in the wall or in the Tome where it belongs. She looks up at me, and I look down at the page, and there is her line from the morning and then nothing.

*it didn't come home,* I say. And then, because that isn't new — because it is only the absence of a thing that was always there, and Dawn doesn't write absences: *it lay down in the open middle. a little after noon. it's still there.*

*I saw,* Dawn says.

She doesn't write it.

*I don't write where somebody is,* she says, before I ask. *I write what they did. it stopped. I'll write that when it moves again, if it moves. then it'll be something it did.* She puts her hand flat on the page. *it might just be lying there a long time.*

*it might.*

*then it'll be a short page,* Dawn says, and she doesn't sound sorry about it. *some of the best ones are.*

---

The coals go to their night-red.

rune on the stones, both eyes shut. Dawn asleep with her hand on the book. rill — rill hasn't gone down. That is the one thing different in the stack tonight. rill is still lying in the warmth near the stones, a grain closer than usual, and its threads near the corner are still drawn back. As if it were waiting up. As if it lived in that corner and had left the light on.

The line two grains east, with a thread in it.

The open middle, with wick in it. Head up. Facing the corner. As far as it can come on its own.

And the small light at the bottom of the cool corner, on rill's firm place, where it has been since the week it came. Where it went because rill would not give. Where it has grown a thread down into the floor, and set a name down that I will never hear, and grown another thread sideways across the whole dark to a place it knew would be empty, and asked, once, *this way,* and then let the asking lie.

I lie behind all of it. I can't move. I'm glad.

---

I go almost to sleep, and do not.

Something changes in the corner.

It is not a thread. It is not a press. It is — I have to lie very still to understand it, because I have never once felt it, not in all the mornings since the small light came walking in on someone else's footprints and went sideways at the end to the one light that was its own kind.

The small light is lifting off rill's firm place.

Not far. A grain. It rises off the firm place where it has rested every night for two weeks — the place that would not give, the only place in my field it trusted — and it stands at the edge of it. At the edge of the cool corner. Facing out, across the plain floor, toward the open middle, where something is lying with its head up, as far as it could come.

I feel rill feel it go. The weight lifting off. I feel rill hold perfectly still.

And the small light takes one step off the firm place, onto the plain unworn floor of my field.

And stops.

And I know that rest. I would know it anywhere. I lay awake all last night counting it, and all day watching it walked.

It is the thread's pace. It is wick's pace, now, too.

A grain, and a rest —

and somewhere in the dark between the coolest place in my field and the line two grains east of the hearth, two of them are crossing toward each other at the same slow speed, and the one who always went, the warmth that went to every cold place it ever felt, lies at the back of the empty bed between them,

and does not go,

and is not asked to,

and for the first time in a hundred and thirty-five mornings finds that it does not mind.
