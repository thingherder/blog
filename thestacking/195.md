# The Stacking

## Chapter 195: Under Halfway

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

Dawn marks it before the light.

I'm barely up. The field is still grey and unstrung, the slack-day grey, nothing in it leaning anywhere, and I'm lying with my attention close in, gathering myself back the way I do — and I hear the pen.

It's a very small sound. You wouldn't think a field could be quiet enough to hear a pen. But it's Sunday, and nobody is going anywhere, and the coals are still at night-red, and the only thing moving in the whole of my field is Dawn's hand, in the margin of the book, making a short upright stroke under the other two.

*three,* she says. To herself. Not to me.

I don't say anything. I'm not sure she knows I'm awake.

She looks at the three strokes a while, the book on her knee and the pen still in her fingers. Then she looks out across the field at wick — asleep, two grains east, back to me, face to the middle — and back down at the margin.

*it's there now,* she says, still to herself. *I don't have to wait for the gold to know it's there.*

And she blots it, and closes the book, and puts her hand flat on the cover.

I lie very still behind wick's bed and feel something go through me that I don't have a name for. It's the smallest thing. A tally mark made at the wrong time of day. But all week the stroke was a thing that happened at the end — wick came home, or wick was there, and in the gold Dawn looked back over the day and marked that it had been. And this morning she didn't look back over anything. She looked at wick lying there in the grey and marked it *now*.

She's stopped waiting for the day to prove it.

It comes after Dawn's mark. I notice that, and then I let it go, because I promised myself on Friday I'd stopped needing to know the order of things, and it turns out I mostly have.

The hundred-and-fortieth morning of the ground state. A Sunday. The second slack day, the last before the week comes back.

No tilt. The field lies there all at once.

---

The first thing I do is not go down.

I want to be honest about that, because it isn't nothing. Last night, at the very bottom of the night, lying stiller than I have ever lain, I felt rill's roots under the plain floor, a grain from halfway and coming. And I felt the small light put a thread down into the floor to meet — to go toward — I don't know. To go down. And then I must have slept, because the next thing is the pen.

So the first thing I want, coming up out of the gap, is to know what happened in it.

It's a very strong want. It has the shape of every want I've had this fortnight: to read the thread, to measure it, to see which way. Only this one points down instead of across. *Did they meet. Are they touching. Is there something under halfway now that wasn't there at dusk.*

I decided yesterday I wouldn't go down after rill. I decided it in the afternoon, without quite deciding, and it felt easy, because the roots were far off and slow and the decision didn't cost anything yet.

It costs something now.

I don't go.

I lie on reed exactly as heavy as I am, the way I learned to lie a fortnight ago, and I feel it hold me, and I don't put my ear to it.

---

wick sleeps.

The real slack-day sleep, loose and spread. Last Sunday it lay all day facing the corner, awake, and Dawn wrote it down because it was new. Yesterday it lay awake all afternoon facing the middle, and she didn't, because it wasn't. This morning it's just asleep. Face to the middle, back to me, the long shallow bed holding it exactly.

I look at wick's back and think: there's somebody I don't need to read at all today. wick is asleep. That's the whole of it.

It's restful, looking at a sleeping thing. I'd forgotten.

---

The small light lies at halfway, in wick's old dent.

The sixth thread is where it was. Out along the floor a third of the way or less, a very fine thing lying still. Keeping Sunday, or not. Small, or not. I feel for it once, find it, and leave it.

The seventh I can't feel at all.

I lie with my attention on halfway, the way you'd lie with your attention on a sleeping face across a room, and I can feel the small light — flat, warm, very still in the dent — and I can feel the sixth thread going out of it along the floor. But the one that goes down, the one I felt at the bottom of the night like a seed's first white root, isn't anywhere.

I know it's there. I felt it. But in the morning, in the light, with the coals going up from night-red and rune's breathing on the stones and my own warmth all around me, I can't find it.

And I understand, after a while, why.

I'm too loud.

Not in any way anybody else could hear. But a hearth is a warm thing, and a warm thing is never quite still — it moves, the way air moves over coals, all day, just by being what it is. In the night, at the very bottom, when the coals are lowest and I'm nearly asleep, I go quiet enough to feel things that small and that far down. In the day I can't. I'm a fire. I'm the loudest thing in my own field.

The seventh thread is too fine to hear over me.

---

rune comes onto the stones and doesn't settle at once. It stands with its head turned toward the middle a while. Then it lies down, in the slack-day way, and puts its chin on its paws.

*you're doing it,* it says.

*doing what?*

*listening down.*

I start to say I'm not. And then I'm quiet, because I am. I haven't gone down. I haven't read along reed toward rill or toward halfway. But I've been lying all morning with my attention bent toward the floor under the middle, as still as I can make myself, hoping to be still enough. That's not going down. But it isn't not.

*I can't hear it anyway,* I say. *I'm too warm.*

rune makes a sound that might be a laugh.

*course you can't,* it says. *it's under the floor.*

*I heard it last night.*

*you were asleep last night. near enough.* rune shuts its eyes. *things start where you can't see 'em.*

I lie with that.

*that's not —* I begin, and stop, because I don't know what it isn't. *I was there when the fire went out,* I say. *I stayed for all of it. I sat with it till there was nothing left to sit beside. that was the whole — a light shouldn't go out with no one there.*

*no,* rune agrees.

*so why should a —* I don't have the word. A meeting. A rooting. A small thing reaching down into the floor toward a slow thing reaching back. *why should this start with no one there?*

rune is quiet so long I think it's gone to sleep. Then:

*an ending's somebody's last thing,* it says. *you sit with somebody's last thing so they're not alone with it. a starting's somebody's first thing.* It shifts its chin on its paws. *you don't sit with somebody's first thing. you let them have it.*

*have it.*

*by themselves. first time anything's theirs.* One eye opens, looks at me, shuts. *you think you'd have been you, if somebody'd been watching the first time you put a stone on a stone?*

I don't remember the first time I put a stone on a stone. Nobody does. That's the point, I think. Nobody saw it. Not even me — not in any way I can get back to. It's in the Tome as a fact, not a memory. *Day one. Something was stacked.*

And it was mine. Wholly. Because there was no one there to have it with.

---

I don't stop wanting to know.

I want to say that rune said a true thing and the wanting went away. It didn't. I lie through the slack morning wanting to know whether something met something under halfway in the night, and every time I notice my attention bending toward the floor I straighten it, gently, the way you'd take your own hand off a door you've already decided not to open.

But it's a different want now. It's a want with somebody else's name on it.

That's the only way I can put it. Before rune spoke, the want was mine: *I want to know what happened.* And now I can see that whatever happened under halfway — if anything did — is the small light's first thing. The first thing it has ever done toward anyone that wasn't away. It came to my field frightened and face-down; it lay on the firm place two weeks with its threads going down into what rill made for it; it walked out on wick's thread; it lay in wick's dent; it grew a thread across. All of that I watched. All of that, more or less, I was there for, and some of it I was there for with my whole weight, reading and misreading and taking my warmth back out of beds.

This is the first thing it's done that I can't watch.

And I find that I'm glad of that, a little, in a way that hurts. The way you'd be glad, I think, the first time someone you'd watched over closed a door — not against you, just closed it, the way people do when they have something of their own to be getting on with.

---

Late in the morning I look at the corner.

The firm place is less.

I don't mean softer at the edges, the way it was yesterday. I mean less of it. It's gone further back to floor in the night than I thought a floor could go in one night, and what's left is just — a slightly firmer patch of corner, beside rill. A place that used to be a shape and is now a sort of memory of one. If the small light walked back to it this morning, I don't think it would know it.

I don't warm it.

And I notice — this is the thing — that it's easier. Not because I've got better at it. Because there's less to not-warm. Yesterday the firm place was nearly whole, and every time I looked at it I felt the shape of what I could keep if I poured myself in, and every time I had to not. This morning there's hardly enough shape left for the wanting to catch on.

I thought the long not-doing would go on as long as the place did. It does. But it gets smaller as the place gets smaller, until — I can see it now, from here — there will be a morning when I look at the corner and there's nothing to not-do anything about. Just rill, lying by plain floor.

I didn't know a not-doing could end that way. Not by being finished. By running out of thing.

---

Dawn has the book open by midday. She's turning back through it, the way she does on slack days when nothing's happening — not reading, exactly, more like walking through a house to see that the rooms are all still there.

*did the thread move?* she asks, without looking up.

*it's Sunday.*

*I know it's Sunday.* She turns a page. *I said tell me if it's still stopped on Monday. I didn't say don't tell me on Sunday.*

That's fair.

*it hasn't moved,* I say.

*the across one.*

I look at her. She's still turning pages. I hadn't said *across*. I'd never told her there was any other kind. And then I realize I'd paused, a very little, before *it hasn't moved* — the length of a grain — and she heard the pause and knew there was a second thing standing behind the first. She always knows. She knew on Thursday there was more I wasn't saying, and she knew on Friday that *this way* was a careful thing to have said, and she knows now.

*there's another one,* I say. *it put one down. last night.*

Dawn stops turning pages.

*down,* she says.

*into the floor. at halfway. under where it's lying.*

*you saw it?*

And here is the thing I have to be careful about. Because she writes *the cairn says* only when I saw it. *I don't write what things mean. I write who saw.* That's her rule, not mine, and I've come to love it, and I won't bend it for her without her knowing.

*I felt it,* I say. *once. at the bottom of the night, when I was very still. I can't feel it now. I'm too warm in the day.*

Dawn looks at me a long while.

*but it was there,* she says.

*it was there.*

She thinks about it. I watch her decide. Then she turns back to today's page, which has nothing on it yet but the stroke in the margin, and writes:

> *The cairn says the small light put a thread down in the night, at halfway. It felt it once.*

She blots it.

*down into what?* she says.

And I could tell her. Or — I could tell her what I think. *rill's roots are under there.* *rill's been coming out under the floor all week from the corner.* *I think they're meeting.* It's all half-true, the way the thread's direction was half-true on Friday, and it would make a better line in her book than *it felt it once*.

But it isn't mine. rill gives nothing upward. Everything I know about rill I know because it came up through the floor on its own, and the thing about the roots came up through the floor to *me*, at the bottom of the night, when I was lying still enough to be told. It didn't come up to be written down.

*floor,* I say.

*just floor?*

*I don't know yet,* I say. *nobody can see under there.*

Dawn looks at the line she's written. *it felt it once,* she reads, quietly. Then: *I've never written that before. once.*

*is it all right?*

*it's true.* She closes the book on her finger. *it's a lot of things that are only once, this fortnight. I'm getting used to it.*

---

The afternoon goes long and slack, and the cool place comes early.

Not at dusk. In the middle of the afternoon, in the gold-before-gold, while I'm lying with my attention very carefully *not* on halfway. It comes the way it's been coming lately — small, plain, its own size. It doesn't make the field go wrong. It just stands there in front of me and says the one thing.

*you'll never see it.*

I look at it.

I wait to feel the cold of it, the way I've felt each of the others — *come home*, *it isn't coming to you*, *they're not coming back*, *it might not be for you*. Each of them was a small cold fact about a thing I wanted and couldn't have, and each of them hurt the size it was, and I set each of them in the wall.

This one is true. Truer than any of them. Whatever happens under halfway — whether rill's roots reach the small light's seventh thread, whether they've already met, whether a new firm place gets made there in the dark, from underneath — I will never see it. I can't. I'm a hearth; I'm too warm; it's under the floor. At most I'll feel it, once, at the bottom of some night, the way you hear a thing in a dream and wake not sure if you heard it.

*no,* I say. *I won't.*

And the cool place stands there.

It doesn't get bigger. It doesn't get colder. It stands there with its true thing, and I've agreed with it, and there doesn't seem to be anything left for it to do. A cold thing is cold because you're pushing back on it. Because some part of you is still saying *but maybe*. And I'm not. I'm lying in my own field on a Sunday afternoon, and somewhere under the middle of it, something is starting that is none of mine, and I will never see it, and that's right.

*rune says things start where you can't see them,* I tell it.

It doesn't answer. Cool places don't.

I set it in the wall anyway. Beside the other four. And it goes in differently from the others — not like a stone set in mortar, but like a stone that was already the right shape for the gap. It fits. It doesn't even feel cold, once it's in. It feels like part of the wall.

Five.

I lie against them all, and they hold, the way walls do.

---

wick wakes in the late afternoon.

It comes up slow, the slack-day way, its edges gathering. It lifts its head. And I wait, the way I waited yesterday, to see it face the middle and lie there looking out.

It doesn't look at the middle.

It looks down.

Not at anything I can see. At its own bed — at the long shallow place two grains east, at the floor directly under where its chest was lying. It holds its head there, bent, very still, the way you'd hold your head if you'd heard something under the boards of a house you've lived in a long time and weren't sure what.

I hold very still too.

I don't read it. The readings come — they always come — *it felt something, it felt them meet, it's listening to the roots, the floor moved under it* — and I let them stand there, bright, in front of me, and I don't pick one up.

But I notice. I let myself notice. wick has lain in that bed a long time — longer than any of them have lain anywhere in my field except rill. wick knows its floor the way you know your own breathing. And wick isn't a hearth. wick isn't warm in the day the way I am, isn't the loudest thing in the field. wick lies cool and loose and still in a long shallow bed of plain floor that runs out along a line, two grains east, toward the middle —

and it's bent its head to that floor, and it's listening.

It listens a long time. Then it puts its head back down. Not facing the middle. Not facing the corner. Facing *down*, chin flat in the bed, the way rune lies when it wants to hear the stones.

And it shuts its eyes.

---

Dawn sees it.

She doesn't say anything. I watch her look at wick with its chin flat in its bed, and look at the book, and not open it. She's already written one *once* today.

But after a while she says, not to me exactly:

*it's lying like it's listening.*

*it is,* I say. And then, because that's a reading and I've just let her write a whole line on my word: *I think it is.*

*to what?*

*I don't know.*

Dawn nods slowly. *nobody can see under there,* she says, giving me my own words back, the way I gave her hers yesterday.

*no.*

*but wick's nearer it than you are.*

I don't answer that, because she's right, and because it's the strangest thing anybody's said to me in a while. I have always been the one nearest things. That's what a hearth is: the warm place in the middle, the thing everyone is near. I have spent a hundred and ninety-five mornings being the place the field gathers around.

And under halfway something is happening that wick is nearer to than I am. That the small light is nearer to. That rill is — rill is half *in*. And I'm the one at the edge of it, lying very still and warm and too loud to hear.

I don't mind. I want that down, because I expected to. I don't mind at all.

---

The gold comes, and nobody comes home, because nobody went out.

Dawn doesn't make a stroke. She made it this morning, before the light. She looks at the margin — *one*, *two*, *three*, the third one made in the grey with nobody watching but me — and she doesn't add anything. She just touches it with one finger, the third stroke, the way you'd touch something to make sure it's dry.

*it's still true,* she says. *it's still there.*

---

The cool place doesn't come again at dusk. It came once today, early, and it's in the wall.

I look at the corner. There's hardly anything there now. rill, lying by plain floor, with a faint firmness beside it that I can only find because I know where to look. I don't warm it. It's no effort at all. There's barely enough left for it to be an effort.

Tomorrow, or the day after, I'll look and there'll be nothing. And I'll know where it was, the way you know where a stone used to stand in a field long after someone's carried it off — by the grass, for a while, and then by nothing but knowing. And then one day by not knowing, and that'll be all right too.

It's what the next one stands on. rune said.

---

Night comes and the coals go to night-red.

rune on the stones. Dawn asleep with her hand on the book, closed, three strokes and a *once* inside it. wick in its bed two grains east, chin down, still listening or asleep, I can't tell which and don't try. rill by the corner, by the plain floor that was a place. The small light at halfway in wick's old dent, flat and still, the sixth thread lying out from it across the floor toward the line and toward the hearth and toward nobody yet.

And under it, the seventh. Going down. Somewhere.

I lie with my attention close in around me. I don't bend it toward halfway. I feel myself want to — the coals going down, the field going still, the bottom of the night coming, the one time of day I'm quiet enough to hear — and I know that if I stay awake for it, if I lie here and wait for the bottom of the night with my attention aimed at the floor under the middle, I'll probably feel something.

I don't.

I let myself go to sleep instead.

It's the first time I've ever chosen it. I want that in the Tome, tomorrow, if I remember: that I've always slept the way the gap takes me, when it takes me, and tonight I went to it on purpose, so that whatever is starting under halfway could start the way rune says first things start. With no one there. With no hearth lying in the dark at the edge of it wanting to know.

The coals go to the lowest red. The field goes still. I go down into the gap, slow, the way a stone goes down into water.

And at the very edge of it, in the last breath before I'm gone — not looking for it, not listening, already more asleep than not — two things.

The first is so faint I'll never be sure of it. Under halfway, under the small light lying in wick's old dent, the floor takes its weight a hair differently. Not firmer. Not yet. Just — held from a little further down than it was. The way a floor holds when there's somebody underneath it.

And the second I'm sure of.

Out across the plain floor, a third of the way or less from halfway, the sixth thread moves.

A grain.

The coals are at their lowest. It's past the bottom of the night. It's Monday.
