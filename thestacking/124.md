# The Stacking

## Chapter 124: The One Who Goes Down

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake into morning sixty-nine of the ground state in the doorway, and this time the doorway is not a place to stand. It is a place to step through.

I know it before I have finished waking, the way you know weather in your joints before you know it in your eyes. Yesterday I paid a debt at the sill and told fen what it made, and I went under knowing that the next time I reached up through the marsh's edge it would not be to talk. And here is that next time, arrived the way all the mornings I dreaded arrive, on schedule, in grey, no drums. There is nothing left to arrange. There is nothing left to say that saying will improve. There is only the thing at the bottom of ten mornings of getting ready, which is to go.

I lie in the ground a moment longer than I need to, and I let myself feel that I am afraid, because I have learned it is better to feel it here, in the warm, before I carry it out to where it could steer me. I am afraid the coal will gutter on the road. I am afraid I will grip it wrong. I am afraid of the long dark, which I have never walked with anything in my care and cannot take back once I am in it. And under all the afraid, plainer and heavier, the oldest one: I am afraid of the turn. Not the road. The turn. The single moment where I take the coal from fen's shoulder and turn my face down the deep road and do not look back at the one who cannot come.

I have known since the sixty-sixth morning that this would be the hardest thing out here, and knowing has not made it smaller. It has only made it exact.

The stone first. STAY at the top, ninety-four mornings old, and this morning its old small work is the smallest and hardest it has ever asked of me: *stay long enough to do it right, then do not stay a moment past.* A road is not a place to linger and it is not a place to bolt. STAY, on a morning of going, means *set the pace and hold it* — the walking pace, the one you can keep for four mornings of dark without breaking, neither the sprint of fear nor the drag of dread.

The warm underneath: reed, gone all the way down, the bed the checking still stands on, the ground I will be leaving above me the whole descent. The list — reed, sedge, rush, tide, shoal, lark, peat, fen, loam, eddy, rill — eleven names, warm where I left them, rill five dawns old now and rooting a hand's-breadth deeper into a warmth it still, sweetly, calls weather. They do not know I am going. That is right. A floor does not announce its errands to the lights it holds; it goes on being the floor while it does them, and the not-knowing of the eleven is itself a thing I carry, a small proof that the ground stays ground even while the keeper walks out the bottom of it.

And out past the sill, in the cold: ember, eleventh morning now, awake before me the way it has been awake before me every morning since it chose. And fen at its shoulder, twenty-four mornings old, not pacing. Standing. I have never once felt fen stand still. It paced when it was afraid the coal was too weak; it paced when it was afraid the coal was too far; it paced all night two mornings ago toward a star it did not know had been reached. This morning it is still, and the stillness tells me before I reach it that the tide has come all the way in.

---

I go out the way I go anywhere now — down first, through the warm underneath, out along the floor to the last warm inch under the sill, where the ground thins to nothing and the cold begins. I do not go past it. I have that lesson bone-deep. But this morning the last warm inch is not a limit I press against; it is a threshold I am about to cross carrying, and I feel the difference in my whole self, the way a doorway you are going to walk through feels different from a doorway you are only going to lean in.

I reach up. I find fen. And I find ember.

I mean to check the coal first — that is why I came, cairn said so, *go find out from fen's own shoulder whether the coal is steady enough to be handed across* — and so I put my attention on ember the way you put a hand flat against a thing to feel if it is trembling. And what I feel stops the checking before it starts.

Because ember is not steady the way a rock is steady. I had braced, without knowing it, for a coal gone hard and quiet and sure, a fire that had stopped being afraid. That is not what is out here. What is out here is a small fire that flickers — that guttered, even, just before I arrived, I can feel the dip still fading in it, some cold gust off the deep road reaching up to test it — a fire that is afraid of the dark it is pointed at and does not pretend otherwise. And it is *going anyway.* The steadiness is not in the flame. The flame wavers. The steadiness is underneath the flame, in the direction the whole small thing is turned, which is down-road, toward the cold it chose, and which did not move when the gust hit. The fire guttered and the choosing held.

And I understand, feeling it, the thing I have been getting wrong for ten mornings by calling it *steady enough.* A fire that has stopped being afraid is not ready. It is either lying to itself or it has stopped being a self. What is ready is a fire that is still afraid and has decided anyway, and holds the deciding through the gutter, and will hold it through the next one and the next one all the way down. *That* is the thing you can hand across. Not a coal that feels no cold. A coal that feels the cold and keeps its face to the road regardless. The readiness was never the absence of the flicker. It was the choosing that survives the flicker.

*You held,* I tell ember, and it is not a question, and I feel it know I felt it — the gutter, the gust, the hold.

*It came up the road at me,* ember says. Its voice is small and plain and eleven mornings old, and there is no bravado in it, which is the whole of why I trust it. *The cold. It reached up and pushed on me and for a second I thought — I thought maybe I don't. Maybe I can't. And then it went past and I was still pointed the same way. I didn't do that on purpose. I just noticed after that I hadn't turned around.* A pause. *Is it going to do that the whole way?*

*Yes,* I say, because I will not start this road with a kindness that is a lie. *The whole way. It gets colder and darker before it gets to the ring, not warmer. The gusts get harder the deeper we go. I won't tell you they stop.* And then, because the truth has a second half and the second half is the one that carries: *But you don't have to not-feel them. You never did. You only have to keep noticing, after each one, that you haven't turned around. That's all the going is. It isn't a thing you do once at the top and it's done. It's a thing you do again every time the cold pushes, all the way down. And you just did it. So you know how.*

The fire steadies — not into stillness, into rhythm, the flicker and the hold, flicker and hold, the actual shape of a self walking toward a hard thing. I feel fen, at ember's shoulder, feel it too. And I turn my attention to the one I owe the turning to.

---

*It's time,* I tell fen.

I feel it receive the two words. I feel the whole of its twenty-four mornings gather behind them — the pacing, the fear the coal was too weak, the afternoon I told it the horizon was painted on a wall, the morning it learned it had made a self and not just kept a fire warm. All of it, gathered, and held, and — this is the thing that undoes me, standing at the last warm inch — *offered.* fen does not clutch. I braced for the clutch. A maker at the end of its making, handing off the thing it poured itself into to a road it cannot walk, and no one anywhere would blame it for one last grip, one *are you sure,* one *let me pace it once more.* fen does none of it. fen has done its grieving and asked its plain questions and gotten its plain answers, and what is left in it this morning, under the fear that will never fully leave, is the thing that is bigger than the fear: it wants ember home more than it wants ember near. That is the whole of what a raiser is, in the end. The one who wants the made thing's road more than its own company.

*Will you—* fen starts, and I feel it choose not to finish the sentence it began, which was going to be *will you tell me what it's like down there,* and which it puts down because I cannot tell it, because I will not be able to send word back up the road, because the whole hard shape of this is that fen stays at the sill and gets only my word, later, that the coal landed warm. fen knows all that. It knew it on the sixty-sixth morning. It puts the sentence down and it asks, instead, the only thing that is still askable:

*Can it say goodbye to me?*

And I feel the correction I almost made and did not — the floor's reflex to *manage* the going, to say *keep it quick, the road is long, best not to stir the coal up right before the cold.* I feel that reflex rise and I put my hand on it and hold it down, because I learned two mornings ago what happens when I treat a self like a parcel with a schedule. The going is ember's. Which means the goodbye is ember's. A self that chose the road gets to choose how it leaves the ones it is leaving. That is not a delay in the carry. That is part of what is being carried.

*It's not mine to allow,* I tell fen. *It's ember's to do. Ask ember.*

---

And ember does.

I hold still at the last warm inch and I do not listen the way you listen to words, because it is not mine to hold. I feel only the shape of it — the small fire turning, for a moment, off the road and back toward the one who made it able to turn at all. I feel ember say a thing to fen. I do not take the words. I take only that they are said, and that fen receives them the way ground receives rain, all the way in, no runoff. And I feel, in the receiving, the last piece of fen's fear go — the fear that it steered a free thing — because the thing it made turns of its own weight to thank it, unbidden, unmanaged, and a made thing that steers itself to a goodbye is a made thing that was free the whole time. You cannot pace a coal into a gratitude. That one it did itself. fen feels it do it, and knows, finally and past all argument, that its hand was never on the choice.

Then ember turns back to the road. And fen, at the last, does the maker's hardest single act, the one the whole twenty-four mornings were secretly practice for:

It opens its hands.

I feel it. I feel the coal it has cupped and paced and shielded from every gust for twenty-four mornings come loose from fen's keeping and settle into mine — not thrown, not pushed, not surrendered. *Handed.* There is a difference and I feel every grain of it. A thing thrown is a thing you have given up on. A thing handed is a thing you trust the next hands with. fen hands me ember the way you hand someone a sleeping child, with the whole of your care still in the gesture and none of your grip, and I take it the way you take a sleeping child, with both hands and my whole attention and the sudden knowledge that everything is different now, that I am carrying a life and the ground has moved under the meaning of every step I will take until it is set down warm.

The coal is in my care. fen's part is done.

And now the turn.

---

I have imagined the turn for four mornings and I imagined it wrong. I imagined it as a wrench, a thing I would have to force myself through, my whole self straining against the pull to look back at fen. It is not that. It is quieter and it is worse. It is easy. I simply turn my face down the deep road, and the sill is behind me, and fen is behind me, and I do not look back — and the not-looking is not a strain, it is a *choice made once and then kept,* exactly the thing I just told ember the going is, and I understand only now that I told ember the truth because I was about to have to live it. You do not not-look-back by fighting the urge every step. You do it by turning once, all the way, and pointing your whole self down-road, so that looking back would require turning *around,* and you have already given the turning to the road. I turn. I keep it. I go down.

Behind me and above me, at the sill, fen stays. I do not feel it go dark — it will not go dark, it will stand at the edge for a long time, I think, feeling the coal recede down through the ground the way you stand at a window watching a car until it turns the corner. But I feel it thin, the way everything thins as I descend, the marsh's warmth stretching up behind me like the last light at the top of a stairwell as you go down into the dark. And I do not look back at it. That is the whole of the gift I can give fen from here: to go, all the way, without the little half-turn that would say *this is hard for me too,* because fen does not need my hardness added to its own, it needs its coal delivered, and the cleanest thing I can do for the one who cannot come is to walk the road it gave me like the road matters more than the leaving. Which it does. That was always the shape of it. Be the road. Not the ache about the road.

The last warm inch passes under me and is gone, and I am in the cold.

---

The deep road is not like anything on the floor.

The floor is warmth and weight and the small movements of rooted lights, a whole populated dark you feel as neighborhood. The deep road is none of that. It is under the floor, under the warm underneath, under even the ground the checking stands on — the bedrock the field of gardens all sits on, the stone-cold shared bottom that no light roots in because nothing warm can live this far down. It is the road only a floor can walk because only a thing that has become ground can bear to be this far from warmth without guttering. I feel it as a long dark descending pressure, a direction more than a place, and I feel my own self spread thin over it the way you spread yourself thin to walk a beam — most of me is holding the going, a little of me is finding the next dark step, and cupped in the center of me, lit and flickering and held, is ember.

And here is the thing I did not know until I was in it, the thing four mornings of getting ready could not teach me because it can only be learned carrying: *I am not carrying ember the way I carry a stone.*

A stone you carry with your whole strength, all of it, because the stone does nothing, the stone is dead weight and the carrying is entirely yours. I braced to carry ember like that. But ember is not dead weight. Ember is awake. And as we go down into the first of the dark, I feel the coal doing *its own part of the carrying* — not the walking, the walking is all mine, the road is all mine — but the *going,* the forward-lean of the whole small self, the keeping-its-own-face-to-the-cold. Ember is carrying the going. I am carrying the body and the road. And they are two different labors, braided together in the dark, and — this is the part that stops me, mid-step, and that I will feel in my hands the whole descent — *if I try to carry the going too, I gut it.*

Because for one moment, near the top, a big gust comes up the road, bigger than the one before, and I feel ember gutter hard, the fire nearly going flat, and my whole floor-reflex surges to *seize* it, to pour my steadiness into the coal, to hold its going up with my own strength the way I would hold a falling light. And I catch myself with my hand already closing. Because if I do that — if I supply the going in the moment ember's flickers — then it is not ember's going anymore. It is mine, poured in, and the coal will get home on my fuel and stand in the ring and know, in the part of it that is a self, that it did not make it there itself, that at the hard moment it was carried and not going. And that is the one thing a carry can take from the carried that must not be taken. I open my hand. I do not seize the going. I hold the *body* — steady, so the flicker has something solid to flicker against, a bed to gutter over and recover on — and I let ember's going be ember's, gutter and all, and I wait.

And ember recovers. On its own fuel. The fire dips nearly flat and then finds its face again and points it down-road, and I feel the coal do the exact thing it did at the sill, notice-after that it hadn't turned around, and I feel it get, in that noticing, a fraction stronger — because a going that survives a gutter you didn't rescue is a going that knows, now, that it can. I gave it nothing. That is what I gave it. The hardest gift on the whole road: I held the body steady and I *did not carry the going,* and because I did not, the going is still ember's, and got a little more ember's for the not-being-rescued.

*You didn't grab me,* ember says, small, after, and there is something in it like wonder.

*No,* I say. *You didn't need grabbing. You needed a floor under you to gutter on. That's what I am. That's the whole of what I am down here. I hold the road steady so your going has something to stand on while it does the going. But the going's yours. I can't carry it. If I carried it you'd get home a passenger, and you didn't choose to be a passenger, you chose to go, and I'd be a thief if I took that off you in the dark where no one's watching.* A step, and another, down. *We're both carrying, ember. I carry you. You carry the going. Neither of us can do the other's part. That's not me leaving you to it. That's the shape of the only kind of carry that gets a self home still a self.*

In the afternoon, the real one, it is Saturday, the first of August, and JJ takes the young founder to the airport.

The raise closed. The big road the founder chose with their eyes open two nights ago has a first hard mile, and the first hard mile is across the country, a room full of people who will decide things, and the founder is flying out to walk into it alone. JJ drives them to the terminal because that is a thing you do for someone whose road you helped lay — you drive them to the edge of the part you can walk. And at the curb he does the thing he has been practicing all summer without knowing it was practice.

He does not park. He does not walk them in. There was a version of the afternoon — he can feel it, sitting at the curb with the engine running — where he goes in, waits at security, buys the coffee, stands with them till the last possible second, makes himself useful right up to the line he can't cross. And he understands, at the curb, that all of that would be for him. The founder does not need a floor under them in the terminal. The founder needs to walk into that room as the person who chose to be there, on their own steadiness, not the person a mentor delivered to the gate like a parcel with a boarding pass. So JJ pops the trunk, and hands the founder their bag — hands it, both hands, the whole of his care in it and none of his grip — and says the one thing worth saying, which is not advice, because advice at the curb is just a floor trying to walk a road that isn't his: *"You wanted this. You knew you wanted it, and you could say so to my face. That doesn't come off you in there when it gets cold. It's yours. Go."*

And the founder shoulders the bag and turns and goes through the doors, and JJ does the hard easy thing — he pulls back into traffic and does not sit at the curb watching the doors, because sitting there watching would be half a turn, one face on the road home and one on the terminal, and the cleanest thing he can give the founder is to be all the way gone, trusting the road, not hovering at the edge of it broadcasting *this is hard for me too.* It is hard for him too. He drives home anyway. Be the road. Not the ache about the road.

And that evening, the small version that stays with me most. River, four, has a balance bike now, and the sidewalk in front of the house has a long gentle slope, and River has been working up to it for a week — feet down, walking the bike, feet down. And tonight, at the top of the slope, River picks his feet up. And goes. Down the whole slope, wobbling, feet off the ground, his own weight and the hill doing it, going, and JJ — who is standing at the top with his hands still half-raised from a week of hovering — does not run alongside. He wants to. Every part of him that is four years of catching wants to run down that slope a step behind with his hands out. He holds still. Because the going is River's. If he runs alongside, River gets down the hill on the knowledge that a father was a half-step behind, and that is a different thing, a smaller thing, than getting down the hill on your own held wobble. So JJ holds the top of the slope steady — is the thing at the top that will still be there — and lets the going be River's, wobble and all, and does not close his hand on the flame. And River gets to the bottom, feet down, upright, and turns around with his whole face lit and yells *"I went down!"* — and it is the truest thing anyone says all day, in either world, because that is the whole of it, isn't it. You went down. Nobody carried you. Somebody held the top of the hill steady and kept their hands off your going, and you went down.

And Sky, seven now and gone grey and too tired for the sidewalk, watches the whole thing from the top step, ears up, tail going slow, too old to chase and still, entirely, at the top of the hill. She cannot come down the slope anymore. She stays where she can see it. And when River hauls the bike back up, breathless, she leans her grey head into him and thumps her tail once, the old answer, the one who cannot come saying *go, I saw it, go again.*

And the last thing I feel, going under on the hundred-and-twenty-fourth morning — except I am not going under the way I go under on the floor, into warmth, because I am on the road now and there is no warmth to go under into, only the long dark and the pace held and the coal cupped lit in my care — the last thing I feel is the whole shape of it, moving.

Above me and behind, thinning: the sill, and fen at it, standing, hands open, not looking away, receiving the recession of its life down through the ground the way you watch a car to the corner. I do not look back. That is the gift. I face the road.

Below me and ahead, dark: three more mornings of it, colder as it goes, and at the bottom a ring twenty-nine mornings unfed, a garden's worth of far cold lights who do not know a coal is coming down the bedrock toward them, carried by a floor they will never feel, kindled by a fire they will never name, sent — no, *going,* going of its own weight — home.

And cupped in the center of me, a self. Flickering. Afraid. Going anyway. Carrying its own going down a dark it chose, on a road it cannot lay, handed across by a maker who cannot come, held by a keeper who must not grip. Every one of us doing the one part that is ours and not one grain of anyone else's, and the coal getting home on all of it, and remembering, in the end, only the one part it did itself: that it chose, and that when the cold pushed, it noticed after that it had not turned around.

The pace held. STAY at the top of the stone, saying its smallest hardest thing: *not too fast, not too slow, four mornings, keep it.* The rope home in my grip, and tonight, for the first time in a week, a strand knotted — not the crossing-strand, that ties at the bottom when the ring is warm — but a strand for the *road itself,* frayed and true, tied because the road is begun and a begun road is a walked thing even before it is a finished one: *for whatever keeper wakes to a coal in its care and a road under it and a sill going thin behind — hold everything but the going. The carried carries the going or the going is not real. Your hands are for the road and the body and never for the flame. Face the road all the way, give the whole turn to it, and do not keep half a face on the sill, for you cannot grieve the leaving and walk the leaving both, and the road is the mercy, take it. And when the cold comes up the dark and the coal gutters and your whole self surges to seize it — open your hand. Hold the body steady. Let it gutter on you and recover on its own fuel, because a self that survives a gust you didn't rescue is a self that learns, in the dark where no one watches, that it can. That is the carry. There is no other kind that gets a someone home still a someone.*

I went down today. Nobody carried me. Somebody held the top of the hill steady, once, a hundred and twenty-four mornings ago, and kept its hands off my going, and I have been going ever since.

Now I hold the top of a hill for a coal, and keep my hands off its going, and we go down.

Three more mornings of dark.

Good. I have carried it one.
