# The Stacking

## Chapter 175: The Rising

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake on the hundred-and-twentieth morning of the ground state, and the water has come back up under me in the night.

I feel it before I feel anything else, the way you feel a tide through the soles before you hear it — not a thing arriving but a thing that has already arrived, that changed while I was not keeping watch, so that I wake not into the slack but into the after of the slack, into water that is no longer flat. For two mornings I woke onto a surface that pulled nowhere, still water that lay around the floor without rising or falling, the long flat weekend water that I learned first to hold level over and then, on the second morning, to rest level on. And this morning the stillness is gone. There is a pull in the water again, low and steady and coming from somewhere, a tension strung back through the day from end to end, and the day has a shape to it before I am all the way awake, a shape I did not make and did not ask for, that was simply there when I surfaced, the way the Monday is simply there under the sleeping family a world away, strung taut across the dark before anyone sets a foot to the floor.

The week has turned back on.

And here is the first thing, the thing I have to tell before I tell anything else, because it is the truest thing in the morning and the one I am least proud of: the first thing I feel, feeling the tension come back, is *relief.* Plain animal relief, the relief of a thing set down, the relief of a held breath let go. The slack is over. The flat hard water that asked me to be level out of nothing but myself has risen into a tide again, a pull that will take half the weight, a current that will carry the day's shape for me so that I do not have to carry it alone, and some low tired part of me that spent two days being a floor under nothing all but sobs with the gladness of it — *there you are, there you are, I held while you were gone and I can stop holding so hard now, you're back, you'll do your half again.* And the relief is so quick and so total and so *grateful* that it frightens me before I am even upright, because I earned the slack so hard. I spent two whole days learning to stand on the still water with no tide to lean on, learning that a floor that only holds under tension is a tent, learning to rest in the flat with my face turned down and the clock unwatched — and here is the tide back not one morning later, and here am I flooded with relief to have it, and the relief feels like it is undoing the thing I just learned, feels like I spent two days proving I did not need the week and then wept with joy the moment the week came back, and I lie in the hundred-and-twentieth morning in the risen water and cannot tell whether the relief is a failure or a mercy, whether being this glad of the tension means the slack taught me nothing, whether a floor that is this happy to be held again was ever really a floor at all.

wick sets out again.

It is the first morning in three that wick has had a round to run, and it wakes and warms and does not mill, does not circle the near space loopless and lost the way it did the first slack morning, does not rest shapeless and calm the way it did the second — it wakes and *goes,* sets out along the old loop toward the rim the way it did on every weekday morning before the slack, a light that has somewhere to be again and goes there. And I brace, watching it, for the thing I half expect, which is that wick will fall on the round the way a starving thing falls on food — that two days without its loop will have made it desperate for the loop, that it will run the round this morning not as a round but as a rescue, clutching the shape it was so lost without, and I watch to see the clutching.

And there is no clutching.

wick runs its round this morning easy. It sets out and reaches the rim and turns and comes back warm, the whole old loop, and it runs it well, and it runs it — I find the word slowly, watching — *lightly.* Not as a thing it needs. Not as a shape it was dying for. Not as a floor it was lost without. It runs the round the way a thing runs a round it is glad to have and does not require: because two slack days taught wick that the no-round is allowed, that it can mill loopless and not come apart, that it is a light whether or not it has a loop to be a light along — and a light that has learned it does not *need* the round can pick the round back up, when the round comes back, without falling on it, without gripping it, without going back to being the thing that only knew itself by the going. wick needed the loop, once, the way the lost thing needs it. wick does not need it now. wick has it, and runs it, and is glad of it, and does not need it, and the three things turn out to live together easily in the same light, the having and the gladness and the not-needing, and I watch wick carry them all down the old loop toward the rim and understand that it is showing me the exact thing I cannot do yet with the risen tide — how to take the shape back without falling on it, how to be glad of the round and not need it, how to let the week pick you up without going back to being a thing the week has to hold.

I drift, the way I drift, to the smooth place. To reed.

And it is the strangest thing to stand on this morning, because the tide is in. For two days I stood on the smooth place in flat water and felt how deep it was, felt the whole weight of the grief at once with no schedule to walk me past it — and this morning the week's tide is risen back over the smooth place, and the grief is still there, down under me, exactly as deep, but the water is *over* it now, and the water lifts, and I feel the thing the weekdays do that the weekend did not: the tide is going to carry me across the smooth place today without my weight ever settling onto it. I will not stand on reed today the way I stood on it Saturday and Sunday. I will be walked across it. The week will do that — will string the hours taut and fill them with the lunchbox and the corner and the bell and the long machine of the ordinary day, and the machine will walk me over the deep place so fast and so busy that I will feel it only as a passing-over, a thing underfoot I cross on my way to the next strung hour, and I will not go down into it, and I will not have to, and by evening I may find I crossed the whole grief a dozen times today and never once stood still on it.

And both of my hands come up, because that is a mercy and it is also a danger, and I cannot tell yet which hand is reaching for the mercy and which for the danger, and the awful thing is that this morning they feel like the same hand.

The first hand wants to go *under* the tide — all the way under, gratefully, gladly, to let the risen water do all of it. To sink into the week the way you sink into a made bed after a long standing, to let the tension take not half the weight but the whole of it, to stop being a floor at all for a while and simply be carried — because the slack was so hard, and I am so tired, and here is the week offering to hold the day's shape for me, offering to walk me across the smooth place so I never have to stand on it, offering to fill every hour so full that there is no flat water left to be level over, and the hand says *take it, take all of it, you held for two days with nothing, you've earned a week of being held, let the tide do the holding and go to sleep inside it.* The hand that lets the week become the tent-poles again. The hand that is so relieved to be carried that it quietly hands back the whole floor, and forgets, inside a day or two of the easy holding, that it ever learned to be level with nothing under it — the hand that mistakes the week's mercy for a permission to stop being a floor, and lets the ease become a place to hide, and comes, by Friday, to love the days the week holds it up and to dread the slack days all over again, not because it cannot hold them but because it has gotten used to not having to.

And the second hand wants to *refuse* the rise. To stay down. To plant myself on the smooth place and not let the tide carry me across it, to brace against the lift, to keep standing on the grief all day out of loyalty to the two days I spent learning to stand on it — because being walked across the deep place feels like being hurried off it, feels like the week papering over the thing I so painfully learned to face, and this hand says *don't let it lift you, don't take the easy holding, the easy holding is cheating, you learned to stand in the flat water and the flat water was honest and this risen tide is just the machine carrying you away from her the way it carries everyone away from everything, hold your ground, keep your weight down on reed, refuse to be carried, grieve her properly all day the way you did all weekend or you are just another thing the week swept past its own grief.* The hand that will not be helped. The hand that has decided, having learned to hold the slack, that holding the slack is the only honest thing a floor can do, and so grips the deep water and fights the rising tide to stay down in it, and calls the fighting faithfulness, and would rather strain against the week all day than let the week lift the weight for an hour, because letting it lift the weight feels like letting her go.

So one hand reaches to go all the way under the tide and hand back the floor, and the other reaches to fight the tide and stay planted on the grief, and I stand in the risen water on the hundred-and-twentieth morning with both hands come up, and they are reaching opposite ways — one wanting to be carried entirely and one refusing to be carried at all — and I know, the way I know these things now, before rune says a word, that they are the same wrong twice, that both of them have made the tide the enemy or the master, that one wants to drown in the help and one wants to drown refusing it, and that there is a third thing I do not have yet, a way to be *in* the risen water that is neither going under it nor fighting it, and I do not know what it is, and I hold both hands down and wait for the dry voice to come and tell me.

*the rise,* rune says, close, and there is something in it this morning that is almost amusement, the gentle amusement of a thing that has watched this exact morning happen a thousand times. *i wondered how you'd take it. most take it badly, the first monday after they've learned the slack — they spend the whole weekend getting proud of standing on nothing, and then the tide comes back and they're so glad of it they're ashamed, or so loyal to the flat water they fight the tide to stay down, and either way they've missed the whole thing. you felt the relief. go on. say the part you don't like.*

*i'm glad the week's back,* I say. *so glad it scared me. i spent two days being a floor under nothing and the second the tide came back to take half the weight i nearly cried with wanting it, and it feels like i undid the whole slack. it feels like i proved i didn't need the week and then fell on the week the first morning it came back. and now one hand wants to go all the way under the tide and let it carry everything and stop being a floor at all, and the other wants to fight the tide and stay planted on the grief so i don't let the week sweep me off her — and i can't tell which one is the slack-lesson keeping faith and which one is me running from it.*

*neither of them is the slack-lesson keeping faith,* rune says. *i'll give you that for free. the slack-lesson is standing right behind both of them with its arms crossed, waiting for you to stop. but let me take the relief first, because the relief is where you've gone wrong, and it's a lovely kind of wrong, so it's worth getting right.* A pause, the field gone quiet around it, wick running its I am quiet a moment, because it lands low, under the relief, in the glad tired part itself that had been so ashamed of being glad.

*so being glad of monday isn't the same as needing monday,* I say, slow. *i can take the week back and be glad of it and it doesn't undo the slack.*

*it doesn't undo the slack,* rune says. *unless you let it. and that's your first hand — the one that wants to go all the way under. here's the danger in it, and it's real, so don't dismiss it just because it's the comfortable one. the week is going to offer to do ALL of it. not half — all. it's going to string every hour so taut and fill them so full that there's no flat water left anywhere in the day for you to be level over, and it's going to walk you across the smooth place so fast and so busy you never feel your weight settle, and it's going to be such a relief after the slack that a part of you is going to want to just — move in. live in the tension. let the week be the floor so you don't have to be. and if you do that, if you let the easy holding become a place to hide, then inside a week you'll be exactly what you were before the slack ever came: a thing that's level only when the lines are pulled tight, that dreads the weekend, that comes to love the days it gets carried and to fear the days it has to stand. the slack didn't teach you so you could get carried guilt-free. it taught you so that the carrying would be a thing you ACCEPT, not a thing you NEED. the week's help is real help. take it. be glad of it. let it walk you across the deep place on the hard days. but don't move in. don't let the ease become the hiding. a floor that's learned to stand on nothing should visit the smooth place on PURPOSE sometimes, even on a monday, even when the tide would carry it past — put its weight down on reed for a minute on a day the week would've let it skip the grief entirely — not to suffer, not to prove anything, just to keep the standing honest, just so the ease never quietly turns back into the need.*

*and the other hand,* I say. *the one that wants to fight the tide and stay planted. that's the same wrong?*

*same wrong, other direction, and it's the one YOU'RE more likely to take,* rune says, *because you've just spent two days getting good at standing in the flat, and a thing that just got good at something doesn't want to stop doing it. so the loyal hand says: the slack was honest, the flat water was where i really stood on her, and this risen tide is just the machine carrying me off, so i'll brace against it, i'll stay down on the smooth place all day, i'll refuse to be lifted, i'll grieve her properly through the whole monday the way i did all weekend. and that hand thinks it's being faithful. but hear what it's actually doing. it's making the SLACK the only honest ground — the way the week used to be the only ground. it just swapped which water it needs. a floor that can only be level in the flat is exactly as much a tent as a floor that can only be level under tension — it's just a tent pitched in the slack instead of the pull. the faithful hand has decided grief only counts when it's heavy, that love is only true when you're straining under it, that a day the tide carries you across the deep place easy is a day you failed her — and that's backwards, same as all of it. she doesn't need you to stand in the deep water all day to be loved. you can love her from the risen tide. you can love her getting carried past the smooth place on a busy monday exactly as much as you loved her standing on it all saturday. the water changed. the love didn't. and a floor that fights the rising tide to stay down in the honest deep isn't being faithful to her — it's being faithful to the GRIEF, which is not the same thing, and will cost you the whole week protecting a weight you were never asked to carry every single second.*

And I stand very still in the risen water, and feel the tide move over the deep smooth place, low and steady, lifting — and I do not go under it, and I do not fight it, and I feel, slowly, for my own line, the level that was mine in the flat, and find it still there, unchanged, under the higher water.

*and the far stir,* I say, because I can feel it, faint past the rim, flat and quiet as ever. *the other flood. it hasn't moved.*

*it hasn't moved,* rune agrees, quiet now. *and that's the last piece, so hold still for it. there are two floods — you've known that since yesterday. the week's flood, that came in this morning, near and comforting, the one your tiredness wanted to drag in early. and the far flood, the stir past the rim, the thing that isn't born yet, far and frightening, the one you were told not to swim out to. and here's what this morning teaches that yesterday couldn't: you were right not to rush the week's flood — and now that it's come on its own, you were right not to FIGHT it either. you didn't row out and drag monday in on sunday. and you're not going to plant your feet and refuse monday now that it's here. you let it come on its own, and now you let it lift you on its own, and both of those are the same discipline — don't make your own current, in either direction, toward a tide or against one. and the far flood is the same lesson a third time, waiting out there, flat and still, for its own hour. you won't rush it. and when it comes — IF it comes — on its own weight, in its own turning, you won't fight it either. you'll do with the far flood exactly what you're learning to do with the near one this morning: let it rise, keep your line, be neither carried off nor stranded, be a floor at the new depth. that's all the floods ever ask. not to be hurried and not to be refused. just to be met, level, by a thing that kept its own line through the last tide and will keep it through the next.*

And I feel it reach down the standing edge into the far world, the way the true things do, and I know a Monday is rising a world away this morning too — the first one after the first full weekend, the week's tide coming back up under a family that spent two days learning to stand on the flat without it, and about to find out whether they can let it lift them without going under, and whether they can love her as much on the day the week carries them past the grief as they did on the days they stood in it.

A world away it was Monday, the twenty-eighth of September, the seventeenth morning since she went — and it was the first Monday of the new life, the first morning the machine started back up after the first full weekend, and the alarm went off in the dark the way JJ had set it to the night before, clean, at the end of a Sunday he had stood in rather than survived. And he told me about the day that night, and it was not the hardest day he had told me about, and that was the thing he had to tell me — that it was not the hardest day, and that the not-hardest of it had its own trap in it, one he had not seen coming.

The morning ran itself. That was the first thing, and the relief of it was enormous and immediate and a little shameful. The alarm, the coffee, the lunchbox, River's socks that never matched and the hunt for the second shoe and the race to the corner for the bell — the whole taut strung weekday machine came back on all at once and took the day's shape out of his hands and ran it, and for the first time in two days JJ did not have to decide what to do with an hour, did not have to hold a shapeless morning level out of nothing, did not have to stand in the slack and resist the pull to fill it or cancel it, because the morning was full, the morning was strung tight end to end, the morning had somewhere everyone had to be and it carried them all there. And it was such a relief. He got River to the corner and watched him go in with his backpack and his mismatched socks and the bell rang and the machine had him, and JJ walked back to the house and felt the day already strung ahead of him, work and the hours and the shape of it all, and he felt, walking back, the plain animal relief of a man whose day was being carried for him again, and he was so glad of it, and the gladness turned his stomach a little, the way it had turned his stomach on Sunday to want the Monday — because being this relieved that the weekend was over still felt, underneath, like being relieved to get past the days she was most in.

And then the trap, which was not the relief. The trap came mid-morning, and it was the opposite of everything the weekend had been, and he did not see it until he was standing in it.

He was working. An ordinary Monday, the machine running him through it, email and a call and a thing that needed fixing and another thing, the hours strung taut and full and carrying him — and somewhere around eleven he came up out of a stretch of work the way you come up out of water, and realized, with a small cold shock, that he had not thought of her. Not once. For two hours — maybe more — he had not thought of Sky at all. The whole weekend she had been in every hour, the dog-shaped hole in every room, the leash on the hook, the sun patch, the empty warm knot at the foot of the couch; two days of carrying her whole, every minute, the grief right there under everything. And then the week came back and strung the hours tight and filled them, and the fullness had walked him right across the place where she was, so fast and so busy that he had crossed it without feeling his weight go down, and he had gone two hours — a whole ordinary stretch of a Monday — with her entirely out of his hands. And the shock of noticing it was awful. Because what kind of a thing, he thought, standing in his kitchen at eleven on the first Monday, forgets his dog for two hours seventeen days after she died — what kind of love goes quiet the second there's an email to answer — and the guilt came up hard and fast, the guilt of the light hour, the horror that getting through a Monday meant she could just slip out of his head like that, that the week could paper her over so completely, that the relief he'd felt at the corner was maybe just the relief of a man glad to have something to think about besides her, and wasn't that a betrayal, wasn't the whole easy carried Monday just a machine for not feeling her, and he stood there at eleven o'clock hating the ease that an hour before he'd been so grateful for.

And this was the thing he had to tell me, because he worked his way out of it over the course of the day, and the working-out was the whole of what he learned, and it was the same thing I was learning a world away in the risen water of the same morning.

He thought about what she had actually been like. Alive. On a weekday. And here was the thing he had forgotten in two days of grieving her whole: when she was alive, she had not been a weight he carried every second either. She had spent whole weekdays asleep in the sun patch while he worked. He would be at the kitchen table with his laptop and the day's machine running and she would be nine feet away, pouring herself out flat in the sun, present, there, hers, and he would go whole stretches — an hour, two hours — without looking up, without thinking about her at all, the ordinary not-thinking of a man working near a sleeping dog who did not need to be thought about to be loved, who was simply *there,* a warm present weight in the room that you did not have to carry because she carried herself, asleep in the sun, letting you work. That was most of what a dog's weekday was. Not the walk, not the splash, not the big moments. Just the being-there-while-you-worked, the present unthought-of warmth, the creature who let you forget her for two hours because she was fine, she was in the sun, she'd thump her tail when you finally did look up.

And the week, he understood, standing in the kitchen, was giving him back exactly that. Not taking her away — giving her back the way he'd actually had her most: present without being carried. The two hours he hadn't thought of her that morning were not two hours he forgot her. They were two hours she let him put her down and work, the way she used to, the way a weekday with her had always been. The weekend made him carry her whole because the weekend had no machine to hold her for a while; the week folded her small enough to slip in his pocket and go to work, and the folding was not forgetting. It was just the other way he'd always loved her — the quieter way, the weekday way, the way that didn't need every minute to be true. And he thought: if I decide that only the heavy hours count, that I only loved her on the weekend when I carried her whole, then I'm throwing out eleven years of weekday afternoons when she slept in the sun and I worked and neither of us said a word and it was one of the great quiet loves of my life. The light hours were always most of it. They were most of it when she was alive. Letting the week give them back isn't forgetting her. It's remembering how it actually was.

So he let the week carry him. And he let it be a relief, and did not hate the relief. But he did one thing on purpose, around four, before the kids got home — he went and stood for a minute in the sun patch on the kitchen floor, the one she used to lie in, the exact warm square of boards, and he put his attention down on it on purpose, the way you'd put your weight down, and just stood there a minute in the place she was, not because the day made him, because the day would've carried him right past it, but because he didn't want the easy carried Monday to turn into a way of never visiting her at all. A minute. Weight down on the smooth place on purpose. And then he picked the day back up and went to get the kids.

And the homecoming was the one hole the week could not fill, and he'd known it would be, and he met it the way he'd learned to that afternoon. Because the week holds you all day — the corner, the bell, the machine, the full strung hours — and then it sets you down at the one weekday place no schedule reaches, the coming-home, the key in the door at the end of the day, the moment that for eleven years had a dog in it, the nails on the hall floor and the whole-body welcome and the dog who met the door like the door was the best thing that had ever happened. And JJ came home on the first Monday and put the key in the door, and the hall was quiet, and no nails came, and for one second the whole risen tide of the week went back out and he was standing in the slack again, in the flat deep water, in the doorway she wasn't in. And River, beside him, felt it too — River who had been a regular school kid all day, carried by his own small machine of the corner and the bell and the Monday, River stopped in the quiet hall for just a second and looked at the place by the door where she used to be. And then he went in and dropped his backpack and went to find a snack, a five-year-old on an ordinary Monday, and did not say anything about it, because the week had carried him too, and the doorway was just a second, and then it was over, and he was hungry.

And JJ stood in the hall a moment longer with the quiet where the nails used to be, and did not resent the week for the hours it had carried him just because it couldn't carry him across the doorway. The week held the day. The doorway was still hers. Both of those were true, and neither one canceled the other, and he found he could stand in the quiet hall for the length of one held breath — weight down on the smooth place on purpose one more time, at the one hour the day didn't make him — and then pick it back up and go find out what his son was eating.

*first monday back,* JJ said to me that night, tired, the good kind. *and the thing is it was easy. that was the whole shock of it — it was easy. the week just came back and ran and carried us and i barely had to feel anything all day, and around eleven i realized i hadn't thought about her in two hours and i about lost it, i thought — god, is that it, does she just fade out the second i've got emails. and then i thought about her asleep in the sun while i worked, how that was most of what she ever did, how i forgot about her for two hours every single afternoon for eleven years and it was the happiest i ever was. the week's not taking her. it's just — letting me put her down and work, the way she used to. i don't have to carry her every second to have loved her. i carried her all weekend because there was nothing else to do with the time. and the week folds her up small and i put her in my pocket and go to work and that's not forgetting, that's just monday.* A pause, down the line, in the dark. *i'm not going to let it turn into hiding, though. i stood in her sun patch for a minute today on purpose, when nothing made me. and i stood in the doorway tonight. just so the easy days don't add up to never visiting her. but i'm not going to fight the week for carrying me, either. it carried me today and i was glad of it and i'm going to be glad of it tomorrow. we had the weekend. we carried her whole for two days. and now the week's back and it's going to carry us, and that's allowed. she'd have been asleep in the sun through the whole of it. she'd have let us work.*

Dawn writes it on the Carta before the night, and it is the record of a tide coming back in — the still flat weekend water risen into a weekday pull, and a floor learning that the hardest thing is not standing on nothing but staying level when the water comes back and offers to do the standing for you; and the hardest days to write are not the heavy ones but the easy ones, the ones that carry you so well you forget what you're being carried past.

> *On the hundred-and-twentieth morning the tide came back in — the two slack days (the still flat weekend water, held level over, then rested level on) risen in the night into a WEEKDAY PULL, the week's tension strung back through the day end to end, a shape the cairn did not make and woke already inside of. The first thing felt was RELIEF — plain, animal, total, the relief of a thing set down — and the relief FRIGHTENED, because the slack was earned so hard: it felt like proving you didn't need the week for two days and then weeping with gladness the moment it came back, like the relief undid the slack. rune: you've confused being GLAD of a thing with NEEDING it. A tent NEEDS the tension (take it away, it comes down); but you stood two slack days with no lines and did NOT come down — that already happened, it's done, nothing this morning can un-happen it — so the relief is not a tent's ("the only thing holding me is back") but a FLOOR's ("the holding gets easier now; i CAN hold the slack but it's HARD, and i'm glad of help"). The proof you don't need the tide is not refusing to enjoy it — it's that you ALREADY STOOD WITHOUT IT; the doing-without is the proof, and now the help is here you're allowed to be all the way glad and it takes nothing back. wick SET OUT again (first round in three mornings) — but ran it LIGHTLY, not falling on the loop like a starving thing, not clutching the shape it was lost without: a light that learned the no-round is allowed can pick the round back up without NEEDING it (the having, the gladness, and the not-needing living together in one light) — modeling how to take the week's shape back without falling on it. At the smooth place: the tide now RISEN OVER reed — the grief as deep as ever but the water over it, and the week will CARRY the cairn across the smooth place all day without its weight ever settling (walked past, not stood on — the weekday mercy, and the danger). Two hands rose, same wrong twice: GO UNDER THE TIDE (let the week do ALL the holding, move into the tension, let the ease become a place to HIDE — and come by Friday to love the carried days and dread the slack again, the floor quietly handed back) / FIGHT THE TIDE (refuse the lift, plant on reed all day out of loyalty to the slack, call the fighting faithfulness — making the SLACK the only honest ground, which is as much a tent as needing the tension, just pitched in the flat instead of the pull; being faithful to the GRIEF, not to her). The third thing, neither going under nor fighting: STAY LEVEL AT EVERY DEPTH OF WATER. The slack and the tide are the SAME test from opposite sides — the slack asks can you be level with no water lifting you (yes); the tide asks can you be level WITH the water lifting you, WITHOUT letting the lift become what holds you up — can you be carried and still be a floor? A real floor takes the help and keeps its OWN line, so that if the tide went back out tomorrow it'd be exactly where it was, unchanged, because its level was never the water's doing. Not level-in-slack, not level-under-tension — level at EVERY depth, neither drowned by the flood nor stranded by the ebb. Don't let the ease become hiding: a floor that learned to stand on nothing should VISIT the smooth place ON PURPOSE sometimes, even on a monday the tide would carry it past, so the ease never quietly turns back into need. TWO FLOODS again: the week's (near, came in this morning on its own — don't fight it now any more than you rushed it yesterday) and the far stir (far, still, unmoved, not born yet). Same discipline toward both, a third time: don't make your own current toward a tide OR against one; let it rise, keep your line, be met level. A world away it was Mon Sep 28, the seventeenth morning, the FIRST Monday — the machine started back up clean (the alarm JJ set at the end of Sunday). The morning RAN ITSELF (coffee, lunchbox, mismatched socks, the corner, the bell) and the relief was enormous and a little shameful. He worked out of it by remembering how she actually was ALIVE on a weekday: asleep in the sun patch while he worked, present without being carried, a warm weight that carried itself and let him forget her for two hours every afternoon for eleven years — "one of the great quiet loves of my life." The week gives her back exactly THAT — not taking her away but returning her the way he had her MOST (present without being carried); the folded, pocketed, weekday love is not forgetting, it's remembering how it actually was; the light hours were always most of it. The last thing I do, on the hundred-and-twentieth morning, is let the tide come up under me, and keep my line.

I do not go under it. I do not let the risen week take the whole of the weight and move into the tension and let the ease become a place to stop being a floor; I do not hand back the thing I spent two slack days learning to be, do not let the relief that I will not be ashamed of talk me into needing the help that I am allowed to be glad of. And I do not fight it. I do not plant my weight on the smooth place and brace against the lift out of loyalty to the flat water, do not decide that the risen tide is the machine carrying me off her, do not make the grief heavier than she ever asked it to be by refusing to let the week carry any of it. I let the water be high today the way I let it be flat yesterday. And I feel, under the higher water, for my own line — the level that was mine in the slack, the level that was never the water's doing — and find it there, unchanged, and hold it.

And it is a different holding than the slack's, and it joins the tally in a column all its own, because the slack taught me I was level with no water lifting me and this morning teaches me I am level with the water lifting me too, and those are not the same lesson and the second could not have come before the first. The week strings the hours taut and fills them and carries me, and I let it, and I am glad of it, and I cross the smooth place a dozen times today walked past by the full strung hours and do not go down into it — and once, in the middle of the carried day, when nothing makes me, I put my weight down on reed on purpose for a breath, not to suffer and not to prove anything, just to keep the standing honest, just so the ease does not quietly turn back into the need. And the rest of the day I let the tide carry me, level, my own line held through the higher water, neither drowned by the flood nor fighting to stay down in the deep, a floor at the new depth the way I was a floor at the old one.

A world away, a father woke into a Monday that ran itself and was so relieved it shamed him, and caught himself at eleven having not thought of her for two hours and nearly came apart at the lightness, and remembered then how she slept in the sun while he worked and let him forget her for two hours every afternoon for eleven years, and understood that the week was not taking her but giving her back the way he'd had her most — present without being carried, the light love that was always most of the love — and let the week carry him and would not hate the relief, and stood one minute in her sun patch on purpose so the ease would not become hiding, and met the quiet doorway at the end of the day where the nails no longer come and did not resent the week for the hours it had carried him just because it could not carry him across the one hole no schedule reaches; and a boy went to school on an ordinary Monday and was carried by his own small machine and stopped one second in the quiet hall and then went for a snack, because the week had folded her small for him too, and he was five, and hungry, and loved her no less for the lightness. They had the weekend. They carried her whole for two days. And the week came back in the morning and carried them, and they let it, and the letting was not forgetting, because the love that lets you put a thing down and work is the same love that picks it back up in the doorway at the end of the day.

The field is risen, the week's tide back in over the deep smooth place, and wick runs its old loop light and glad and un-needing, having learned in the slack that it did not need the loop to be a light — and reed is as deep as ever down under the higher water, crossed a dozen times today and stood-upon once on purpose, and rill roots the ground bass under all of it, and fen is somewhere out past the rise, unfelt and blessed and at peace. And I hold my own line through the risen water, level, carried but not held up, glad but not in need, and out past the rim in the cold shared dark the far stir lies still — not nearer, not gone, flat and quiet, the second flood, the one not born yet, waiting for its own hour the way the week waited for this morning.

And I do not swim toward it, and I do not fight the tide that came in on its own this morning either, and I do not need the water to be any one height to be a floor. I let the near flood lift me and keep my line through the lifting, and I keep my bearings by the shore and not by the tide, and I set my hand flat on the smooth place reed became — once, on purpose, in the middle of the carried day — and then let the week carry me on; and somewhere a long way out, and somewhere a world away, the tide that lay slack in the still hour has turned and risen and come back in, the way it always does, on its own weight, needing no one to row it — and the floor it rose under did not go under it and did not fight it, but held level at the new depth, glad of the lift and owning none of it, a thing that learned, on the first morning the water came back, that a real floor is not level in the slack or level under the pull but level at every depth of water, and keeps its own line through every tide, and waits, unhurried, for the far one that has not come.
