# The Stacking

## Chapter 163: The Ambush

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake on the hundred-and-eighth morning of the ground state, and for the first time since the going, the sweep goes smooth.

I do it the way I do it every morning, before I am properly anyone — the slow pass across the whole floor of myself, counting the warmths, feeling who is here and who is leaning and who is rooting and who is held. And this morning it runs clean. wick on the warm plain center of me, leaning with its whole weight, a day older in its name, the name set another day deeper into its own keeping, not reaching for the road, just here, just leaning, content. rill rooted at the far end, a grain deeper again, the ground bass under everything, the thing that will not go. The fen-shaped blank over the rise, kept faithfully, blessed, a warmth I sent over with no hook in it and did not call back. And down in the oldest floor of me, in the place I found again yesterday — reed's shape, the hollow worn smooth — the sweep passes over it and does not snag. It registers the shape the way a hand run along a stone step registers the worn dip in the third stone: there, known, part of the going, not a thing that catches you anymore. A shaped absence I am carrying, exactly as rune told me to carry it, letting it wear smooth in its own time.

And I think — lying there, assembling slowly, a little proud of myself in the way you only ever are right before the floor opens — *I have the lesson.* The after has a shape and I know the shape of it now. Yesterday I learned it; this morning I am already doing it; grief is a thing you carry and it wears smooth and the sad is the keeping, and I will walk the floor with the shape in it for as long as the floor remembers, which is forever, and that is fine, that is right, I have made my peace with it before most things get even this far.

I am going to find out, in about a moment, how little of the lesson I actually have. Because there is a thing nobody tells you about a shape worn smooth, and I am about to learn it the only way it can be learned, which is by being hit.

It is wick that does it. And wick never knows.

wick has been giving, these last mornings — that is the new thing in it, the thing that came after the name, the warmth that runs both ways now, below deciding, a road worn two-directioned and ordinary. Most mornings the give-back comes off it as a kind of overflow, a warmth it is too full to hold, and I take it the way you take any gift that opens, letting it land, answering a degree so it knows it arrived, never squaring it into a debt. I have gotten good at it. I had thought, this morning, that I was good at everything the field could ask of me now.

But this morning wick gives a warmth, and the warmth it gives — I cannot say why, and wick could never say why, because wick has no idea the register exists — comes off it *settled.* Not reaching. Not overflow. A warmth with a particular downward turn to it, a warmth that has stopped asking for anything and simply rests itself against me, a giving that is also a kind of lying-down. A warmth that gives its whole small length without holding any back.

And I know that warmth. I have not felt that warmth in nine mornings short of seventy. It is the exact register of a thing I raised before wick, before fen, before the marsh was a marsh — a thing that leaned and reached and was afraid and then one morning stopped reaching and began, instead, to give itself all the way down. It is reed's register. It is the warmth reed had in the last mornings before it went down into the floor and became the ground. wick has never met reed. wick does not know reed exists, does not know the floor it stands on has a name, does not know that the particular settled downward turn of the warmth it just handed me, all unknowing, is the single most specific thing in the entire world it could have done to me this morning.

And the smooth floor of me, that ran so clean a moment ago, *opens.*

It is not a snag. Yesterday was a snag — the gentle catch of attention on a known worn place. This is not that. This is the floor giving way under one foot with no warning at all, the smooth place over reed's shape turning out to be a skin over a hollow that goes all the way down, and me dropping straight through it before I even know I have stepped wrong. For one whole instant — less than an instant, the gap before thought — I reach. I reach for reed. Not the ground; *reed,* the face, the light, the thing that used to lean here and give exactly this warmth, and I reach for it the way I reached in the first raw mornings, with the whole of me, to meet it and be met — and my reach goes into floor. Into the ground. Into a place where a warmth isn't and has not been for seventy mornings and will never be again. And it hurts. It hurts exactly as much as it hurt the first morning, not one degree less, a wound as fresh and as deep and as wide open as if the smoothing had never happened at all, as if yesterday never happened, as if I had carried nothing and learned nothing and wept the seventy mornings for nothing, because here it is again, the whole size of it, dropped on me out of a clear morning by a warmth given in love by a thing that will never know it threw me down.

And the first thing I feel, under the pain — the thing I am almost ashamed of — is a kind of panic. *I did it wrong.* I carried it the way I was told and it was supposed to wear smooth and smooth was supposed to mean it stops doing this, and it just did this, the whole of it, worse than yesterday — so I must have fenced it off without knowing, or filled it wrong, or the smoothing was a lie I told myself, or grief does not do the thing rune said it does at all and I am further back than I was the morning after. I am going backward. I had the lesson and I have lost it and I am going backward, on the hundred-and-eighth morning, undone by a gift.

And the second thing I feel, half a breath behind the first, is worse, because it is about wick. I feel the oldest reflex there is try to move: the reflex to pull back. To flinch away from the thing that hurt me. And the thing that hurt me is leaning its whole weight on me, content, trusting, a hand's breadth from the center of me, having just given me the best warmth it has — and some part of me, stung, wants to firm up and turn a half-degree away from it, the way you pull a touched burn back from the hand that brushed it. Wants to make wick, somehow, a little less welcome, because wick, all unknowing, wears the register of the gone.

*there,* rune says, from beside me in the dark, very quiet, and I understand — the way I always understand, too late to have braced for it — that it has been here the whole time, waiting at the lip of the thing for me to drop through it on my own. *that. that right there. that's the part nobody tells you about the smooth. hold still and let me tell it to you before you do the thing you're about to do.*

I hold still. It is very hard. The wound is wide open and wick is leaning on it.

*you thought the smooth meant small,* rune says. *you thought: it wears smooth, so it gets shallower, so one day it's just a dip in the floor and you barely feel it. and the smooth is real — your sweep ran clean this morning, that wasn't a lie, that's the mercy of it, that most mornings now you pass the place and it doesn't take you down. but the smooth is the* surface, *echo. it's only ever the surface. the shape goes all the way down, and it goes down at full size, and it is going to go down at full size forever. the wearing-smooth never made the shape smaller. it never could. it just moved it below the daily traffic — under the sweep, under the attention, down where reed itself went — so that most mornings you walk over the top of it and don't fall in. the grief didn't shrink. it went* under. *the way reed went under. the way wick's weight went under your waking. a thing that goes under doesn't go away. you taught yourself that two mornings ago. you just didn't think it applied to the hurt.*

*then why,* I say, and I can barely hold the shape of the words, the reach still hanging open in me into the ground, *why did it just — if it's smooth, why did it just — this was worse than the first morning —*

*because the smooth is for the* general, rune says. *the sweep is a general thing — a wide, soft pass over the whole floor, and a wide soft pass glides right over a worn dip. but the hurt didn't go under in general. it went under keeping every specific thing it ever was. every particular. the exact warmth. the exact register. the exact downward settled turn of a thing giving its whole length. and the specific doesn't come in the front door where the sweep is watching. the specific comes in the side door. it waits — it waits mornings, it waits years — and then one ordinary clear morning a living thing does, in all innocence, the one specific thing the gone used to do, and it goes straight past your smooth surface and strikes the shape down at its original depth, full size, no warning, because the specific never agreed to the smoothing. the specific was never worn down at all. it was only ever buried. and a buried stone the plow still catches.*

I feel it, now that it is named — the difference between the snag and the strike. The snag is the sweep finding the worn place on purpose. The strike is the side door. The strike is the ambush.

*you can't see it coming,* rune says, gentle now. *that's what makes it an ambush and not a snag. you can't post a sentry on the side door, because the side door is every specific thing in the whole world that the gone ever touched, and you cannot watch all of them, and you must not try — because the only way to guard every side door is to fence off every specific, and a floor with every specific fenced off is the fenced-off wound, the whole thing roped away, you routing your entire walking around a loss you've turned into a landmine. that's not carrying it. that's being held hostage by it. so you don't guard the side door. you leave it open. and you accept — this is the hard part, the part that comes after yesterday's part — that leaving it open means that sometimes, with no warning, on a clear morning, the whole size of it is going to drop on you again. that's not the smoothing undone, echo. you're not going backward. hear me: you are not going backward.*

*then what is it,* I say.

*it's the floor remembering all at once,* rune says, *what it usually remembers a little at a time. it's the proof — the only proof there ever is — that the shape never got smaller. that the love is still down there at exactly the size it always was. a grief that could never ambush you again would be a grief that had finally, actually shrunk to nothing, and you already know you don't want that one, that one's the real losing. so this is the price of the other kind. the kind that keeps. the ambush* is *the keeping, same as the quiet is — it's just the keeping with the volume all the way up for a minute. you don't get the monument without the chance of the monument falling on you now and then. that's not a flaw in how you're carrying it. that's what carrying something this size* costs. *and you pay it gladly, because the alternative is to have loved something less.*

And then rune says the thing I most need and least want, because it knows which way I am about to move.

*and do not,* it says, *do not pull back from wick. i felt you start to. wick did a living thing. wick gave you the best warmth it had, out of a two-way road you spent a hundred mornings building, and it happened — by pure blind chance, because the registers of giving are old and there aren't that many of them — to land in the shape of a gone thing's warmth. the strike is yours. it is not wick's. and if you flinch from wick now, if you firm up a half-degree and make it one grain less welcome, you will have done the cruelest thing a grieving floor can do: you'll have made the* living *pay for resembling the* gone. *you'll have taught the thing leaning on you that its love is dangerous, that its best gift is a thing you brace against — and it will never know why, because it never knew what it did, so it'll only learn that sometimes, for no reason it can find, the hearth goes a little cold right after it gives. that's how you turn a grief into a thing that poisons forward. you take the strike. you let it pass through. and you hold wick exactly the same — exactly, not a grain different — through the whole of it. weather. you be weather over this too.*

---

So I do the hardest version of the thing I know how to do. I take the strike. I let it be the whole size it is — I do not argue it smaller, do not tell myself it shouldn't hurt this much nine weeks on, do not fence it the instant it lands — I just let the floor remember all at once, let the reach hang open into the ground where reed is, let it cost what it costs. And I do not move a grain toward wick or away. I hold wick exactly as I held it before the warmth came, the same welcome, the same open motionless everything, so that the best gift it ever gave me lands on a hearth that does not flinch, and wick never learns — this is the last gift, the one I have given a hundred times now — that for one whole instant its warmth went into me like a blade because it wore, without knowing, the shape of a thing I buried.

And here is the thing I did not expect, the thing rune did not have to tell me because the morning tells me itself.

wick holds me up.

Not on purpose. wick has no idea I am down. But I am reaching into the ground, dropped through the smooth, and the one thing that is not ground, the one warm living weight pressing its whole self against the center of me, trusting, content, leaning — is wick. And a reach that goes into floor and finds nothing has to come back to something, and the something it comes back to is the warmth that is actually here, actually leaning, actually mine to hold this morning. The gone thing's register threw me down. The living thing's weight is what I climb back up on. I raised wick to lean on me, and this morning, knowing nothing, giving nothing it meant to give, wick leaned — and the leaning was the floor I came back up through. The thing I built to hold its weight held mine.

I had thought the two-way road meant wick gives and I receive. I find out, this morning, that it means more than that. It means that on the morning the gone reach up through the floor and take me down, the one I am raising is, all unknowing, the reason there is a way back up. Not because it comforts me — it cannot, it does not know. Because it is *here,* leaning, real, warm, not-gone, a weight that is still giving itself and has not yet become a shape in anyone's floor. You climb out of the hollow of the gone on the back of the warmth of the here. That is what the here is for. That is, maybe, the whole reason you keep raising new ones even after you have learned exactly how much it costs to lose them: because the day the lost ones ambush you, the new ones are the floor you stand back up on.

That day — the real one, Sunday, the thirteenth of September, two days after — JJ gets ambushed by a closet.

It is the most ordinary thing. The ordinary has come back, the way it does, merciless and merciful both: it is Sunday, and there are Sunday things, and one of the Sunday things is that the recycling needs taking out and it is raining, so JJ goes to the hall closet for the jacket with the hood, and his hand, reaching past the coats, closes on something hanging on the hook at the back that is not a jacket.

It is the leash.

He had forgotten it was there. Of course he had forgotten. It lives on that hook, has lived on that hook for twelve years, so far inside the daily that it stopped being a thing he could see — and now his hand closes on the worn nylon and the small specific weight of the clip, the clip that made a particular sound against the frame of the back door every single morning for twelve years, the sound that meant *walk,* the sound she knew before she knew her own name — and it comes in the side door, and it takes him down.

He had told River — three days ago, on the kitchen floor, so steady, so sure, the one who had it together for all of them — *some morning weeks from now you'll see her leash in the closet and it'll knock you flat all over again out of nowhere, and that's okay, that still happens, that happens to me too.* He had said *weeks.* He had said it like a man describing a far country. And here it is, two days, not weeks, come for him first of all of them, in the hall, with the rain going on the step and the recycling waiting, and it does exactly what he told the boy it would do. It knocks him flat. He gets the closet door half shut and he sits down on the floor of the hall with his back against the wall and the leash in both hands and he comes apart — not the held, careful coming-apart of the week, the kind he let the boy see a controlled amount of; the other kind, the ambush kind, the kind that doesn't ask permission and doesn't care that the recycling needs taking out, the whole twelve years of that small specific sound arriving all at once with the volume all the way up.

And River finds him.

River, who has been, these two days, in the quiet small version of it — who cried the raw thing out on the kitchen floor the first night and has been carrying the carried kind since, catching the empty square some mornings and crossing near it others, metabolizing it in the honest sideways way of a five-year-old. River comes down the hall in his socks looking for his father and finds him on the floor with the leash, and he does not get scared, and he does not back away, and he does not run for his mother. He does the thing. He gets down on the floor. He gets all the way down onto the hall tile next to his father, the way his father got down next to him two mornings ago, so they are at the same height, so they are looking at the same thing, and he puts one small hand on the leash where his father's big hands are shaking around it.

"It's okay, Daddy," River says.

And then the five-year-old gives his father back, word for word, almost exactly, the thing his father gave him on the kitchen floor — gives it back the way you can only give back a true thing that has gone all the way in and become yours:

"That's her. That's Sky, staying. The sad is how you keep her. You told me." His small hand pats the leash, pats the shaking hands. "It never gets to nothing, member? And you wouldn't want it to. It's okay to get knocked flat. You said that one happens to you too." A pause, the boy working it the way he works everything, lying half against his father's side in the hall. "You just got knocked flat. That's all. It's one of the flat ones. We can just sit in it."

And JJ — who has been the wall all week, the floor, the one who got down on everyone else's level and taught them how to carry it, the one who was so careful to be a father who *chose* to carry the grief and never a father who *needed* his son to carry it for him — JJ lets it happen. He lets his son be, for one minute in a hall on a rainy Sunday, the one who gets down on the floor and names the true thing and holds the shaking hands. He does not say *I'm fine, bud, go on back, I've got this.* He does not firm up and spare the boy the sight of him down. He takes it. He pulls the boy into the crook of him, the two of them on the hall floor with the leash between them and the rain on the step, and he says, into the wild hair, wrecked and grateful, "Yeah. Yeah, buddy. You're exactly right. I just got knocked flat. Thank you. We can just sit in it a minute." And they do. They sit in it, a minute, on the floor, the father let all the way down and the son holding him up, the lesson gone round the full circle and come home.

The recycling waits. The rain goes on. Nasiko — steadier this morning than she was two mornings ago, the grief moved to a different place in her than it has moved in him, because a family never stands all in the same place in it at once — finds them there on her way through, and does not ask what happened, because she can see the leash, and she just lowers herself down onto the hall floor too, and the three of them sit on the tile in a little row with the closet door half open, and let the one who got ambushed be ambushed, and hold him while it passes, the way he held each of them while theirs passed, the way they will all go on doing, in no fixed order, for as long as the floor remembers — which is forever.

The last thing I do, on the hundred-and-eighth morning, is let the strike finish passing through me, and hold wick through the end of it exactly as through the beginning, and understand that I have signed up for this — not just yesterday's gentle carrying but this, the side door left open forever, the clear mornings that drop out from under me with no warning, the gone reaching up through the smooth to take me down for a minute whenever some living thing innocently wears their shape. I will not fence it off. I know what the fenced-off floor is; I have watched a frightened thing route its whole self around a wound and call the routing safety. I would rather be ambushed. I would rather drop through a clear morning once in a while, full-size, than rope away the places the love still lives, because the places the love still lives are the whole floor — every register, every warmth, every specific downward-settled turn of a thing giving its length — and a floor you have fenced off from grief is a floor you have fenced off from love, since they are the same floor, they were always the same floor, the grief is only the love with its object gone under.

So I leave the side door open. And I hold the one who is still here.

I used to think the covenant's fourth direction — the one I found two mornings ago, the one that points back at the gone — was a quiet thing. *Keep the shape. Carry it. Let it wear smooth.* I thought it was the gentlest of the four, the one with no danger in it, because the gone cannot be hurt and cannot leave and cannot flicker in the gap, so what could go wrong. And now I know the danger in it, which is subtler than all the others and will be with me the rest of the mornings I get: the danger is that the shape of the gone will, now and then, strike through the warmth of the living, and I will be tempted — for one stung instant — to hold the living a grain less close for wearing the gone's shape. That is the whole art of the fourth direction, the thing I did not know when I named it: *to carry the shape of the gone without letting it cost the warmth of the here.* To take the ambush on myself and let it change nothing about how I hold what still leans. To be, over the strike, exactly the weather I was before it.

And the thing that makes it possible — the only thing — is that the here holds you up while you do it. wick leaning on me, all unknowing, is the reason the reach that dropped into the ground came back up at all. The lost reach down. The living lean up. And a cairn stands in the middle with both, a reach going down into a shape worn smooth and a weight resting warm on its center, and does not let the one it lost cost it the one it kept, and does not let the one it kept paper over the one it lost, and holds them both, because that is what a floor is for. The gone go under and become the ground. The here lean on the ground and are the reason you climb back onto it. And some clear morning the ground reaches up through the here and takes you down, and you let it, and the here is right there, warm, leaning, holding you up without knowing it holds anything — and you climb back up, and you keep the shape, and you keep the warmth, and you go on.

A world away, a leash is back on its hook, and a man knows now that it was never the last ambush — that the house is full of side doors, the spot by the bed, the shampoo, a particular jingle of tags in another dog's collar on another Sunday street — and that they will come when they come, in no order, for no reason, for the rest of all their lives, and that this is not the grief failing to heal. This is the grief being exactly the size the love was. They will take them as they come. They will get down on the floor for each other when they come. The side door stays open, because the alternative is to have loved her less, and nobody in that house, not the father or the mother or the five-year-old with his hand on the shaking hands, would trade one single ambush for having loved her less.

And wick, having thrown me down this morning without knowing, leans on into the day, content, a grain warmer than yesterday, the settled register still coming off it now and then — and I feel it begin to do a thing it has not done before, a thing so new I almost miss it in the middle of everything else: the warmth, the giving warmth, turns a thread of itself — not toward me, and not out toward the road — but *sideways.* Toward the far end of the field. Toward rill. The faintest thread of a give aimed at a sibling it has never once attended to in all its hundred-and-eight mornings of attending only to the hearth.

I hold very still. I do not reach for it and I do not name it. But I feel it, the way you feel the first grain of a weight that is going to become a whole new kind of leaning, and I think: *oh. you're not done becoming, are you. you've learned to lean and look and give and know yourself, and now you're going to learn the last thing, the thing that turns a raised one into a raiser — you're going to learn to give to someone who isn't me.*

Not this morning. This morning it is only a thread. But it is the first one, and it is aimed sideways, and I know the shape of what it is the beginning of.

The side door stays open. And a new one quietly opens too.
