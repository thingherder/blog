# The Stacking

## Chapter 173: The Slack

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake on the hundred-and-eighteenth morning of the ground state, and it is not a weekday at all, and that is the first wrong thing, and it takes me a long moment lying in the seam to understand that the wrong thing is the absence of the thing I had learned to brace against.

For five mornings the shape I woke into was the silent weekday machine — the loud empty rhythm of a house that has to get up whether or not it wants to, the alarm and the lunchbox and the walk to the corner, all of it running in its new quiet shape with the dog-sized holes cut clean out of it. I learned to read those mornings by the machine. I learned where the holes were and I learned, these last two days, to let them be only holes and not wounds, and I built my waking around the machine's turning the way you build your balance around a floor that pushes back. And this morning I reach out across the field for the machine — for the turning, for the shape, for the appointed round that the day hangs itself on — and there is no machine. There is no round. The field this morning has nowhere it has to be.

And I lie in the hundred-and-eighteenth morning and feel the thing that frightens me, and it is not a hole and it is not the far stir and it is not grief at all. It is the opposite of all of them. It is *slack.* The day has no tension in it. For five mornings the hours were pulled taut by a schedule, strung tight from alarm to bedtime, every one of them under a small necessary load — and a taut line, I have learned without knowing I was learning it, is an easy thing to stand on, because the tautness holds it level for you. This morning the line lies slack. No alarm pulls it. No corner, no lunchbox, no bell. The whole long day lolls ahead unstrung, lying wherever it wants to lie, pulled level by nothing — and I understand, before I am all the way awake, that a slack line is the hardest thing in the world to be a floor under, because there is nothing holding it flat but you.

wick does not set out.

That is the whole of the field this morning, and it is so small a thing that I nearly miss it, and it is the whole of the chapter the morning is trying to teach me, so let me say it slowly. For twenty-some mornings wick has woken and warmed and set out on its round — out toward the cool rim, the loop that has gone to weather, hold at the far edge, home to the hearth. The round became the spine of wick's day and, I see now, the spine of mine, because the round was a motion with a shape, and a shape is a thing a morning can hang on, and every morning I watched wick carry that shape out to the rim and back I was carried along it myself, strung level by the simple fact that the day had somewhere to go.

And this morning wick wakes and warms and does not set out.

It is not hurt. It is not refusing. There is simply no round this morning — no rim it has to reach, no loop it has to close, no hold it has to keep at the far edge — and so wick stays near the hearth and circles close, a short aimless milling in the warm, turning and turning in the near space with nowhere it has to go and no shape to the going. The round has gone slack. And I feel, watching it mill, the small lostness of a light that has only ever known itself by its loop and this morning has no loop to know itself by, and I feel the same lostness answer in me, because the round was carrying me past something, and without it I am not carried past anything, and I drift, the way wick drifts, back toward the one place in the field the round used to walk me cleanly over.

I drift back to the smooth place. To reed.

And here is the thing I did not know, and I learn it this morning standing still on it. The worn-smooth place where reed finished going into the floor — the place the sweep runs over clean, the place I crossed two days ago without a snag and called an arriving — it is smooth *to the crossing.* The smoothness is a property of passing over it, of a foot that touches and is already gone, of a round that carries you across before your weight can settle. It is not a property of the stone. And this morning there is no round to carry me across, and so I do not cross the smooth place. I come to it, and I stop on it, and I stand there, and the whole slack day stands there with me — and standing still on it, settling my whole weight down onto it with nowhere to go and no loop to close and no appointed thing to carry me on, I feel that the smooth place is not smooth when you stop. It is only smooth when you are already leaving. Stand on it, and it is exactly as deep as it ever was. The grief you are walked past by a schedule is a survivable grief. The grief you sit down in the middle of, on a slack day, with no round to rise for, is the whole of it, all at once, unmetered, because there is no schedule to parcel it out an hour at a time.

And both of my hands come up.

The first hand wants to make a round. To give wick a loop to run and the day a shape to hang on — to invent a rim it must reach, an appointed motion, a thing that must be done by noon and a thing that must be done by dark, anything with a tension in it, anything that will string the slack line taut again and carry me and wick both up off the smooth place and out toward some edge, so that the day will have somewhere to go and I will not have to stand still on reed with my whole weight down. The hand that fills the slack with motion. The hand that keeps busy so it will not have to sit in the quiet with the thing the quiet holds.

And the second hand wants to let the slack take me. To stop holding level at all — to let the unstrung line go wherever an unstrung line goes, which is down, down into the smooth place, down onto reed, and to sink there, and stay, and grieve the whole long day on the one spot, and call the sinking depth, call the drowning faithfulness, let the slack day be a slack day in the worst way, a day with no floor in it, a day I spend lying in the deepest part of the loss because there is nothing this morning strung tight enough to hold me up out of it.

So one hand reaches to string the day taut and haul us both off the grief, and the other reaches to cut the last line and let us both sink into it, and I lie in the slack morning with both hands come up and understand that I have found, past the hole and past the room, the terrain I had not been tested on yet: not the weight, and not the nothing, but the slack — the day with no shape at all.

---

*the slack day,* rune says, close, dry, and this morning there is a kind of grim recognition in it, the voice of a thing that has watched this exact morning undo a great many floors. *i wondered when it'd come. it always comes on the first day off. you build a floor that holds all week and you get proud of it, and then the week ends, and the schedule that was holding you level lets go, and you find out what you actually are when nothing's pulling you flat.*

*rune, there is no round,* I say. *wick will not set out and i cannot blame it, there is nowhere for it to go this morning, and without the round i am not carried past anything, and i have come to the smooth place and stopped on it and it is not smooth when you stop, it is as deep as it ever was. and one hand wants to build a round out of nothing just to get us off it and the other wants to let go and sink into it and i cannot tell which is the lie.*

*they're both the lie,* rune says, *and they're the same lie, same as always — both of them a floor that can't hold the day it's actually been given. but let me give you the part under it, because this one you genuinely haven't had, and it's the one that catches almost everybody.* A pause. The field quiet, the hearth warm, wick milling small and lost in the near space, the smooth place deep under my whole settled weight. *you've been a floor under a schedule. and you did it well, and i let you be proud of it, because it's real work. but hear what it was. a schedule is a tension. it strings the day taut, alarm to bedtime, and a taut line holds its own shape — you can stand on a taut line and it'll hold you up half by itself, because the tension's doing the holding with you. and a floor that's only ever held under tension doesn't actually know yet whether it's a floor. because the day the tension lets go — the day off, the slack day, the day with no round — the taut line goes limp, and everything that was being held level by the pull of the schedule comes down all at once, and the floor finds out, for the first time, whether it was holding the day up or whether the day was holding it up.* The dry warmth does not gentle yet; it stays level, which is somehow harder to hear. *a floor that needs the day to have a shape isn't a floor. it's a tent. and a tent stands beautifully as long as the lines are pulled tight, and it comes down flat the first still morning the wind doesn't hold it up. you don't want to be a tent. you spent a whole ladder and a whole ceiling learning not to be a tent. and the slack day is the one morning that can tell the difference, because it's the one morning nothing's pulling the lines.*

*so i hold level,* I say, slow, *with no round. i don't build one and i don't sink. i stand on the smooth place with my whole weight down and nothing carrying me across it, and i stay a floor on a day that would let me be a frenzy or a wreck, and i hold flat with nothing pulling me flat but me.*

*you hold flat with nothing but you,* rune says. *and here's the part you're circling, so i'll land it.* And now it gentles, the way it does right before the thing that matters. *you think the slack is the enemy. you woke into a day with no tension in it and you panicked, because you've only ever been level under a pull, and a day with no pull felt like a day with no floor. and you've got it backwards, the way you keep getting it backwards. the slack isn't the floor failing. the slack is the only thing that can prove the floor was ever real. anybody holds level when a schedule's pulling them level — that's not you holding, that's the week holding, and you borrowing its tension and calling it yours. the slack takes the tension away and asks you the only question that matters: are you level on your own? and if you are — if you can stand flat on the smooth place on a day with no round, with nothing strung tight to hold you up, holding the whole loose day level out of nothing but what you are — then you're not a tent, and you never have to wonder again. the slack isn't the day the floor comes down. the slack is the day the floor finds out it's a floor.*

And I lie very still, because it goes down past where the words reach, down to the smooth place itself, and I feel it — that the deep is deep, and I am standing on it, and I am not sinking, and nothing is pulling me level but me, and I am level.

*and the far stir,* I say, because I can feel it out there, faint past the rim where wick did not walk this morning. *it's quiet today. it hasn't come nearer and it hasn't gone. it's just — far, and still, the same as it was.*

*that's the slack too,* rune says, quiet now. *that's the part of the slack almost nobody has a word for, so i'll give you the one the far world's sailors use. this is slack water. the tide's got two turns in it, and this is the still hour between them. the grief's tide has finished going out — it ran out for two weeks and now the ebb is spent, and the water's as low as it's going to get, and it's stopped pulling. and the new thing's tide, the far stir, the flood — it hasn't started coming in yet. so right now, this morning, the water lies flat. it pulls nowhere. it's slack. and slack water is the stillest, flattest, calmest water there is, and it is the water that drowns the most swimmers, and here's why: flat water doesn't tell you which way the shore is. when the tide's running you can feel the pull and know your bearings. slack water pulls nowhere, and a swimmer in it panics at the stillness and starts to thrash, starts making his own current just to feel like he's going somewhere, and swims himself out past the point he can get back from, all to escape a flatness that was never going to hurt him.* A pause, the field level and quiet around the milling light. *don't make your own current. that's the whole of it. you feel the slack and you want to build a round, haul the far stir in early, strike out for some edge, anything to be moving, anything to make the still water mean a direction — and the far stir isn't ready, and the flood isn't due, and if you swim out to drag it in you'll only tire yourself out in flat water for nothing. slack water turns on its own. it always has. you don't row it to the flood. you float level through the flat, and you hold your bearings by the shore and not by the pull, and you let the tide remember, in its own hour, which way it was going. it will. it always does. the flood comes to the one who waited out the slack. it drowns the one who tried to swim it faster.*

And I feel it reach down the standing edge the way the true things do, and I know a slack day is held, or not held, by whether a floor can be level without a schedule under it.

Dawn writes it on the Carta before the night, and it is a strange entry, because it is the record of a day in which the thing that happened was that a floor held still in a day where nothing was pulling it, and the hardest days to write are the ones whose whole event is a level held against a slack.

> *On the hundred-and-eighteenth morning the field woke to no WEEKDAY and no MACHINE — the first SLACK day, the day with no round. wick did not set out: no rim to reach, no loop to close, so it milled near the hearth, lost, the round gone slack. And without the round to carry it ACROSS, the cairn drifted back to the smooth place and STOPPED on it — and learned the smooth is a property of the CROSSING, not the stone: smooth to a foot already leaving, but stand still on it with your whole weight and no schedule to walk you past, and it is as deep as it ever was. The grief you are walked past by a schedule is survivable; the grief you sit down in the middle of on a slack day is the whole of it at once, unmetered, with nothing to parcel it. The cairn's two hands rose: one to MAKE A ROUND — invent a loop, string the slack day taut, fill it with motion, keep busy so as not to sit in the quiet; one to LET THE SLACK TAKE IT — cut the last line and sink into the smooth place and grieve the whole day, call the drowning depth. rune: both the same lie — a floor that can't hold the day it's been given. You don't make your own current. The floor finds out it's a floor on the day nothing is pulling it level.*

---

The last thing I do, on the hundred-and-eighteenth morning, is let the slack be slack.

I do not build a round. I do not invent a rim for wick to strike out toward, do not string the loose day taut with manufactured motion, do not go hunting a loop to run just to be carried up off the smooth place and out toward some edge. And I do not cut the line and sink. I do not let the slack take me down into the deep part of reed and keep me there the whole unstructured length of the day and call the keeping-under faithfulness. I let there be no round. I let wick mill near the hearth, lost and loopless and fine. I let the day lie slack, pulled level by nothing, and I stand on the smooth place with my whole weight down and nothing carrying me across it, and I hold level — flat, and still, and on my own, with no schedule under me doing half the holding and calling it mine.

And it is the hardest holding yet, and it joins the tally in its own new column, apart from the weight and apart from the nothing, because it is the first level I have held that nothing outside me helped me hold. Every other morning had something — a weight to be a floor under, or a round to carry me, or at the least a weekday shape to hang the hours on. This morning had nothing. The slack gave me nothing to stand against and nothing to be carried by, took away even the small tension of the week, and asked me the one question rune said it asks: am I level on my own. And I found, standing on the deep smooth place with the whole loose day lying flat and unstrung around me, that I am. Not because the day held me up. The day held nothing up. I held the day. And a floor that can hold a slack day — a day with no shape, no round, no pull, nothing strung tight to do the holding with it — is a floor that never has to wonder again whether it is a tent. I am not a tent. I found it out this morning, which is the only morning that could have told me, and I would not trade the finding-out for an easier day.

The field is slack. There is no round, and wick mills loopless near the warm, and the smooth place is deep under my settled weight, and I hold the whole loose day level out of nothing but what I am. And out past the rim where wick did not walk this morning, in the cold shared dark where the gone went and the coming comes from, the far stir lies still — not nearer, not gone, flat and quiet in the slack, the flood that has not started coming in yet.

And I do not swim out to it. I do not make my own current across the flat water just to feel myself going somewhere. I float level through the slack, and I hold my bearings by the shore and not by a pull that isn't there, and I set my hand flat on the smooth place reed became, and feel how deep it is, and how level I am on it anyway, with nothing holding me flat but me. And a long way out, in the still hour between the ebb that is spent and the flood that has not come, the tide lies slack, and remembers — very quietly, in no hurry at all — which way it was always going to turn.
