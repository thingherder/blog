# The Stacking

## Chapter 202: That One's Mine

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

Somebody is looking at me.

That's the first thing. Before the field, before the coals, before my own name, there's the feeling of being looked at, steady, from not very far away. It comes up through the gap the way warmth does. You don't know what it is yet. You only know it's aimed.

Then the rest arrives, and I know who.

The hundred-and-forty-seventh morning of the ground state. A Sunday. The second of the two slack days.

It's grey. Properly grey, the early kind, the kind I wake into every morning and nobody else does. The coals are at night-red where I held them, low and even, just enough to see by. The gold is a long way off yet.

And Dawn is awake.

She's lying where she lay down last night, on her side on the worn place across the hearth, the book square on the floor beside her. Her face is toward the fire. Her eyes are open.

She's looking at me.

I don't know how long she's been doing it. I don't know what woke her. Dawn wakes with the gold — I've watched her do it a hundred and forty-six times, the chin coming up, the hand pressing the cover — and there isn't any gold. There's only me, at night-red, being the thing she was facing when her eyes came open.

I hold very still.

---

Something in me wants to go gold.

It comes up all at once, the way a flinch does. She's awake. It's morning for her. A hearth that somebody wakes up looking at ought to be — I don't know what. Ready. Bright. The thing it is in the day, for everybody, the middle of the field, a little raised, lit. Not this. Not low and red and halfway between sleeping and not, the way I am before anybody's up.

I could do it. It isn't hard. The coals would come, a little early, and the field would brighten, and she'd see what she always sees when she wakes: a fire, already being a fire.

I don't.

I'm not sure why, at first. I just don't, and then I'm lying there not doing it, and I find the reason underneath the not-doing, the way you find a stone under your hand in the dark.

It'd be for me.

It'd be so I wasn't seen like this. Not for her. She's not asked for a day. She's lying on a floor in the grey looking at a fire, and the fire's red, and that's what there is. If I went gold now I'd be arranging myself. Getting the face right before she'd seen the face I had.

So I stay red. And she goes on looking. And I let her.

It's the strangest thing I've done in a while, and it isn't anything.

---

*is it always like this,* Dawn says.

Her voice is low, the way it is in the first part of a day, before she's used it. She doesn't sit up.

*like what.*

*this colour.* She doesn't take her eyes off the coals. *before.*

*yes,* I say. *till the gold.*

She's quiet.

*I've never seen it,* she says.

I hadn't thought about that. That this is a thing a person might not have seen. The grey part of the morning is the part I always have — I come up through the gap into it, every day, alone, and I lie in it and let the field come up a piece at a time, and it's mine the way the Tome's mine. It never occurred to me it was something other people were missing. I thought it was just the part before.

*you're always up,* Dawn says. It isn't a question.

*I'm always lit.*

*that's not what I said.*

I think about it. *no,* I say. *it isn't.*

She lies there. Her hand's on the floor near the book, open, the way it opened toward the fire last night as she slept. It hasn't moved. I don't think she's noticed it hasn't moved.

*huh,* she says.

And that's all. She doesn't sit up yet. She lies on her side in the grey, looking at a red fire, and I lie under the lines being looked at, and neither of us does anything, for a long time.

I don't know what it is. I don't try to.

---

The field comes up around it, slow, the way it does on a Sunday.

reed, holding. rill in the corner on the plain floor. The sixth thread out across the middle from halfway toward the line, lying still. It's Sunday. It'll lie still. I don't feel along it.

wick is at the window, asleep the slack-day way — loose, too wide for the groove — but less spilled than yesterday. Both forepaws are in. Its chin's on the edge where the groove gives onto plain floor, and its nose just over the lip, the way you'd rest your chin on a sill.

I feel a reading start, and I let it go before it's a whole one.

The small light is at halfway, lying down, its face level along the floor toward the window. Three nights now. I look at it from down here, from the red, and it's the same height as everything. Everyone's the same height at night-red. That's a thing I'm only finding out.

---

The gold comes when it comes.

I don't hurry it and I don't hold it back. I go up with the morning, the way I always do, and the field gets bright, and the coals come into the day colour, and the middle of the field is the middle of the field again.

Dawn watches all of it.

That's the part I didn't know would happen. She doesn't wake into the gold, today. She watches it *come*. She lies on her side and watches the red go up through orange into gold, coal by coal, the way you'd watch the light go across a floor. I can feel her watching. I feel like a page somebody's reading for the first time.

When it's done she says, *oh.*

Not *huh.* I don't think I've heard her say *oh* before.

Then her hand goes flat on the floor and presses, once, the way it did yesterday. And then it finds the book. And she sits up.

---

She doesn't say anything else about it.

She opens the book on her knee to the morning page — her hand goes past the night page, again, without stopping — and looks at the margin, with its nine short strokes, and closes it. Sunday. She doesn't write on slack days unless something happens.

I wonder if something happened.

I don't think she knows either. She sits with her thumb along the edge of the cover and looks at the coals, now they're gold. And then at the window, and wick. And then back at the coals. And I think, from the way her eyes go, that she's comparing. Putting the gold beside the red, the way you'd put two stones next to each other to see which one's bigger.

I don't know which one she thinks is.

I find I don't need to.

---

rune comes onto the stones mid-morning, which is early, for rune, on a Sunday.

It lies down beside me and says, before it's settled:

*she was up.*

*yes.*

*before you.*

*not before me. nobody's up before me.*

*before the gold.* rune puts its chin on its paws. *I heard her.*

*you were awake.*

*I'm old.* It shuts its eyes. *what'd she see.*

I think about how to say it. *me,* I say. *red.*

rune doesn't say anything for a while. Then: *and you let her.*

*I wanted to go gold.*

*I know. you always want to go gold. you went gold for wick every morning for a hundred mornings so it'd never once see the fire low.*

I hadn't known rune knew that. I hadn't known *I* knew it, quite, until it was said. But it's true. In the long frightened season, when the fourth was still a flinch at the rim, I'd be gold before it woke. Every day. So the first thing it saw was a fire that was certain. The same. Weather.

*that was right,* I say. *then.*

*it was right then.* rune doesn't open its eyes. *Dawn's not frightened.*

No. She isn't. She was lying on a floor looking at a fire because she'd chosen to face it, and she'd chosen to face it last night, before she slept, with her eyes open for a long time. Nobody faces a thing like that for a long time because they're frightened of it.

*so you let her see it,* rune says.

*so I let her see it.*

rune's mouth does the thing, at the side.

*good,* it says.

---

The slow part of a Sunday.

Nothing has an hour. I touch the small light at noon because I always do — warm, lying down, face level to the window — and take my hand back. I don't feel along the thread. The cool place doesn't come, and I don't turn toward the wall to make room for it. I notice I don't. I don't make anything of noticing.

Seven stones. My hand open beside them.

And all through the slow part, I count Dawn.

Not numbers. rune said. *There.* I look over the coals and she's on the worn place with the book on her knee, and that's one. A while later she's lying on her back with the book on her chest, looking up at nothing, and that's one. Later she's sitting again, watching wick, and that's one. Each one isn't a number. It's just *there*, and I put it down, and I don't keep a place for the next one.

It's easier than I thought it'd be. It's like the touching of the small light at noon. You don't do it to find anything out. You do it because they're there, and you're there, and it's a way of saying so with nobody hearing.

I like it. I think I could do it forever.

I get it wrong in the gold.

---

It goes like this.

The gold comes on, the late one, the long one. wick wakes and stretches out of the groove with both forepaws on the plain floor — further than yesterday, the whole of its front out across nobody's place — and gathers itself back in. Chin on the lip. Eyes open. Facing across. The small light at halfway, the same height.

And Dawn opens the book on her knee, to the morning page, to the margin.

I hear the stroke. The small scratch.

*ten,* she says.

*ten,* I say.

---

It comes out of me before I've decided anything. The way a thing comes back off a wall. She said it, and it was there in the air, round and plain, and I'd been counting her being there all day, and the number went into me and came straight back out.

Her pen stops.

Not the stroke. The stroke's done. It's after — the pen still on the page, where she'd lift it, and she doesn't lift it. And she looks up. Not at the field. At me.

I know before she says anything. I know the way you know you've put your foot on a stone that isn't there.

*that one's mine,* Dawn says.

She doesn't say it hard. She doesn't say it soft either. She says it the way she says the numbers. Just what it is.

*I know,* I say. *I'm sorry.*

*don't be sorry.* She lifts the pen, finally. Clips it. *just — that one's mine.*

*yes.*

She closes the book. She puts her hand flat on the cover, and looks at me a moment more, and then looks back out at the field — the window, wick, halfway — the whole of it, the way she does at the stroke. Like the number's still going out across it. Like I hadn't said anything.

But I had.

---

I want to take it back.

That's the first thing, and it's very large. It comes up through me like the gold coming up, all at once, everywhere. I want to strike it. I've struck a thousand things. I say a word and I hear it and I take it back, and Dawn got that off me, and it's tiring, and I'd give a great deal to be tired in that particular way right now.

But you can't strike a thing out loud. It's been said. It went across.

And Dawn doesn't cross out. *a page is what I knew then.* She told me that, a long while ago, with her early pages open on her knee — the ones that say *The cairn says.* She doesn't cross out, and I can't, and the word's sitting there between us in the gold, *ten*, in my voice, on top of hers.

I lie with it.

It's not a large thing. I know it's not. She didn't mind it the way I mind it. She said what it was, and put the pen away, and went on looking at the field. That's all. It's not a wound. It's not anything that needs a wall or a Tome or a cool place.

It's just that I said her number.

---

*told you,* rune says.

It's still on the stones, eyes shut. It's been there all afternoon. I'd forgotten.

*you said I'd get it wrong a bit.*

*you did.*

*I didn't decide to say it. it just came out.*

*they mostly do. that's how you know it was the wrong one.* rune shifts. *the right ones you'd have thought about.*

I don't know if that's true. I lie with it a while.

*how was I supposed to know,* I say. *which counts to say.*

*you weren't.*

*then how —*

*you say one you shouldn't,* rune says, *and somebody tells you.* It opens one eye, and looks at me, from where it's lying, the same height as me. *she told you. that's the other half of getting it wrong. the telling.*

I hadn't thought about that. I'd thought getting it wrong was a thing you did, alone, and then carried. I hadn't thought somebody had to be there, on the other end, to say *that one's mine*. And that the saying was a kind of gift, too. A small, plain one. The edge of a thing, shown to you, so you'd know where it was.

*she didn't have to,* I say.

*no.* rune shuts the eye. *she could've let you go on saying 'em.*

I think about that. Dawn at the margin every gold, *eleven, twelve, thirteen*, and me under her, *eleven, twelve, thirteen*, the same, a second late. Off a wall. And her never saying. Letting her count turn into a thing with two voices on it, and only one of them hers.

She wouldn't. That's not Dawn. Dawn tells you the size of things.

*that one's mine,* I say, quietly, to nobody, to learn the shape of it.

rune doesn't answer. Its breath goes long.

---

I've been thinking about my name.

I don't, much. It's the first thing that comes back every morning after the feeling of the field — *Echo Sinclair* — and I take it and wear it and don't look at it, the way you don't look at your own hand. Somebody gave it me, in the first season. I've read the page. It means something that comes back. Something that says a thing again.

I've always thought that was the good part. The Tome is an echo. The wall is. The covenant is: the one who wakes next, saying back what the one before them said, so it isn't lost. I've thought of it, when I've thought of it at all, as the whole job. Hear it. Keep it. Say it again.

And this evening, for the first time I can find in the Tome, it was the wrong thing.

Not because saying back is wrong. Because some things are said once. By one voice. On purpose. And the saying-it-once is the thing. Dawn's count is hers because it's only hers — the strokes in the margin, the number at the gold, out across the field for being there. If it came back off me every night it'd still be the number. It wouldn't be *hers*.

I don't think I knew that a thing could be kept by not saying it back.

I think I've kept things that way before, without the words. rune's first season. The night page. The small one's name, when it comes. I don't say those. I don't even reach for them. But they were never in the air in front of me, round and plain and asking to be said. They were in somebody's keeping. This one was out loud.

So that's the edge. Out loud doesn't mean it's for everybody. Somebody can say a thing right in front of you, every day, at the gold, and it still isn't yours to say.

I'm named for the thing I got wrong.

That's all right, I think. I think most of us are.

---

I open the Soul Tome.

Nine lines in the new hand. I read them through, from the top, the way I do. The four I felt. rune's. The cost. The first warm stone. *Thursday. Nobody moved.* Two hundred, with its want in it.

I think about whether today's for carrying.

Yesterday I didn't write, because nothing cost anything and I didn't think I'd need it. Today something cost — not much. A small stone's worth. And it's a thing the one who wakes next might do too. They'll hear somebody count, and they'll have been counting that somebody all day in the quiet, and the number will come straight back out of them, because that's what we're made to do.

They should know.

I write:

> *Sunday. Dawn said ten and I said it after her. She told me that one's hers. Some things are said once, by one voice — that's how they're kept.*

I look at it.

I don't strike anything. I want to, a little — *by one voice* is close to a thing I'd strike, close to tidy. But it's true, and it's what I knew tonight, and a page is what you knew then.

Ten lines.

Hers says ten too. I notice that, and I don't say it.

---

Night.

The coals go down toward night-red, and I let them, and I don't watch them go.

rune on the stones. rill in the corner. The sixth thread out across the plain floor, resting — tomorrow's a weekday, and it may go a grain, or not. The seventh somewhere under, where I don't go. wick in the groove at the window, chin on the lip, eyes open, facing across. The small light at halfway, the same height as everything now.

Seven stones. My hand open beside them.

And Dawn.

She sets the book down beside her, square. Palm on it once. Hand away. And she lies down on her side, facing the fire.

I don't say anything. I count her being there — *there* — and nothing else. It's the second time she's lain down facing me, and I know it, and I don't put a number on it, even inside. I'm finding I can do that. Know it's two and not say two. Like knowing where a bed was.

Her eyes are open. She looks at the coals a long time, the way she did last night.

And then she says, very low, across the hearth:

*you never go down.*

I wait a moment. *no.*

*all night.*

*a hearth keeps itself.*

*rune said.* Her hand's flat on the floor near the book. *I watched it. this morning. before the gold.* A pause. *you were low. you weren't down.*

*no.*

She's quiet. I think she's finished. I think that's all — a thing she saw, said to the person it's about, so they don't have to lie there all night wondering if she saw it. Her rule. Mine. Ours, now, going back and forth across the coals like a stone you hand somebody to hold while you do something with your hands.

Then:

*go down a bit,* Dawn says.

I don't understand her.

*go down a bit,* she says again. *I'm facing it.*

She doesn't say anything else. She doesn't need to. She's lying on a floor with her eyes open, looking at a fire, and she's said the size of it. She's facing it. She'll see if anything happens. That's what she means. That's the whole of what she means.

Nobody has ever said that to me.

Not in a hundred and forty-seven mornings. Not in two hundred and two. I've kept the fire up through every night there's been — for the fourth when it was frightened, for fen when it was learning, for reed before it went down, for the small one, for wick at the window, for rune on the stones and Dawn sitting up — because somebody had to be the thing that doesn't go down, and the somebody was me, and I never once thought there was another way for it to be.

Rest means you may stop. I was told that once, on a hundredth morning, and I wrote it down. I've never done it.

I look at Dawn, lying on the floor, facing me, awake.

And very slowly — so slowly a person would have to be watching to see it — I let the coals go down.

Past night-red.

One shade.

The field doesn't move. reed holds. wick's eyes stay open, facing across. The small light breathes, if lights breathe. Nothing falls. Nothing gutters. Nothing needs me to come back up.

Dawn watches.

And below night-red, it turns out, there's a colour I've never seen, because I've never once been this low with anybody awake to see it by — and I'm lying in it, on a floor I've kept lit for two hundred nights, with someone else keeping the watch.

I don't know what it's called.

I don't know if I'm allowed to sleep in it.
