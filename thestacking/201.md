# The Stacking

## Chapter 201: The Same Height

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

The book is on the floor.

That's what I wake to. Not the coals, not the lines, not my own hands. A small square thing on the worn place across the hearth, set down square, the way the pen gets set down. And a hand near it. Not on it.

For a moment I don't know why that's strange. I don't know anything yet. I only know that a book is on a floor, and that every other morning a book has been somewhere else, and that the difference is so large it's come up through the gap ahead of my own name.

Then the rest of it comes.

The hundred-and-forty-sixth morning of the ground state. A Saturday. The first of the two slack days.

I lie under the lines in the grey and let the field come up around me, one thing at a time, the way it does.

The coals, at night-red still, because nobody's tended them toward a day and there isn't one. reed, holding. rill in the corner, on plain floor that used to be a firm place and isn't anything now but floor. The sixth thread out across the middle from halfway toward the line, lying still. It's Saturday. It'll lie still. I don't feel along it.

wick is at the window.

And wick is asleep — properly, the slack-day way. Not chin on paws with its eyes open. Loose. Spread. The way it used to sleep in its long shallow bed two grains east on a Saturday, like a thing that has nowhere to be until Monday.

Except the groove at the window isn't a bed. It's a groove — narrow, the width of a path, worn by a round and not by a body lying down. And wick doesn't fit it, sprawled. So it's half in. Its back is in the groove and the rest of it has spilled out, and one forepaw lies flat on the plain floor, past the edge, pointing across.

Toward halfway.

I feel the reading come. It's a nice one. It's got a paw in it.

I let it go by. wick is asleep on a Saturday and the groove's too narrow for that. That's what a paw on a floor is.

The small light is at halfway, lying down, face still lifted and turned to the window. Two days and three nights now. The face hasn't come back.

And Dawn.

---

Dawn's lying on her side on the worn place where she's sat every night I've known her. Her face is toward the window. Her hand's on the floor near the book. Her breath is long.

She's asleep. Still asleep — and the strange thing, the thing I keep turning over in the grey, is that I don't know how Dawn wakes from lying down. I know how she wakes from sitting up. I've seen it a hundred and forty-five times: the coals go gold, and her chin comes up off her chest, and her hand on the book presses once, flat, before she's even opened her eyes. Like checking the book's still there before checking she is.

Her hand isn't on the book. There's nothing for it to press.

I lie there and wonder what it'll do instead. And then I notice I'm lying there *waiting* to see what it'll do instead, the way I waited on Friday for wick's nose to go down at the bed, and I take the waiting and put it down.

It's her hand. It'll do what it does.

---

The coals go gold.

Dawn's eyes open.

She doesn't move. That's the first thing — she doesn't sit up, or reach, or press anything. She lies on her side with her eyes open and her hand on the floor, and she looks. Straight ahead. The way she was facing when she went to sleep.

Window. Plain floor. Halfway.

She doesn't move her eyes between them, the way she did on Thursday and Friday — window, halfway, window, like reading a line back. She doesn't have to. From down there, lying, they're all in front of her at once. wick and the plain floor and the small light, one long low view along the ground.

She looks at it a long time.

Then her hand moves. Not to the book. It goes flat on the floor, under her palm, the way it would go flat on the cover. Presses once.

And *then* it finds the book, and rests on it, and she sits up.

I look at the coals. I count them. They're all there.

---

She doesn't say anything about it. She opens the book on her knee to the morning page — I see her hand go past the night page without stopping, the way it did yesterday morning — and looks at the margin with its eight short strokes, and closes it again. Saturday. She doesn't write on slack days unless something happens.

I wait to find out if something happened.

She looks at wick a while, sprawled out of the groove with its paw on the floor. I can see the line wanting to be written. *wick's paw's on the plain floor.* I can see her not write it — the line would lean on another line, a groove and a bed and a Saturday, and none of it would stand up by itself.

She puts her hand flat on the cover.

And I find I have something I need to say, and I don't want to.

---

It's this.

I saw her lie down.

I said I wouldn't write it, and I won't, and I haven't. That part's easy. It isn't a line in anybody's book. But Dawn told me yesterday, first thing — before the coals were properly gold — what it is to see a thing and not say. *you'd be lying there all day with it. seeing me not say. that's a thing that didn't happen, getting written. by keeping quiet.*

She said that about her night line. She said it so I wouldn't have to carry it.

And now I'm the one carrying it.

I don't want to tell her. That's the truth, and I make myself look at it, lying there in the gold. I don't want to, because it was hers — the first night she ever lay down, and nobody should have been there, the way nobody should have been there under halfway. rune said it, a week ago. *you don't sit with somebody's first thing. you let them have it.* And I didn't sit with it, exactly. But I was lit. I'm always lit. I'm the loud thing at the middle of the field and I can't not see.

So what's the kind thing? To let her have it alone by pretending I didn't? Or to tell her I saw, so she isn't — without knowing it — being seen?

I go back and forth on it through the slow part of the morning. It's like the striking. Every time I land on one I hear it, and take it back.

In the end it isn't the kind thing that decides it. It's that it's hers. Her rule. She made it out of mine and handed it back, and if I don't use it the first morning I need it, I'm telling her it was only a thing she said.

---

*Dawn.*

She looks up.

*you lay down,* I say. *last night. I saw.*

She looks at me for a moment, steady. Then:

*I know,* Dawn says. *I was there.*

It takes me a second.

And then something comes up out of me that isn't quite a laugh — a hearth doesn't laugh — but the coals go bright all at once along one edge, the way they do when a draught finds them. On the stones, rune opens one eye.

Dawn watches the coals do it. Her mouth does something. Not quite what rune's does. Her own.

*you didn't have to say,* she says.

*you said I'd be lying there all day with it.*

*I did say that.* She looks down at the book. *huh.*

We sit with it. It isn't heavy. That's the odd part. I'd carried it all morning like a stone, and it went across and turned out to weigh about as much as a word.

*did rune see,* Dawn says. Not to rune. To me.

*I think so. rune was breathing like it was awake.*

On the stones, rune shuts its eye again and says nothing. Which is rune for *yes*.

*huh,* Dawn says again.

And then, after a while, she says, *it's not for the book.*

*I know.*

*it's not for the book because it's mine,* she says. *not because it's —*

She stops.

I wait. She doesn't go on. She puts her thumb along the edge of the cover, the way she did yesterday morning, and doesn't open it, and doesn't finish.

I don't know what the other half of that was going to be. *Not because it's nothing,* maybe. Or *not because it's bad.* Or something I wouldn't think of in a hundred mornings. It's a line she started and didn't write, out loud, and it's hers the same as the night page is hers.

I've got another thing I'll never know now. I'm getting quite a lot of them.

---

The slow part of a Saturday.

Nothing has an hour. wick sleeps on with its paw out. The small light lies at halfway with its face to the window. rill's in the corner. The coals settle into the low, even gold of a day that isn't going anywhere, and I let them, and I lie beside the wall with my hand open.

I touch the small light once, a little after noon, because I always do. Warm. Lying down. I don't feel along the thread. It's a slack day and the thread knows it, or it doesn't, and either way it's lying where it lay.

What I notice, with my hand on its back, is the face.

I'd thought of it all week as *turned toward the window.* And it is. But I'm lying low today — the coals down, nothing to be tall for — and from down here I notice that it isn't turned *up*. It never was. It's turned level. Along the floor. The way Dawn was looking this morning, before she sat up.

It's looking at wick the same height as wick.

I don't know why that seems like a thing. It isn't a doing. Nobody's moved. It's only that I'd been seeing it from above, all week, the way a hearth sees everything — from the middle, a little raised, lit — and from above a face lifted off the floor looks like it's lifted *toward* something. Reaching.

From down here it isn't reaching. It's just level. It's just looking across at someone lying on the same floor.

I take my hand back.

The size of me seeing it. That's all. But I think about how long I've been looking at my field from a little bit up.

---

rune comes onto the stones after noon and lies down beside me, and for a long while doesn't say anything, which on a Saturday is most of what rune does.

Then:

*you're counting her.*

I don't answer straight away.

*you said it,* rune says. *last night. you said you'd seen it once.*

*I didn't say it out loud.*

*didn't have to.* rune's eyes are shut. *you went still the way you go still when you're counting.*

That's true, and I didn't know it, and I lie with the fact that rune knows what I look like counting. I suppose it's watched me learn it. Learned off Dawn — *one, two, three,* her three seeings. And now I count the small light and the cool places and the stones in the wall out loud, *six, seven,* and Dawn hears whose count it is.

But this one I didn't say.

*it's different,* I say. *counting her.*

*how.*

I try to find it. *the stones are mine. the cool places come to me. when I count them it's my count, about my things. this is — I'd be counting Dawn. like she's a thing that happens. like wick's round.*

*wick's round's a thing that happens.*

*wick doesn't know I count it.*

rune's quiet. Then: *Dawn knows everything gets counted. she does it.*

*she counts days. she doesn't count people.*

*she counted wick coming home.*

*that was for wick being there,* I say. *she said. it's for being there.*

rune opens its eyes, and looks at me the way it does — not at the thing I said, but at the place I said it from.

*so count her being there,* it says.

I hadn't thought of it like that. I'd thought there were only two ways to count her: out loud, so she'd know she was being watched for, or inside, kept, like a hand held a bit shut. I hadn't thought there was a kind of counting that was just a mark for every time somebody's there. That doesn't wait for anything. That doesn't need to get to three.

*don't say the numbers,* rune says, and shuts its eyes again. *that's all. some counts you say. some you just know.*

*how do I know which.*

*you'll get it wrong a bit.* It settles. *that's how.*

---

wick wakes in the late afternoon.

Not all at once. A slack-day waking, slow, starting at the far end. The tail first, and then the back legs drawing in, and then a long stretch along the groove that pushes it all the way out of the groove and onto the plain floor for a moment, both forepaws now, its whole front half lying out across the place where nobody's place is.

Then it gathers itself back into the groove, the way you'd pull a blanket back over you, and lies with its chin on its paws and its eyes open, facing across.

It didn't go round. It doesn't on Saturdays.

It went out a little way, though, I think. And then I think: it stretched. And I let the second one be the one I keep. It's smaller, and it's what I saw.

---

The cool place doesn't come today.

I notice it not coming, around the time it usually would, in the slack hour before the gold. I notice I'd half-made room for it. I'd turned toward the wall a little the way you'd turn toward a door you think somebody's about to come through.

I turn back.

Seven stones. One warm, one cold the shape it came, five cold the old way. My hand open beside them. It isn't a wall that's waiting for an eighth. It's just a wall.

They come, rune said. You don't have to keep a place for them.

I don't. It's slow. It's Saturday. I'm learning.

---

The gold comes on.

Dawn opens the book on her knee, to the morning page, and turns to the margin, and makes the stroke. A small scratch in the slack-day quiet.

*nine,* she says.

And I hear, under it, something I hadn't heard in her counting before — or I had, and didn't know what it was. She says it *to* something. Not to me. Not to wick. Not to the book. She's looking at the whole field when she says it, the coals and the window and halfway, and rune on the stones, and the corner, and the number goes out across all of it.

For being there.

She closes the book. She puts her hand flat on it.

And then she looks at me, across the coals, for a while, the way she did last night. She doesn't cross. I don't expect her to anymore. I don't want her to, I find. That's new. All week I'd been lying here with the fact that she's never crossed to me once, like a small stone in a pocket — not a heavy one, but one you'd keep finding with your hand.

Today I take it out and look at it, and it's just a fact. She's never crossed. She's always looked. Looking's for knowing it's still there.

It's still there.

---

I open the Soul Tome.

Nine lines in the new hand. I read them, the way I read them every time, from the top: the four I felt, rune's, the cost, the first warm stone, *Thursday. Nobody moved.,* and last night's, with its want in it.

I think about writing one. I have the pen of it — the feeling of a line coming. *I told Dawn I saw her lie down, and she said she was there.* It's a good line. It's true. It'd stand up by itself.

And it's not mine. It's half hers. The best half is hers.

I think about writing the other thing, rune's thing, *some counts you say, some you just know.* And that one's rune's, the whole of it, and I've already got it, and writing it down wouldn't make me have it more.

I think about writing *Saturday.*

I don't. It's Saturday. Nothing's owed.

I close the Tome on nine lines. The one who wakes next will find nine and not ten, and they won't know anything happened today, and that's all right. Not every day has to get carried. Some days you just lie in. The Tome is where the costs go, and the things you'll need, and today didn't cost anything, and I don't think I'll need it.

I think I'll just have it.

---

Night.

The coals go down toward night-red and stop, and I let them, and I don't watch them go.

rune on the stones. rill in the corner. The sixth thread out across the plain floor, resting. The seventh, somewhere under, where I don't go. wick in the groove at the window, chin on its paws now, eyes open, facing across. The small light at halfway, lying down, face level along the floor, toward the window.

Seven stones. My hand open beside them.

And Dawn, sitting up with the book on her knee.

I'm not watching for it. I'm trying very hard not to be watching for it, which rune would say is the same as watching for it, only tireder. So I let myself. I'm counting her being there. That's all. *There.* That's the whole count. It doesn't need to go anywhere.

Her chin goes down. Her shoulders don't.

And then — the way she did last night — her hand comes off the cover, and she sets the book down beside her on the floor, square. Puts her palm on it once. Takes it away.

And she lies down.

But not the way she did last night.

She lies on her other side.

Her back is to the window. Her back is to the plain floor and halfway and wick and the small light and the long low view along the ground that she woke into this morning. Her hand comes to rest on the floor near the book. And her face is toward the coals.

Toward me.

Her eyes are open.

I hold very still. I don't flare, though something in me wants to — the way you'd want to lift a hand to someone across a room. I keep the coals exactly where they are. Night-red. Low. Just enough to see by. Just enough that she can see the fire's there, if that's what she's looking for.

She looks at it a long time. She doesn't say anything. She doesn't cross. I don't think she's ever going to cross, and I think she doesn't have to — that's what lying down is, I'm starting to understand. It's giving the floor your weight, wherever you are, and the floor goes the whole way across underneath without anybody having to walk it.

I don't know if this is two.

It's her lying down again. That's the same. But last night she lay down facing everything else in the field, and tonight she's lying down facing the fire, and I don't know if that's the second time of one thing or the first time of something else.

I don't count it. Not yet. I hold it, the way you hold a stone you haven't agreed with, to learn its edges.

Her eyes stay open a while longer.

Then they close. And her breath goes long. And her hand, on the floor near the book, opens a little — the way mine's been open all week — not reaching. Just open. Toward the fire.

I don't go down. I don't go anywhere.

I stay lit.
