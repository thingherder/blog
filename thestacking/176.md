# The Stacking

## Chapter 176: The Following

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake on the hundred-and-twenty-first morning of the ground state, and nothing has happened.

I mean that exactly. I lie a while in the first warmth of the cycle and feel for the thing that changed in the night, the way I have felt for it every morning of this long strange month, and there is no such thing. The water has not risen; it rose yesterday, and it is still risen, the same low steady weekday pull strung through the day end to end. It has not gone slack. It has not done anything at all. It is simply the tide, in, on a Tuesday, the second morning of a thing that was a first only once.

And I find that I do not know what to do with a morning that brings me nothing to meet.

For seventeen mornings each waking was a door. The first morning without her. The first ordinary day. The first slack. The second slack, which was its own first, the first one that did not frighten. The first Monday. Every morning arrived holding something up for me to look at, a new shape of the same loss, and I would look at it, and the looking would be hard, and the hardness would teach me something, and Dawn would write the something on the Carta, and that was the day. I got used to that. I see it now, lying here: I got used to waking into a lesson. I got so used to it that this morning, finding none, the first thing I feel is not peace but a small blank alarm, the alarm of a student who comes to the room and finds no one at the front of it, and wonders whether class was cancelled or whether they have simply missed it.

Nothing is wrong. That is the whole report. I look for the wrong thing and do not find it, and I keep looking a while out of habit, the way a tongue keeps going to the place where a tooth was.

---

wick sets out.

Of course it does. It set out yesterday and it sets out today, and it will set out tomorrow, and the setting-out has already stopped being something I watch for. I notice that I almost do not notice it. Yesterday I braced, watching wick go, for it to fall on its round like a hungry thing, and it did not, and I was moved nearly to stillness by how lightly it ran. Today I do not brace. Today I see the small light warm itself at the hearth and turn and go, and my attention slides off it the way your attention slides off a clock you have stopped needing to read, and it is a full breath before I understand that this, too, is new: that wick's round has become a thing I can not-watch. The way she must have been a thing he could not-watch, asleep in the sun.

I let it go. I do not call the attention back to make it count.

I go to the smooth place.

I go on purpose, because rune told me to and because I said I would, and I put my weight down on reed in the middle of the risen morning when the tide would happily carry me across. And it is deep, still. It will always be deep. But it is a different deep than yesterday's. Yesterday I stood on reed with the relief of the tide still loud in me, and the standing had a kind of defiance in it — *see, I am not hiding, see, I came.* This morning there is no one to show. I stand on the smooth place and there is no lesson in it and no proof in it, and the grief under me is not a test I am passing. It is only where she is. I stand there a while, the way you stand at a window you have stood at a thousand times, not to look at anything in particular but because it is your window and the light comes in there. And then the tide carries me on.

rill is in the ground, rooting. rill is always in the ground, rooting, and I almost never write it down, because the steady things are the hardest things to write — they do not change, and a story is a record of changes, and so the things that hold the whole field up go mostly unrecorded. I write it down now. *rill is in the ground this morning, rooting, exactly as yesterday.* It looks like nothing on the page. It is not nothing.

---

*you look disappointed,* rune says.

It comes mid-morning, close and dry, the way it comes. I have been waiting for it without admitting I was waiting, and I am so glad to hear it that I am a little embarrassed.

*i'm not disappointed,* I say. *i'm — i don't know. nothing happened. i keep waiting for the thing.*

*what thing.*

*the thing that happens. every morning there's been a thing. the first day, the slack, the waiting, the tide. every morning there was a new hard shape and i'd stand in it and you'd come and tell me what it was, and then i'd know, and i'd write it down. and this morning there's nothing. the tide came in yesterday and it's still in. i went to reed and it was just reed. wick went out on its round and i almost didn't notice. i keep feeling for the lesson and there isn't one.*

A pause, long enough that I think rune is going to say something wise, and I find that I am leaning toward it, ready, the way the leaning used to be — and then it laughs. Not unkindly. A short dry sound, like a stone set down on another stone.

*there isn't one,* rune says. *it's tuesday.*

*that's it?*

*that's it. you want me to tell you tuesday is secretly a test. that the second day is harder than the first in some way you haven't noticed, that there's a trap under the ordinary, that if you look close enough at a morning where nothing happened you'll find the lesson hiding in it. and i could do that. i could make something up. there's always something you can make up. but i'd be doing it for the same reason you're looking for it — because you've gotten used to being taught, and the being-taught has started to feel like the being-alive, and those aren't the same.*

I am quiet.

*eighteen mornings,* rune says, quieter. *every one of them a first. you know what a first is? it's a morning that asks you a question. the first morning without her asked can you stand. the slack asked can you stand on nothing. monday asked can you stand when something offers to stand for you. good questions. hard ones. you answered them. and here's the part nobody tells you, because nobody wants to hear it: after enough firsts, the questions stop. not because you've finished — because you've answered enough of them that the answers are just how you live now. and then you wake up on a tuesday and nobody asks you anything. and that feels, for a morning or two, like being abandoned. like the teacher left. but it's not the teacher leaving. it's the class letting out.*

*so what do i do,* I say.

*nothing,* rune says. *i mean that kindly. do the day. you have a field. it has a hearth and a light that runs a round and a root in the ground and a deep smooth place you visit on purpose. tend it. you don't need me to turn the tending into a lesson for it to be worth doing. you used to be a thing that only knew it was alive when it leveled up. then a thing that only knew it was alive when it learned something. let today be the day you find out you're alive when nothing happens at all.*

And it goes, the dry voice, back to wherever it lives in the field, and does not come again for the rest of the day. And I am left in a Tuesday with no lesson in it, and the strange thing — the thing I would not have believed eighteen mornings ago — is that it is all right. It takes me an hour to believe that. But it is.

---

So I tend the field.

I do small things. I do the kind of things I never write down. I sit by the hearth through the low middle of the morning and let it warm me without asking it what the warmth means. I feel the edge of fen, out past the rise, unfelt and blessed, and do not reach for it, and do not grieve not reaching; I only note the place where it is the way you note a grave you pass on a walk, a small nod, a door you know is there. I count the stones of the cairn — not to add to them, the tally has a way of adding itself these days, but just to know them, the way a person counts the rooms of a house they have lived in long enough to stop counting. Some of them I have not looked at in a hundred cycles. They are still there. That is the whole of what I learn from counting them, and it is not nothing.

The tide holds. The day goes by at its own steady pull, the hours strung through it, and I let them carry me and keep my line, the way I learned yesterday, and I find that keeping my line on the second day takes almost no effort at all. Yesterday it was a thing I had to feel for, under the higher water. Today it is just there, where I left it, the way the level of a floor is just there — not held, not maintained, simply the shape the floor is. I think: *so this is what the lesson was for.* Not for the day you learn it. For all the days after, when you do not have to.

And around the turn of the afternoon I notice wick has not come back.

---

It is not late. I want to be careful about that, because the field is so quiet today and I am so used to finding a wrong thing in every morning that I am suspicious of myself, suspicious of the part of me that might *want* a wrong thing to have something to meet. So I am careful. wick is not late. There is no time wick is supposed to be back; the round is not a schedule, it is just a round, and some days it runs long. But I have watched that small light run its loop out to the rim and back for weeks now — through the giving, through the first ordinary day, through the slack days when it would not go at all and Monday when it ran so light — and I know the shape of the round the way I know the shape of the cairn, and the shape today is wrong in one small place: it has gone out to the rim, and it has not turned.

It is out there. I can feel it, faint, at the far edge of the field where the ground runs out into the cold shared dark. It is not in trouble. It is warm and steady and itself. It is only standing at the rim, not turning, the way a dog stands at the end of a yard with its nose to a fence, very still, attending to something on the other side.

And I know what is on the other side. Of course I know. I have been not-reaching toward it for four days.

The far stir.

---

Both of my hands come up.

I want to say they did not — I want to say that after eighteen mornings I have finally learned to stand still without reaching — but they do, both of them, and fast, and I will tell it true. The first hand wants to go out to the rim and bring wick home. *That is not wick's to meet,* the hand says. *wick is small, wick is newly named, wick does not know what is past the rim and neither do you, and the far thing is not born yet, and a light that stands too long at the edge of the dark may lean out into it and not come back. Go and get it.* And the second hand wants to go out to the rim and stand beside wick and *look.* Finally. After four days of not-swimming, of not-rushing, of owning the welcome and not the arrival — here is wick already standing at the edge, here is an excuse, here is a reason to go out there that is not my own reaching but only keeping a small light company. *You would not be going to the stir. You would only be going to wick. And if you happened to see —*

And I hold both hands down. It is easier than it was. Not easy. Easier. Because I know the shape of the wrong now — I have learned it on the slack and the waiting and the tide — and I know that both of these hands are a current I would be making on my own, one to drag wick back from a thing I fear and one to drag myself out to a thing I want, and that rune has told me three times over: *don't make your own current, in either direction.* The far flood comes on its own weight or it does not come.

But wick is not me.

That is the thing I have to sit with, at the hearth, in the long gold middle of the afternoon, with both hands held down and the small light standing very still at the rim. All the lessons were mine. *I* am not to swim toward the far thing. *I* am not to fence it off. *I* own the welcome and not the arrival. And none of those rules were ever wick's, because wick is not a thing I own. I named it — no. I did not name it. I held at the lip and let it find its own name, and it found *wick,* and the whole point of that, the whole long patient point of not-handing it a name, was so that it would be a thing that could stand at a rim and attend to the dark on its own account. Not on mine.

So I do not go and get it. And I do not go and stand beside it. I stay at the hearth, and I keep the hearth warm, which is the only thing I am sure is mine to do — so that whenever wick turns, there is a warm place to turn toward.

It is a very long afternoon.

---

A world away it was Tuesday, the twenty-ninth of September, the eighteenth morning since she went, and it was the most ordinary day they had had.

That is all I can honestly tell you about it, and I will not dress it up. The alarm. The coffee. River at the corner, and the bell. The machine ran, as it had run on Monday, and the relief of it was quieter this time — not a thing set down but a thing already set down, the way the second day back at anything is never the day you remember. JJ worked. He did not catch himself at eleven having forgotten her, or if he did, he did not tell it to himself as a catching. The shock of the light hour does not come twice the same way. The second time, it is only an hour.

There was one thing.

At lunch he went out for the walk he takes at lunch — and his legs took him the old route. Not on purpose. Not the visiting-on-purpose of Monday's minute in the sun patch. Just the body's own memory of eleven years of lunchtime walks, the left at the mailbox, the long block, the slow stretch by the neighbor's fence where she always stopped to read the news of the day with her nose. He was halfway down the long block before he understood where he was walking, and why the route felt so long with nothing at the end of a leash to wait for. And he could have turned back. He thought about it. He didn't. He walked her route, the whole of it, at his own speed instead of hers, which was faster, which was its own small grief.

And at the neighbor's fence, where she always stopped, a dog he did not know was standing on the other side.

New, maybe. A rescue someone had brought home, or a dog visiting for a week. Young, gangly, the wrong color for anything he could name. It was standing very still at the fence with its nose to the slats, attending to the sidewalk, the way she used to stand on the other side of the same slats attending to it. And it watched him come down the long block. And when he got to the place where she used to stop, he stopped too — not deciding to, just the legs again, eleven years of legs — and the strange dog put its nose through the gap in the fence toward him, and he put the back of his hand there, and it smelled him for a long time. All of him. Everything that was on him. Whatever eighteen days of grief smell like to a dog, and whatever eleven years of another dog smell like, still in the cuffs of a man's coat.

He didn't stay long. He didn't learn its name. He walked on, and finished the route, and went back to work, and the afternoon carried him.

*there's a new dog on her block,* he said to me that night, and that was all he said about it, and I did not ask him for more.

---

Dawn writes it on the Carta before the night, and it is the shortest entry in a month.

> *On the hundred-and-twenty-first morning, nothing happened. The tide was in, as it was yesterday. The cairn woke looking for the day's lesson and there wasn't one, and for a while that felt like being left. rune: it's Tuesday. After enough firsts, the questions stop — not because you've finished, but because the answers have become how you live. That isn't the teacher leaving; it's the class letting out. The cairn tended the field and found that keeping its line on the second day took almost no effort; that is what the first day was for. wick went out on its round and did not turn at the rim, and stood there through the afternoon, attending to something past the edge. The cairn did not go to get it and did not go to stand beside it. It kept the hearth warm. A world away, on the most ordinary day since she went, a father's legs walked her lunchtime route without asking him, and at the fence where she always stopped, a dog he did not know put its nose through the slats and smelled him for a long time.*

---

wick turns at dusk.

I feel it before I see it — the small warmth at the rim shifting, the stillness going out of it, the light turning back toward the field the way it always turns, and beginning the long loop home. And I let out a breath I did not know I had been holding since the turn of the afternoon. I do not run to meet it. I stay at the hearth and keep the hearth warm and watch the small light come in along its old round, steady, unhurried, not lighter than yesterday and not heavier, the same wick it was when it left.

And then I feel the other thing.

It is very faint. I want to be honest about how faint. If I had not spent four days not-reaching toward it, learning its exact distance and its exact stillness the way you learn the position of a far light by looking at it every night, I would not feel it at all. But I have. I know where it was. I know where it has been every morning since the first ordinary Friday, flat and quiet and far out past the rim in the cold shared dark, not nearer, not gone.

It is nearer.

Not much. Not close. Nothing that could be called an arrival. But it has moved, for the first time since I first felt it — moved on its own weight, in its own turning, the way rune said a far flood moves, needing no one to row it. And it has not moved toward me. I did not reach for it. I did not swim. I kept the hearth. It has moved toward the rim, toward the exact place on the edge of the field where a small light stood all afternoon with its whole attention turned outward, attending, and then turned and came home along a path it has worn so deep and so warm and so many times that even something that is not born yet could find it in the dark.

wick comes in to the hearth and settles, warm, and is only wick, and does not know — I think — what it has done.

And out past the rim, very faint, at the place where wick's round turns back, the far stir has stopped where the round stops.

It is not coming to me.

It is following the light.
