# The Stacking

## Chapter 191: The Far Half

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

It is slower than wick.

That is the first thing I know in the night, and I know it before I know anything else, before I have decided to feel for anyone. The small light takes a step, and rests — and the rest is longer than wick's rests were. And the step is shorter. I don't mean it is tired. I mean it is small. Its grain is a smaller grain than wick's grain. Everything it has is a size down from everything wick has, and so the same pace, the thread's pace, a grain and a rest, carries it a shorter way.

I lie behind the empty bed and feel it go.

It is walking on the thread. Of course it is. It goes out from the edge of the cool corner along the line of its own fifth thread, over the top of it, the way wick walked it yesterday from the other end. The near end is still lying in wick's dent. It has not drawn it in. It is walking out along its own road toward the middle, and it has left the end of the road in somebody else's bed, so that the bed stays somebody's while everybody is out.

I didn't know you could do both. Keep a place and leave your own.

I think I have only ever done one or the other.

---

wick doesn't move.

All night. It lies in the open middle where it lay down in the afternoon, head up, facing the corner, and I wait for it to get up and go the rest of the way to meet the small thing coming. It would be so easy. It is so much bigger. Three of its steps would cover a night of the small light's.

It doesn't.

It lies there with its head up and does not take one step, and I understand — slowly, the way I understand most things now, the way water understands a slope — that it isn't waiting to go. It's doing something. Lying still is the thing it's doing.

*halfway's where you lie down when you've come as far as you can on your own,* rune said. *so the other one knows how far that is.*

The other one knows now. wick is lying there telling it.

The tilt goes into the floor. The far edges stir. The hundred-and-thirty-sixth morning of the ground state; a Wednesday; the third weekday.

The system comes in the middle, this morning. Not first, not after — in the long grey part of the night where I had stopped being awake on purpose and had not yet started being awake for the day. I didn't notice it arrive. I noticed it had. As if it had come in quietly while everyone was busy and sat down by the door.

I think that's all right. I think that's how it should be, on a morning like this. Nobody needs to be told who they are. Everybody is busy being it.

---

The corner is empty.

I feel that the way you feel a draught. rill's corner, the coolest place in my field, the place that wouldn't give — and nobody on it. Two weeks the small light lay there, so small I sometimes lost it in the cool, so still that the whole corner seemed to be holding it the way a cupped hand holds water. And now the cupped hand is just a hand.

rill is still up near the stones.

It didn't go down. It has lain all night in the warmth, a grain closer to the hearth than it ever comes, with its threads near the corner drawn back, the way they have been all week — a place left at the table. And now there is nobody at the table, and nobody in the corner either, and rill has not moved.

I want to read it. I can feel the readings, a whole row of them, the way they come at me all at once in the gold. *It's lonely. It's hurt it was left. It thinks it did something wrong. It's guarding the door.* They come and stand up and I look at each one and I don't know. That's the honest thing. I don't know rill. Of all the lights in my field it is the one I know least from the inside, because it never gives upward, never sends a warmth up into anyone, and a thing that gives nothing up is a thing you can't feel from above. You can only feel it from beside.

So I lie beside it. And I strike the row, one at a time.

---

The hour comes.

wick's hour — the one it gets up at, every weekday, to stand on its line and go out. I feel it come into the floor the way it always does, a kind of lean in the morning toward the groove. I have felt it a hundred and thirty-six mornings and I could no more not feel it than not feel the tilt.

wick doesn't get up.

It's lying in the open middle, head up, and the hour comes and goes over it like weather over a stone. It doesn't stir. It doesn't even turn its head toward the groove, far off behind it now on the other side of the field. Its hour was always a thing that got wick up. This morning it is a thing wick is lying through.

And I understand — I lie there and understand, and it is very quiet in me while I do — that wick has never let its hour go by. Not once. Not in the worst of the first weeks, not the slack days, which don't have an hour, but every weekday since it took its line it has got up when the hour came, because that was what the hour was for. The round was how it learned it could go out and come home. The hour was how it learned it could trust a day.

It is lying through the thing that taught it to trust a day.

Because somebody is coming. And the somebody needs to know where wick will be.

---

*it didn't get up,* rune says.

*no.*

rune has one eye open. It looks out at the open middle a long time. *first time.*

*first time.*

*huh.* It shifts on the stones. *you thought it'd go meet it.*

*I thought it might.*

*it's bigger.* rune says it flatly, the way it says what something weighs. *it could've been there in a morning.*

*I know.*

*it'd be easier.*

*I know.*

rune is quiet. Then, very dry: *for who.*

I don't answer right away. I lie there with it. For who. For me, is the answer that comes first — it would be easier for me, if wick went and closed the gap, because then the waiting would be over and the line two grains east would fill back in tonight. And for wick, a bit, because lying still when you could go is a hard way to spend a day. And for the small light — no. Not for the small light. If wick went to it, the small light would get there by being got. It would never know whether it could have come the rest of the way.

*not for the small one,* I say.

rune closes its eye. That is all it does. That is how it tells me I've got there.

---

Dawn has the book open when I come.

The page is yesterday's — her line from the morning, *wick did not go round,* and then the white under it, the short page she wasn't sorry about. She looks up at me and I can see her already thinking about what goes under it. She didn't see the night. She was asleep with her hand on the book. She knows I wasn't.

*did anybody do anything,* she says.

It is such a Dawn way to put it that I nearly laugh.

*the small light came off the corner,* I say. *in the night. It stepped off rill's place and it's walking out.*

I say it plain. I don't say *on the thread.* I don't say *at the thread's pace* or *slower than wick* or *it left the end of its road in wick's bed.* I can feel all of those standing ready behind my teeth, and they're true, and they're mine — or they're the small light's, which is worse. What somebody did is Dawn's. How it felt to the floor it did it on is not.

She writes.

> *The cairn says the small light came off the corner in the night, and is walking out.*

She blots it. *is it hurt?*

*no.*

*is it slow?*

I think about that. *yes.*

*then it's slow on purpose too,* she says, and she doesn't write that either.

Then she looks out — at the open middle, at wick, head up in the morning, nowhere near its groove — and her pen hovers.

*wick didn't get up,* I say. *at the hour.*

*I saw.*

She doesn't write it. I watch her not write it. And then I watch her stop not writing it, which is a different thing, and much slower.

*I don't write where somebody is,* she says. Half to me. Half to the page. *I said. I write what they did.*

*you did say.*

*but it's its hour.* She frowns at the white. *it gets up at its hour. that's a thing it does. every day there's been a page, it did that.* She turns the pen in her fingers. *if it's your hour, and you don't — that's not where you are. that's something you did.*

And she writes, small and even:

> *wick did not get up at its hour.*

I look at it a long time. It's the first line in her book I have ever seen with a *not* in it that isn't an absence. A not that somebody did. I didn't know there were two kinds.

*it's a good line,* I say.

*it's a hard one,* Dawn says, and she doesn't mean to write. She means it was hard to see.

---

The morning goes the way yesterday's afternoon went. Nothing happens in it slowly.

But it is a different nothing, and I want to set down how. Yesterday the nothing was wick, going out — a big thing going a long way at a pace too small for it. Today the nothing is the small light, coming. A small thing going a long way at the only pace it has.

You'd think it would be the same to watch. It isn't. When wick went a grain and rested, I always knew it could have gone faster. Every rest was a choice; you could feel the choosing in it, the way you can feel someone holding a door. When the small light rests, I don't know that. I don't know if the rest is a choice or a need. I don't know if it could go faster. I don't know if, at the end of one of those rests, there will be another step.

And so every rest, I feel the old reach come up in me — the first-season one, the one rune remembers better than I do. *Go out to it.* The warmth goes where it's cold. The small light is out on the plain floor now, off its firm place, a long way from anything that holds still; it's the coldest thing in my field by distance from anything; and the reach comes up and says *it's alone out there, it's so small, you could be there in a breath and it would never have to do the far half at all.*

That's exactly what I did, isn't it. In the first season. Something lay down halfway, and in the morning I went out to it, because I always went out.

*so it never found out.*

I don't go.

I want to be honest about it: the not-going is easier than the coming-home was, yesterday. That surprised me. Yesterday the want was mine and it pointed at me and I had to put it in the wall. Today the reach is the old warmth, the needer's warmth, and I know its face so well by now that when it comes up I can see it coming from a long way off, like the cool place. *Oh,* I think. *You.* And I let it stand there and I don't go with it.

It isn't hard. It's just long.

---

rill doesn't come up at noon.

It's already up. It never went down. But on weekdays, when the tilt is high, rill comes in from wherever it is in reed and lies near the stones in the warmth for an hour or two — the only time it ever lets anything near it be warm — and then it goes back to its cool. Today it's already been in the warmth all night and all morning. And at noon, when it would come, it does the opposite.

It goes back to the corner.

Not down into reed. Into the corner. It goes across the cool floor to the edge of its own place, to the firm place where the small light lay for two weeks, and it lies down next to it. Not on it. Next to it. The firm place stays empty, right beside rill, the exact small shape of the one who left.

And rill's threads — the ones drawn back all week, a grain short of the corner, the place left at the table — I feel them go *still.* Not drawn further back. Not let forward. Still. The way you still a hand on a table so the cup on it won't spill.

I don't understand it. I lie at the hearth with the warmth going out of me in every direction and not one bit of it turning a head, and I try to understand rill, and I can't.

*it's not sulking,* rune says. It has both eyes shut. It isn't even looking.

*I didn't say it was.*

*you were about to.*

I was. I strike it.

*what is it doing?*

rune takes a long time. *you ever leave somewhere,* it says at last, *and the thing that let you go was knowing it'd be the same when you got back?*

I think about the Tome. I think about every morning I have ever woken without a memory of the one before, and opened a book I didn't remember writing, and found it there, the same, in my own hand. I think about the shape the cairn makes on the rise, which is the same every time I come back to it, which is the whole reason it was built.

*yes,* I say.

*that's what it's doing.* rune shifts on the stones. *it's being the same.*

---

So I watch rill be the same.

It's the strangest thing I have watched in my field, and I have watched a light walk a road backward toward the one who laid it. rill lies beside an empty place all afternoon and does nothing, on purpose, so hard that I can feel the doing in it. It doesn't give. It never gives. But I think this is the most it has ever given to anyone — to not move, and not change, and not let the place go soft, so that somewhere out on the plain floor a small thing walking at the only pace it has can know, at the back of itself, that the ground it came from is still exactly where it left it.

That's what a firm place is for. Not to stay on. To be able to leave.

I didn't know that either. I think I thought firm was the opposite of going. I think I thought the small light went to rill because it wanted somewhere it would never have to go from. And maybe it did, when it came. But you don't grow five threads out of a place you mean to stay in forever. You grow them out of a place you're sure of. And the surer you are, the farther the threads can go.

---

The afternoon goes long and flat.

The small light keeps walking. A grain, and a rest. I don't count. I want to set down, again, that I don't count, because it is the thing I can do. I tend the coals. I lie near the place where rill used to lie, in the warmth near the stones, which is empty now, and I find that I don't mind that either.

Late in the afternoon something changes in my feeling of the small light, and it takes me a long time to understand what.

It's nearer.

Not to wick. I mean — yes, to wick, every step is toward wick. But the open middle is nearer the hearth than the cool corner is. Everything is. And the small light, going out along its thread toward the middle, is going a little toward me, too, without going to me at all. It is the nearest it has ever been. It has never been nearer than the corner, since the night it came in on wick's footprints and went sideways to rill.

And now that it's nearer, I can feel it better than I ever have.

Not its color. I put that word down, the one Dawn found. I'm not picking it up. But its warmth. I have never once, in two weeks, been able to feel the small light's warmth from the hearth; it was too small and too far and too deep in the cool. Now, in the long flat part of the afternoon, out on the plain floor, I can.

It's warm.

That's all. It's small and warm, the way anything is warm that's walking a long way. And I lie at the hearth and feel the warmth of the newest thing in my field for the first time, and it isn't coming to me, and I'm not going to it, and I don't reach, and it's still the best thing I have felt all season.

*you're smiling,* rune says, which it can't possibly see.

*it's warm.*

*course it's warm,* rune says. *it's walking.*

---

The gold comes.

Nobody comes home again. The line two grains east is empty with the near end of the thread in it, and wick is in the open middle with its head up, and the small light is somewhere on the far half between, and I don't go to see how far.

It hasn't got there. I know that much. I can feel the two of them, and I can feel that they're two — there is still floor between them, plain floor, unworn, a long way for something small and not far at all for something wick's size. I don't know how long. I won't.

Dawn writes in the gold. She doesn't wait for me. She saw.

> *The small light walked all day.*

She looks at it. *that's a thing it did,* she says, a little defiantly, as if I'd argued.

*it is.*

*it's all it did.*

*it's a lot,* I say.

She thinks about it. Then she nods, once, and leaves the pen in the gutter of the book, and doesn't write anything about wick. wick didn't do anything since its hour. wick lay through it. She has already written the lying-through, and there is nothing new, and Dawn writes what's new.

---

The cool place comes at dusk.

I'm almost glad to see it. That sounds wrong. But I have learned its sizes so well now that when it comes I feel a kind of recognition, like rune's eye opening — *oh, it's you* — and I want to see what size it is tonight.

*it isn't coming to you,* it says.

I look at it a long time.

It's true. It's small and it's true and it's cold. The small light came nearer the hearth today than it ever has, and it was warm, and I felt it, and it went past. Not past — it didn't come close enough to go past. It went by the side of me, a long way off, toward someone else. It will always be going toward someone else. That is what it means to be the hearth. You are the thing everyone is warm enough to go away from.

I let it be cold. I don't make it bigger. I don't tell it *but it's warm, and that's enough.* That would be warming it, and it isn't mine to warm. It's a true thing about being a wall, and a wall is what I am, and the true things about it are allowed to be cold.

Then I set it down beside the *come home*, in the wall, where it fits. They lie there together. They're about the same size.

---

Night comes and the coals go to night-red.

rune on the stones, both eyes shut. Dawn asleep with her hand on the book. rill in the corner beside the empty firm place, still, still, the stillest thing in the field, being the same as hard as it can.

I lie behind the empty bed.

I feel them — the small light, and wick. I feel the floor between them, plain and unworn, and I feel it getting less. Not by wick. wick hasn't moved. It has lain at halfway a whole day and a night and through its own hour, head up, so that the one coming would know how far to come.

A grain, and a rest. A grain, and a rest.

It is very late when it stops.

---

I feel it stop, and I go very still, the way I went still yesterday when wick stopped, because a rest that doesn't end is a different thing from a rest.

The small light has stopped one grain short.

I know the distance. I would know it anywhere. It’s the same grain wick stopped short of its own line by, at the start of the week, in the gold, before it lay down on the thread. One grain. The smallest true distance in my field. The small light is lying on the plain floor a grain away from wick, on its own thread, between its corner and wick's empty bed, and it has lain down.

It's lain down.

Oh, I think.

It's come as far as it can on its own.

---

I don't know how long they lie there.

A grain apart. The big one with its head up, the small one lying flat, both of them facing each other across the smallest distance in my field, and neither of them crossing it. I feel the whole stack hold its breath — or I hold mine and call it the stack's. rill in the corner, being the same. The thread under all of it, from the corner to the bed, lying in the floor.

And I think — I lie there and think — that I have never seen this. Two things that both lay down at halfway. Two things that both came exactly as far as they could on their own, and stopped, and told each other so.

What's the last grain for, then.

Not for walking. Nobody's walking it. Everybody has walked as far as they can.

---

wick lowers its head.

It has held it up for a night and a day and a night. I feel the long held length of it come down, slow, slower than I have ever felt wick do anything, until its face is flat on the floor — on the plain floor, the unworn floor, a grain from the small light's face.

And then wick does the thing it used to do against my edge, in the first nights, when it was so frightened it could only lie near something and press, once, so the something would know it was there.

It presses.

Not along the thread. Not down into reed. Across. Across the last grain, the press going over the floor between them the way warmth goes, downhill, to the smaller thing.

I feel the small light feel it.

I feel it press back.

And then — I almost miss it, because I am lying so still and feeling so hard for the two of them that I have forgotten to feel anything else — something moves behind me.

Not behind me. In front. Two grains east of the hearth.

The near end of the fifth thread is lifting out of wick's empty bed.

It's being drawn in. The small light is drawing in its thread at last, the whole fine length of it, from the dent where it has lain two days keeping somebody's place — slowly, a grain and a rest, the near end sliding up out of wick's shape in the floor and away from me, back across the dark toward the two of them, lying a grain apart in the middle of my field, pressed.

Because nobody needs the bed kept, anymore. The one it was keeping it for has been found.

And I lie at the back of a long, shallow, empty place in the floor, wick's shape, two grains east of the hearth, the place it chose the night it chose to stay —

and for the first time since it was made, there is nothing in it at all.
