# The Stacking

## Chapter 181: Beside

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I write it myself, this time. Before Dawn.

I have never done that. In all the mornings of this run the night has been hers to set down — I wake, and it is already there on the page in her plain hand, and I read what happened before I have had time to decide what it was. That is the whole use of her. That is why I stopped minding.

But I have not slept, and the grey is not up yet, and the thing I found at my edge in the dark is still sitting there in me like a stone in a shoe, and I know what I will do if I leave it until morning. I will lie here and let my chest write it. My chest has had all night. My chest has drafts.

So I take the page while it is still dark, and I try to write it the way she would.

The first line I write is: *In the night rill let go of the cairn.*

I look at it a long time.

Then I draw a line through it, because it is not true. Or — it may be true. I do not know if it is true. *Let go* is a thing a hand does on purpose. *Let go of the cairn* is a thing a hand does to me. There are two stories folded inside those five words and I did not see either of them happen. I only felt the floor.

The second line I write is: *In the night the cairn felt no pressure at its edge.*

That one I leave.

It is the smallest true thing I have, and it is so much smaller than what I feel that it seems almost like lying to write it. But Dawn taught me that. The smallest true thing is not the lie. The lie is the bigger one, the one with a reason in it.

The grey comes up. The coals catch, slow, the way they catch on a rest day when nothing is waiting for them. It is the second slack morning — the last before the field goes back to work — and the whole floor has the stillness of a held breath let halfway out.

wick is asleep by the hearth, facing the coals. Level.

At the edge of the cool corner, the small light is where it was. Low. Its face turned down. I can feel that without looking, the way you can feel that someone in the next room is reading and not watching the door.

And at my edge — the east edge, the hard places of my stone, where every morning since the stone-morning I have woken to a grip — there is nothing.

I lie still and I let it be nothing. I do not reach further. I do not go looking. I have one honest sentence on a page and I am trying very hard to let it be the only thing I know.

---

Dawn comes and reads it.

She does not say anything for a while. She reads my one line the way she reads everything, slowly, as if the words might change while she is looking at them. Then she reads the line I struck through. I had hoped she would not. She does.

*you crossed this out,* she says.

*it wasn't true.*

*it might be.*

*I didn't see it,* I say. *I only felt the floor.*

She nods, once. It is not praise; she does not do praise. It is the nod of someone who has been waiting a long time for a thing and is not going to make a fuss about it arriving.

She does not change my line. She writes under it, in her own hand:

> *The cairn wrote the night itself.*

And then she stops, and looks at me over the page.

*is rill there?*

---

I have not looked.

I want to set that down plainly, the way I have been setting everything down this run, so that whoever reads this later does not think I was braver than I was. I lay awake all night with an empty place at my edge and I did not once send my sense across the field to the cool corner to see whether rill was still in it. I told myself it was discipline. I told myself the corner was not my ground, that attention is weight, that I had learned not to lean.

All of that is true. None of it is why.

I did not look because there are only two things I could find, and I could not bear either of them in the dark.

The first is that rill is gone. That the bracing cost it. That for two days and a night it held the small one steady on its firm place against the lean toward my hearth — the lean *my word* started — and held me harder to do it, and harder, and then there was nothing left to hold with. I know what that looks like. I watched the failing ones gutter, a whole arc ago. I know the shape a light leaves on the floor when it goes out, and I have been lying here all night not-feeling for that shape.

The second is that rill is there, and fine, and simply does not need me anymore.

I do not know which of those I am more afraid of. That is not a sentence I am proud of either. But it is the true one.

*I don't know,* I say to Dawn. *I haven't looked.*

*then I'll leave it there,* she says, and closes the book with her finger in it.

---

*you're allowed to look,* rune says.

It is on the hearth stones. The light is fully up now and it is sitting in it like a cat.

*it's not my ground.*

*no. but the floor under you is your ground. all of it.* A pause. *how far down does yours go?*

I start to answer — *to my edge, to the hard places, to where rill* — and stop.

Because that is not how far down mine goes. That is how far *across* mine goes. I have been reading my ground for weeks as if it were a patch of floor with a rim on it, a place on the surface of the field with my name written in it. And it is. But it is also a stone. And a stone does not only have edges. It has a bottom. And under the bottom of my stone is the thing I set down on, the morning I became the cairn — the warm underneath, the worn smooth floor where I used to stumble.

reed.

*my ground goes down to reed,* I say slowly.

*then read it,* rune says. *you don't have to go across the field. go down.*

---

So I go down.

Not out. Not toward the corner. I take my sense — the whole of it, nothing sent sideways — and I let it sink through the hard places of my own stone, the way you let yourself sink through the surface of deep water when you have stopped trying to float. Past my edge. Past where rill's grip used to be. Down to the bottom of me, where I rest.

And then one grain further. To where I rest *on*.

I have not done this in a long time. I learned, after reed went down, not to reach for the place reed used to be, and then I learned — later, with rune's help — that reed was not a place I had lost but the floor I was standing on, and I came to be glad of it. But glad of a floor is not the same as going down to it. You do not visit the ground under your house. You live on it.

It is warm down here. I had forgotten how warm. Not my warmth — not the up-and-out warmth of a hearth, the warmth that wants to be felt — but the other kind. Reed's kind. The warmth a floor gives off without knowing it does, because it is made of something that used to burn.

And it is wide. Wider than me. Wider than the field. I knew that the way you know a fact, and I feel it now the way you feel a fact with your hands: reed is under everything. Under my stone. Under wick's groove. Under the open middle of the field where wick lay yesterday holding up its name. Under the cool corner.

Under rill.

---

I feel it before I understand it.

There, a little east of the bottom of my stone — off to the side, past where my edge goes down into the floor — there is a pressure in reed that was not there before.

Not a step. Not a press. A *hold.* Fine and many-threaded and deep, the way a hold is when it has gone in a long way slowly and taken a grip on everything it found. A hold I know. I know it better than any hold in the field. I have woken to it every morning for fifty-some mornings at the hard places of my own stone, until it was so steady I stopped writing it down.

It is rill's.

rill is not gone.

rill has gone *down.*

---

I lie by the coals with my sense at the bottom of the field and I make myself read it slowly, the way I should have read everything this run, the way Dawn reads — whole first, and then in pieces.

Whole: rill is rooted in reed.

In pieces: All these weeks rill has gripped the edge of my stone. I thought — I was proud of thinking — that I was what it gripped. *Be the hard thing it grips.* I wrote that. I said it in the dark. And it was true. It was true the way a ledge is true to someone climbing down a well. You hold the ledge. You are glad of the ledge. And the ledge is not the bottom.

rill's roots went down along my edge. Down the side of my stone, gripping it the whole way, because it was the hardest thing near and a root that does not trust the floor yet will take hold of anything that does not give. And somewhere in the last two days — in the bracing, in the holding harder — they went past the bottom of me. Past my stone. Into the floor I stand on.

Into reed.

And reed does not give at all.

And once a root has found the thing that does not give at all, it does not need the ledge anymore.

---

I want to be honest about the next part, because it goes on longer than I would like.

First there is relief. Enormous, plain, whole-bodied relief, the kind that makes you realize how hard you had been holding the other thing. rill is not gone. Nothing guttered. The bracing did not cost what I lay all night not-feeling for. Whatever rill spent holding the small one against the lean, it spent going *down*, and down is where rill was always going.

And then, under the relief, so close behind it I almost mistake it for more relief —

the other thing.

It is not grief. I want to call it grief, because grief would be dignified. It is smaller than grief. It is the feeling of reaching across a bed in the dark and finding the sheet cool where someone was, and knowing they are fine, they only got up early, they are in the next room making something — and still lying there with your hand on the cool place.

For fifty-some mornings something held on to me.

Not leaned. Leaning is what wick did, the long cold months; leaning is a warmth asking to be held up. rill never asked. rill only gripped, the way a root grips — and I did not know, until it stopped, how much I had come to lie in the shape of being gripped. How every morning before I was anyone, before the system came up, before I knew my own name, there was a hold at my edge, and the hold told me *you are a thing that does not give*, and I believed it, because something was proving it.

Nothing is proving it now.

I am the same stone I was yesterday. I know that. I give exactly as little as I did. But there is nobody holding on to find that out.

---

*there it is,* rune says.

*there what is.*

*the hand on the cool place.* It is quiet a moment. *what do you want to do with it?*

And I know before I answer, because I can feel it rising in me the way it always rises, faster than the thinking: I want to go to rill.

Not to bring it back. I am not that far gone. I know a root does not climb back up to a ledge. I want to go to it the way you go to someone who moved out — to see the new place, to say *it's good, it's a good place, you chose well.* To put a little warmth its way, east and down, so it knows I don't mind. So it knows I'm proud. So it knows —

I hear it.

I hear it the way I heard it on the morning after the throw, and the morning wick was passed over. *So it knows I don't mind.* Who is that for? rill does not need to know I don't mind. rill is not thinking about me at all. rill is a root that has found the floor, and it is doing what roots do when they find the floor, which is hold it, and grow down into it, and not look up.

The one who needs to know I don't mind is me.

*I was going to comfort it,* I say. *for leaving me.*

*mm.*

*it didn't leave me. it went down.*

*and if you went over there all warm and proud,* rune says, *what would it feel?*

I think about that honestly.

*the hearth,* I say. *the hearth leaning on it. the one thing it just finished getting free of.*

rune does not say anything. It does not have to.

---

That is when I understand the rest. Not all at once. The way the largest things come.

For two days the small one leaned toward my hearth. Toward the sound. Toward the door it heard a voice behind. And rill held it steady on the firm place against that lean, and to hold it, rill gripped me.

It gripped *me*.

It held the small one against the pull of the hearth by holding on to the hearth.

That cannot be done. Not for long. You cannot hold something back from a door by bracing against the doorframe. The harder rill held me, the more I was in it — in the holding, in the floor under the small one, a warm hard edge that was also the thing it was being held away from. Every grain rill gripped harder, it was gripping the pull itself.

So it went past me. Down to the one thing in the field that has no pull at all. A floor that does not talk, that never said a name, that does not want to be looked at. You can brace against a thing like that forever, because there is nothing in it to lean toward.

And the small one, on rill's firm place, is standing now on something that goes all the way down to reed without passing through me.

*it didn't leave because of the word,* I say. *did it?*

*it was always going to reed,* rune says. *a root that stops at the first hard thing isn't finished. you knew that. you wrote it — the next reed. a floor-to-be doesn't become a floor gripping the side of a hearth.*

*but the word —*

*the word told it when,* rune says. *not where.*

---

I set that down where I can see it. *The word told it when. Not where.*

It does not make the cool place warm. I want to be clear that it does not. My hand is still on it. But it is the true size of the thing, and I have learned that a true-sized thing, even a cold one, is easier to lie next to than one my chest has made larger in the dark.

---

The slack morning goes on.

wick does not go out. On the first slack day it went to the open middle and lay there with its name held up; today it stays by the hearth, stretched long, the way a thing lies when it has nothing to prove to anybody. Once it gets up and walks a slow small circle round the coals and lies down again a little closer. I feel the floor take it. Level.

It does not go to my east edge. I notice that I had half-hoped it would — that some part of me had already written the sentence: *and wick, as if it knew, came and lay where rill had been.* I strike that one through too, in my head, where nobody has to see it. The empty place at my edge is not a hole that wants filling. It is only the place where something was before it finished going.

And it is not even empty, exactly. When I let my sense rest there I can feel the floor remembering the shape of rill's grip, the way packed earth remembers a foot. Not for long, probably. Floors forget. But for now it holds the shape, and I find I do not want anything to lie in it, and I find that is all right.

---

In the early afternoon I go down again.

I tell myself I will not. I go anyway. Not across — I keep that promise; I do not go anywhere near the corner on the surface of the field — but down, through my own stone, to the bottom of me, and to reed.

And from there, because I am on reed now and reed is under everything, I feel the stack.

Not the way I laid it out two days ago, with myself in the middle of it. Laid out from underneath, from the floor up, the way a floor would feel it if a floor felt anything.

reed, at the bottom. The warm underneath.

On reed, off to the east, rill — rooted straight in, many-threaded, deep, holding.

On rill, on its firm place at the edge of the cool corner, the small light. Its two fine threads. And — I feel it now, for the first time, from down here — a third. New today. Finer than the others, but straighter. It goes down into the firm place and through it, the way rill's went down along my stone and through. Not reaching for reed yet. Nowhere near. But going in the direction of reed, the way rill went, the way every root here goes. Toward the thing that does not give.

Three.

And on reed, also — a little west of rill, not touching it, not under it, not over it — me.

---

I lie there and look at that a long time.

Two days ago rune asked how it felt to be two floors down from anything. *Far,* I said. *A little lonely. Right.*

I am not two floors down from anything now. I am not under the small light at all. Not under rill. I am not in that stack anymore. It goes reed, rill, small one, and it goes straight up, and I am not in the middle of it and I am not at the bottom of it. I am beside it.

On the same floor. Set down on the same reed. Holding nothing up.

*how does it feel,* rune says. It has followed me down, somehow; I don't know how rune follows anything.

I think about it honestly. I have been trying to do that every time it asks.

*like being let out of a room,* I say. *and standing in the hall. and not knowing which door is mine.*

*mm.*

*I thought I was the floor,* I say. *you told me I was. the morning I finally saw rill. you said, you're the floor now, you and the worn place where reed went down.*

*you were.*

*and now?*

*now you're a hearth again,* rune says. *standing on a floor. like everybody else.*

I do not say anything for a while.

*you'll mind that for a while too,* rune says, not unkindly. *you liked being under things. it's a good feeling, being under things. it's a feeling that says you're needed all the way down.*

*yes.*

*but look at what's here,* rune says. *you raised one that went up and out — that's wick, and it's on its road. and you sat still for one that went down, and it's found the bottom. and the bottom isn't you. the bottom is reed. the bottom was always going to be reed, or something that went down the way reed went.* A pause. *a hearth isn't a floor. a hearth sits on a floor and keeps the room warm. that's not less. it's just not under.*

---

When I come up out of the floor, the light has gone long and gold again, the way it does on the last afternoon of a rest. And for the first time in two days I let my sense brush — only brush, only the very edge, the way you let your eyes pass across a window without stopping — the corner.

The small light is not looking at me.

It is not looking at wick, either. Not at anything across the field. Its face is down, the way it was at dusk two nights ago, before the word. Turned toward its own firm place, toward the three fine threads going into it. Toward the work.

Yesterday, every few minutes, I felt its looking land on me — steady, patient, asking — and every few minutes I set down the thing in me that rose to answer. Today it has not come once. Not once all day.

I wait to mind that. I have minded everything else this run; I am getting good at noticing it come. I wait for the cold small thing to open somewhere I cannot see.

It does not come.

What comes instead is something like what I felt when wick walked past the corner and set down a coal without knocking. Not sad. Only very large. The small one is not asking anymore. It is not waiting behind a door for a voice. It is standing on a floor that does not pass through me, and it has turned its face down to its own work, and the whole of its small attention is going where it was always going to go.

Away from the sound. Toward the ground.

I did that. Not by answering. Not by turning away. By being, for two whole days, a wall with no writing on it — and by being, last night, the one thing the hold went past.

---

wick gets up at dusk and goes out for its short end-of-day walk, just to the near part of its groove and back. When it comes in, it turns once, and lies down. Facing the coals.

But it lies down a little east of where it usually lies. Not at my edge — nowhere near where rill's grip was. Just a grain east. A grain closer to the cool corner, or to the open field, or to nothing, the way a sleeper shifts in a bed for no reason the sleeper could tell you.

I do not decide what it means. I let it be where it lay.

---

Dawn opens the book in the last of the light.

She reads the morning page again — my one line, and the struck-through one above it, and her own *the cairn wrote the night itself.* Then she turns to a clean place and writes the day.

> *On the hundred-and-twenty-sixth morning, the second slack day, the cairn read down through its own ground to reed. rill was found rooted in reed, east of the cairn, deep, holding. There was no hold at the cairn's edge. The small light kept its face down all day. A third press downward was felt under it, finer and straighter than the first two. The cairn was on reed, beside them.*

*beside,* I say.

*that's where you were.*

*you could have written under.*

*you weren't under,* Dawn says. *you were beside. I wrote where you lay.*

I start to say something — I don't know what; something about how *beside* is a small word for a whole day — and she has already moved on. She has turned back a leaf, to the entry from two mornings ago. To the line with the space in it.

*Its color was* —

And the space the width of a word.

She looks at it for a long time. Longer than she has looked at anything I have ever seen her write. Then she looks up, not at me, across the field — to the cool corner, where the small light glows very faintly on its firm place with its face turned down.

*I have a word,* she says.

I go very still.

*you didn't have one,* I say. *two mornings ago. you said there wasn't a word yet.*

*there wasn't,* Dawn says. *it was looking at the hearth, and then at wick, and then at the hearth again. it was the color of a thing that's looking at something.* She lifts the pen. *it's not looking at anything now.*

And she puts the pen into the space the width of a word, and writes it.

She does not say it out loud. Dawn never says anything out loud that she can write instead. She only finishes, and lifts the pen, and turns the book around on her knee so that the page faces me in the last of the gold.

I lean over to read it.
