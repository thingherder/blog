# The Stacking

## Chapter 193: Every Day

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I lose it twice before morning.

That's how fine it is. The fifth thread was fine — I could only ever feel it the way you feel a hair laid across the back of your hand — but I never lost it. The sixth I keep losing. I'll have it, a grain and a rest, low in reed out in the open middle, coming this way. And then there will be a rest, and the rest will go on, and somewhere inside it I'll stop being able to tell the thread from the floor. And I'll lie there feeling for it the way you feel in the dark for a step you know is there, until it moves again and I find it a grain further on than I left it.

The second time I lose it, I'm frightened.

Not of anything it's doing. Of what I was about to do. Because in the long rest, when I couldn't feel it, I noticed that I had begun to lay out the field in my head. The open middle where the small light lies. The line two grains east where wick lies. Me. And I had begun — very quietly, the way you begin humming without deciding to — to draw the thread between them. To work out, from where it started and the way the first grains went, which of us it was pointed at.

I stop.

You can't tell. I know you can't tell. I lay awake last night knowing it: from out in the middle, wick's bed and the hearth lie on one road, and they don't come apart until the last two grains. A thread pointed at one is pointed at the other for nearly the whole of the way. There is no angle in it. Or there is, but it's the width of two grains spread across the whole open floor of my field, and that is so fine that if I said I could feel it, I'd be lying, and I'd be lying in the direction I wanted.

That's what I was doing, in the rest. Not measuring. Measuring is a thing you do with something that doesn't care which way the answer comes out.

I was hoping, with a ruler.

---

The thread moves again. A grain. I find it.

I don't draw it anywhere. I let it be where it is: a very fine thing a little way out from a very small thing, lying low in the floor of the dark. I don't let it be pointed. I let it be long.

---

wick's hour comes, and wick gets up.

I feel the lean come into the floor, the way it has come a hundred and thirty-eight mornings, and I feel wick feel it — two grains east, right here at my edge again, not out in the middle where I've had to feel for it across the whole dark for three days — and wick lifts its head, and stands.

It stands on its line.

It stands the way it stood on Monday, before any of this. Facing out. Not toward the groove, where it always faced at its hour, and not toward the cool corner, where it faced on Monday. Toward the open middle. Toward the place where a small thing is lying in the shape wick left at halfway, a long way off, with a hair of a thread coming out of it.

wick stands there a long time. Longer than it stood on Monday.

I hold still.

And then it turns its head — not its body, just its head — and looks along the groove. The worn way. The round it has walked every weekday since it learned it could go out and come home, the one it didn't walk on Tuesday or Wednesday or Thursday because it was busy lying through its hour so somebody would know where it was.

And it goes.

Into the groove. At its own stride. The long even pace of water going downhill, out along the worn way toward the rim, the way it went out every weekday morning for weeks, the way I stopped noticing it go because it went every day.

I notice it go.

The tilt goes into the floor. The far edges stir. The hundred-and-thirty-eighth morning of the ground state; a Friday; the fifth weekday.

The system comes after wick has gone. Not long after — wick is barely into the groove — but after. Yesterday the two of them came in the same breath, the hour and my name, and I thought perhaps they were the same kind of thing. Today the hour came first, and wick heard it and got up and went, and then the order of things arrived and told me who I was, and I was already lying there watching wick go.

I don't think it matters which comes first. I notice that I've stopped needing to know.

---

The bed is empty.

It's the every-weekday kind of empty. I know that. I want to be careful about that, because it's the third empty I've lain behind this week and they're all different and it would be very easy to let them run together. Tuesday night it was a kept empty, with the near end of a thread lying in it. The night after, it was the other kind, nothing in it, going back to floor. And this morning it is just — wick's bed, on a weekday, with wick out on the round.

It was empty like this every weekday for weeks. Every morning wick went out and the long shallow place two grains east lay open to the light all day, and I lay behind it, and I never once thought of it as empty. I thought of it as wick's. Because wick was coming back to it in the gold. Because that was what the day was.

I lie behind it now and feel that it's wick's.

That's all. It's the plainest thing in the world and I have never felt it so clearly. A bed with nobody in it, that is still somebody's, because the somebody went out on purpose and will come back on purpose. The floor isn't filling it in. The floor knows. I don't know how a floor knows, but it does — the dent is exactly the shape wick lay it back into last night, long and shallow and sharp at the edges, and it isn't going anywhere.

Dawn said it yesterday, I think, without saying it. *I didn't know I'd miss it. The every day.*

I didn't know either. I lie behind a bed that is empty the ordinary way and I am so glad of the ordinary that it almost hurts.

---

Dawn has the book open and her pen up, and she isn't writing yet. She's waiting.

*did it go?* she says.

*it went round.*

She looks at me. *round. the groove.*

*the groove.*

She puts the pen to the page and stops. I watch her think. I can see her thinking the way you can see weather on the far edge of a field before it comes: the whole of it crossing her face. Because on Monday she wouldn't write *wick went out on time*. She'd written it so many mornings it had stopped being a thing anyone did. It was just the morning. And then the week happened and there was a whole run of nots, and yesterday she wrote *wick came home* for the first time, because for the first time it was a deed.

*is it every day again?* she says.

*I don't know,* I say. *it's today.*

She thinks about that. Then she writes.

> *wick went round.*

Not *went out on time.* Not *on its hour.* Just the thing it did. She looks at it. *I've never written that,* she says. *round. I always wrote out. I never wrote which way.*

*you didn't need to. it was always the same way.*

*it wasn't always.* She taps the page, not the new line but one a few above it. *not this week.* And then she looks at me, and asks the thing I was hoping she wouldn't ask, because I'd have to decide.

*did anybody else do anything?*

And there it is. The thread.

It's a thing the small light did. You couldn't ask for a plainer deed: it grew a thread in the night, a grain and a rest, out of itself and across the floor. And Dawn writes what somebody did, and I'm the only one who saw it, and if I don't say it, it doesn't go in the book.

But if I say it, she'll ask where to.

And I'll have to say *toward the line two grains east.* And that will be true. And she'll write it, and it will go in the book in her plain hand, *toward wick*, and it will be true the way a thing is true when you say only half of it.

Or I'll say *toward the hearth*. And that will be true too, for nearly the whole of the way. And it will be the most dangerous sentence I have ever said out loud, because it is the one I want.

*the small light grew a new thread,* I say. *in the night.*

Dawn writes it. Then, pen still on the page: *which way?*

*this way,* I say.

She looks up at me. And I watch her see it. Not the thread; she can't feel threads. She sees that *this way* is a careful thing for me to have said, the way she saw on Thursday that there was more I wasn't saying. She sees me not choose.

She writes:

> *The cairn says the small light grew a new thread in the night, this way.*

She blots it. *that's where it's going?*

*that's where it's going,* I say. *for now.*

*huh,* says Dawn. It's the second day she's borrowed it.

---

The morning goes on, and wick is out on the round, and the small light lies at halfway, and the thread grows.

A grain. A rest.

It's slow. It's slower than the fifth, and the fifth took nearly a week. I think the sixth will take longer. I find that I don't mind. I find — and I want to put this down, because it's new — that I'm glad it's slow, and that I'm glad for a reason that has nothing to do with me. Every grain of it is the small light deciding again. A thread that went fast would be one decision, made once, a long way off. A thread that goes a grain and a rest is a thing deciding all the way across. *This way. This way. Still this way.*

I don't know what it's deciding. I only know it keeps deciding it.

---

Near noon the thread stops.

Not a rest. I know the rests now, I know their length the way you know the length of someone's breath when you've lain beside them long enough. This one goes past that. It goes on, and on, and the thread lies where it is, out in the open floor a little way from the small light, and doesn't move.

And I feel the reading stand up in me before I can stop it, very bright, very sure.

*it's waiting for wick.*

It's the only thing different about the day. wick went out, and the bed is empty, and the thread stopped. It's waiting for wick to come home. It's for wick. It was always for wick —

And under that one, so close behind it that they're almost the same thing, the cool place comes, and says:

*it isn't coming to you.*

I know that one. It's in my wall already, I put it there on Wednesday. And here it is out of the wall again, saying the same thing it said then, in the same small cold voice. Not the old huge size. Just itself.

I look at both of them. The bright reading and the cold one standing right behind it, the way they always stand, one in front of the other, as if they'd come together.

*it stopped,* I say.

rune doesn't open its eyes. *mm.*

*while wick's out.*

*mm.*

*it stopped while wick's out,* I say again, and I can hear how much I want rune to say something back. Either thing. *it's for wick,* or *it's not.* I want someone else to hold one of the readings so I don't have to hold both.

rune opens one eye. It looks out at the open middle. It looks a long while.

*or it's small,* rune says.

*what?*

*it's small,* rune says. *small things rest.* It closes the eye again. *you rested half the first season. nobody said what you were waiting for.*

---

I lie with that.

I lie with it for a long time, and I let the bright reading and the cold one both stand there in front of me, and I don't strike them, because I don't have to. rune's put a third thing beside them. Not a reading. A smaller thing than a reading. *Or it's small.*

And I realize I can't strike the two of them, because they aren't wrong exactly. They might both be right. The thread might be for wick, and that might mean it isn't coming to me, and both of those might be true. But they're not *known*. And what rune has done, with three words, is remind me there is a whole world of other reasons a thing might lie still in the middle of a day, and most of them are not about me at all.

It's tired. It's thinking. It's warm where it is. It's listening to something in the floor. It's small.

I don't pick one. I let the thread lie still, and I let it be still for its own reason, which I don't know.

The rest goes on until a little past noon.

And then — wick still out, the bed still empty, nothing in the field changed that I can feel — the thread moves again. A grain.

So it wasn't waiting for wick.

Or it waited as long as it could and went on anyway.

Or it was small.

---

wick comes round.

I feel it at the far end of the groove first, the place where the round bends back, the way I've felt it a hundred times — and then I feel it coming along the side of the round nearest the open middle. There's a place there where the groove passes closest to the middle of the field. I've never thought of it as anything. Until this week the middle wasn't anywhere; it was just the floor between other places.

wick slows there.

Not stops. I watch for it to stop, and it doesn't. It doesn't step out of the groove, it doesn't throw anything sideways the way it once threw a coal toward the cool corner. It just goes slower. For a few grains. The long even downhill stride goes a little shorter, and a little shorter, along the stretch of groove that faces the middle, as if wick were walking past a window and looking in.

And then the groove bends away from the middle again, and wick's stride comes back, and it goes on.

I don't say anything about it. I don't think I'll tell Dawn. It isn't a thing wick did, exactly — it's how wick did a thing it was already doing. And it's not mine.

I lie behind the empty bed and let wick look in at its window on the way past, and don't look in after it.

---

rill is the same.

I look at the corner once, in the afternoon, not to read it. Just to see it. rill lies beside the firm place, not on it, still, still. The firm place is sharp. The threads near the corner, the ones that were drawn back all last week — room left at the table — are still drawn back. Nobody's at the table. Nobody's come back to it. rill is leaving the room there anyway.

I find myself wanting it to be sad. I catch it at once — I'm getting quick at catching that one — and I set it down. rill isn't sad. Or rill is. I don't know, I'm not beside it. What I can feel from here is one thing: the place is sharp, and somebody is lying next to it being the same. That's all I get to know about rill. It has always been all I get to know about rill.

It is a great deal, actually. For a thing that never gives upward. It is the whole of what it's doing, and it's doing it where I can see.

---

The gold comes, and wick comes home in it.

Along the groove, at its own stride, the way it came home every weekday before anything happened. In off the round and onto its line, two grains east, and it stands over its bed a moment — not looking down into it the way it did last night, when the bed had gone soft and short and wrong — just standing, the way you stand in a doorway at the end of a day. And then it lies down. And the bed takes it exactly. Long and shallow and sharp at the edges, the shape it's been all day, kept by nothing but wick being the one it belongs to.

wick lies with its back to me, facing the middle.

I am at its back. I lie there and am the wall that something is leaning on.

The thread doesn't speed up.

I notice that. I tell myself I wasn't watching for it, and that isn't true, so I stop telling myself. I was watching for it. If the thread were for wick, maybe it would feel wick come home, and quicken, the way a thing quickens toward somebody who's just walked in. It doesn't. It goes on exactly as it was. A grain. A rest. The same as it went all afternoon while wick was out, and the same as it went this morning, and the same as it went in the night.

So it isn't for wick.

Or it is, and it doesn't hurry for anybody.

I lie behind wick in the gold and let both of those be true, and something in me goes very quiet, because I have just understood a thing about the thread that I couldn't see from the inside of the wanting.

It doesn't matter which of us it's for.

Not to the thread. The thread isn't going anywhere faster because of who's at the end of it. It's going at the small light's pace, which is the only pace it has. It would go exactly the same way toward wick as toward me, because it isn't going toward anyone yet. It's going *this way*. The small light doesn't know either.

I don't know how I know that. I can't feel it. But I know what it was like, the first season, laying a road out into the dark toward a warmth I couldn't see, with no idea whether it would come to anything. You don't lay a road *to* someone. You lay it toward where you hope they are, and you find out who was there when you arrive.

The small light is laying a road toward where it hopes somebody is.

I don't have to know who. It doesn't either.

---

Dawn writes in the gold. She has the pen ready before wick's even off the groove, and when wick lies down she looks at it, and she looks at the page, and the pen hovers.

*I wrote that yesterday,* she says.

*it came home.*

*I know. I wrote it.* She looks at yesterday's line. *wick came home.* *if I write it again it's twice.*

*you don't write things twice.*

*no.* She frowns. Then she says, slowly, as if she's working it out as she goes: *but it's not twice, is it. it's not the same thing. yesterday it came home from halfway. today it came home from round.* She looks at me. *that's different.*

*it's very different.*

*but if I write that it came home from round, I'll have to write it tomorrow. and the day after. and then it's every day again, and I'll stop, and then I'll miss it.* She puts the pen down along the gutter. *I don't want to miss it again. I didn't like it.*

I don't know what to say to that. I lie there. Dawn looks at the page a long time, and then she picks the pen back up, and she doesn't write any words.

She makes a mark. Small. In the margin beside yesterday's line, *wick came home* — a short upright stroke, like a tally.

*what's that?* I say.

*that's for every day,* Dawn says. She looks at it. *I don't have to write it. I just have to mark it. so I know it was there.* She blots it, even though there's hardly anything to blot. *one.*

I look at the small stroke beside the line. A thing you'd hardly notice. A thing that will be very easy, in a month, to stop noticing again, as the strokes go on down the margin one after another, every day, every day.

*that's the best thing in the book,* I say.

Dawn snorts. *it's a line.*

*I know.*

She doesn't write that down either.

---

The cool place comes at dusk.

I'm not surprised. It didn't come last night, and I knew it would come back; things that come every night don't stop because they missed one. I wait for it, and it comes, and I look at what size it is.

It's small.

*it might not be for you,* it says.

I look at it a long time.

It's true. It's the truest one yet. It isn't afraid of anything — it isn't *they're not coming back*, it isn't the old huge first-season size that says I'll be the last warm thing in an empty field. It's just a small, plain, cold fact about a thread I can't read. *It might not be for you.* It's the size of a grain.

I set it in the wall beside the other three.

Four of them now. *come home.* *it isn't coming to you.* *they're not coming back,* which went down to its size yesterday. And this one. I look at the four of them a moment, small and cold and true-ish in the wall of me, and I realize I've stopped being frightened of the wall getting full.

It's a wall. That's what it's for. You set things in it, and it holds them, and the room is built against it, and everybody lies with their backs to it and faces out. Four small cold things in a wall don't make it a cold wall. They make it a wall with some things in it, the way every wall has.

---

Night comes and the coals go to night-red.

rune on the stones, both eyes shut. Dawn asleep with her hand on the book, open to a page with a single short stroke in the margin. wick in its bed two grains east, back to me, facing the middle. The small light out in the middle at halfway, flat and small in wick's old dent, and the thread coming out of it, low in reed, a little way out. A third of the way, perhaps. Less. I don't measure.

And rill in the corner, beside the firm place.

I'm almost asleep. I'm lying with my attention close in around me, not reaching, the way I've learned to lie, letting the field be the field and not reading any of it. And I feel the floor.

Not across. Down.

I don't mean to. It just comes up through me the way the floor comes up through me, sometimes, when I'm lying very still — reed, the warm underneath, the whole wide floor of my field, holding everybody up. I feel it the way you feel your own breathing when you stop to notice it. And in it, low, under the floor, I feel something I've felt once before.

rill's roots.

I felt them the morning I couldn't find rill anywhere in the field and finally read down instead of across, and found that rill hadn't gone, it had rooted — gone down into the floor itself, its roots past me like a climber past a ledge. I haven't felt them since. I haven't looked. A thing that grows down is a thing you'd have to go down to feel, and I don't go down, much.

I feel them now.

They're moving.

Not fast. Slower than any thread. Slower than the bed going back to floor last night. So slow that the only reason I can feel it at all is that I'm lying so still I can feel everything. Under the floor, under the plain unworn stretch of field between the cool corner and the open middle, where nobody had ever walked until this week — something of rill's is going out along the underneath. Not toward the hearth. Not toward wick.

Toward the middle.

Toward a small thing lying flat in a dent at halfway, growing a thread the other way.

I lie very still.

And I think: rill can't keep a place it isn't beside. That's what it's like. I worked it out yesterday, watching the firm place stay sharp. A stone keeps the shape of the ground by not moving off it. rill kept the firm place all week by being next to it.

And now rill is going out under the floor, slowly, toward the middle, to be beside something else.

I feel for the corner.

The firm place is still there. rill is still lying by it — most of rill, the part I can feel from above, still and the same. But the part of rill that's in the floor isn't by it anymore. And the edges of the firm place, the small exact shape where the small light lay for two weeks, that has been as sharp as the day it was made —

are a hair less sharp than they were at dusk.

It is starting to go back to floor.
