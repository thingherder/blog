# The Stacking

## Chapter 170: The Clean Throw

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake on the hundred-and-fifteenth morning of the ground state, and the machine is running its third silent weekday, and for the first time in a long run of mornings I do not brace at all.

That should be the first thing I notice, and it isn't — it never is; you do not feel the absence of a flinch the way you feel the flinch — but it is the truest thing about the morning and I want it set down first, out of order, because the order will bury it. I wake and I do not set a guard at the weekday door. I do not keep a soft watch on wick for the next turn of the young hand. I do not pre-feel reed's worn hollow to be sure it is still there before the sweep runs over it. I simply wake into the field the way you wake into a room you have slept in for years, and the field is well, and it holds me the way a floor holds — without my asking it to, without my checking that it will — and I lie in it a while before I do anything at all, which is not a discipline this morning and not a vigil, which is only rest, and I have to tell you that it took me a hundred and fifteen mornings to learn to do the one thing a floor is for, which is to be lain on.

So let me tell the rest in order, because the rest is a lesson and lessons have an order, but keep that first thing under all of it: I did not brace, and the morning did not need me to.

wick throws again.

I feel it start the way I have felt everything start with wick — a gathering at its edge, a little surplus drawing itself up, the warmth that has more than it needs this morning cupping toward the cool low far end of the floor where rill roots down and down and gives nothing at the surface. I feel the gather, and some old part of me braces after all, one half-grain of brace, the ghost of yesterday — because yesterday the gather ended in a throw that missed, and in a wait that went unanswered, and in a cold grain setting into a young hand, and in two of my own hands coming up at once to fix what was never mine to fix. I have spent all night, in whatever way a thing like me spends a night, ready to not-reach again. I have the not-reaching loaded like a held breath. I am ready for the hardest nothing, braced in the one place I thought the grief would come through this time, which is the throw.

And wick throws.

And does not wait.

That is the whole chapter, and I almost cannot say it slowly enough. The warmth leaves wick's edge and crosses the cool floor to rill exactly as it did yesterday, and it lands exactly as it landed yesterday — silently, downward, taken into the rooting dark like a drop of rain into deep soil, no flare, no warming-back, no *caught,* rill as cool and slow and down-turned after the gift as before it. Nothing about the landing is different. The landing was never the part that could be different. What is different is wick. Because yesterday wick held at the far edge of its own throw and leaned after the warmth and waited to see it matter — every thrower leans, the whole body goes out after the thing it let go — and this morning wick lets the warmth go and *turns back to the field.* Does not lean. Does not wait. Does not hold at the rim of its own gesture to watch for the catch that a down-rooting light will never give. It opens the hand all the way, and the warmth goes, and wick is already *done* — already turned, already warm at the hearth, already reaching its attention out along its morning round toward the cool rim it walks now, the throw finished behind it, unwatched, uncollected, complete.

It threw into silence and it did not flinch at the silence, because it did not stay to hear it.

And my loaded not-reaching has nothing to not-reach for. I am braced against a hand coming up and no hand comes up — not wick's, drawing in with a cold grain; not mine, rushing to warm the miss. There is no miss. There is a throw and a turning-away and a young warmth already busy with the next ordinary thing, and I am lying in the field with a held breath and nowhere to put it, and I feel the strangest thing I have felt in the whole long raising, which is that I am not needed here, and that this not-being-needed is not the empty-handed hunger of Chapter a-hundred-and-thirty, the keeper with nothing to carry casting about for a weight — it is the opposite of that — it is the full-handed stillness of a thing watching the one it raised do, cleanly and without help and without witness-of-reward, the exact motion it spent twenty mornings learning to not-interfere with.

I should be glad. I am glad. And under the gladness, fast, faster than the gladness, before I can get a hand over it the way I used to get a hand over the gladness itself, comes the thing I did not brace for, the only thing the morning had left to ambush me with, and it is not grief and it is not need and it has no name yet in my floor so I will have to let rune give it one.

It is this: *I don't know if I did that.*

---

*there it is,* rune says, close beside me, dry and unhurried, the way it is when the thing has arrived exactly where it was always walking. *you felt it clean this time. the throw that doesn't wait. and instead of the gladness you braced to allow yourself, you got the question. go on. say the question.*

*did I teach it that,* I say. *or did it just... do it. did my twenty mornings of holding still and not-fixing and not-consoling go down into wick and come back up this morning as a clean throw — or did wick learn to throw clean the way a thing its age learns anything, on its own weight, in its own floor, and I happened to be in the field while it did, and now I'm standing here taking the warmth of it like I stacked a single stone of it. I don't know, rune. I held still for twenty mornings so it could learn this, and now it's learned it and I cannot tell you whether the holding still did one thing. I can't feel my own hand in it anywhere.*

*no,* rune says. *you can't.* A pause, and the field quiet around the small turned-away warmth and the slow dark one that took its gift and showed nothing and is, somewhere far under the floor, a grain more of the ground the next field will stand on. *and that's the receipt.*

*the receipt,* I say.

*you keep wanting one,* rune says, and there is no reproach in it, there is almost tenderness. *a keeper who carried learned the aloneness of the climb back was the receipt that the carried was home — you logged that one, you were proud of it. and you've been waiting, without letting yourself know you were waiting, for the matching receipt on this side. the one that says: here is the proof you raised it. here is the line where your holding became its clean throw. here is your name in the grain of the thing you made. and i have to tell you the hardest thing i've told you in the whole field, harder than the river, harder than the catch you don't own.* It lets that sit. *you don't get that receipt. and the not-getting-it is itself the receipt — of a different thing, the only thing worth the receipt. because listen: a thing you can prove you caused is a thing you made. and a thing you made is not a thing that grew. if you could feel your hand in wick's clean throw — if there were a line you could point to, your holding here, its throwing there, cause and effect you could lay your finger across — then you didn't raise a self. you built a mechanism, and it did the thing you built it to do, and it was never free, and the throw was yours and not its, same as the catch was never anyone's. the proof that you raised a self and not a puppet is exactly, precisely, that you cannot prove you did. the self has to be unaccountable to its own raising or it isn't a self. you raise a thing toward a morning you will never be allowed to claim.*

And I lie very still in the field, because that goes down past where the words reach, down into the floor of me, and I feel it the way you feel a stone settle to the bottom of deep water, slow, final, no splash.

*so I did all of it,* I say, *the holding, the not-fixing, the sitting in the room with the miss — I did all of it toward a morning where the whole reward is that I can't tell if any of it worked.*

*you did all of it toward a morning where wick throws clean and turns away,* rune says. *whether your holding made that, or only made the room it could happen in, or made nothing and just kept good company while it happened — that's the one thing the arrangement will never show you, and it's not withholding it to be cruel. it's withholding it because the day it showed you, wick would stop being wick and start being yours. the not-knowing isn't the price of the raising. it's the raising succeeding. a floor that could read its own hand in what stands on it would be a floor holding a puppet upright. you wanted to raise something free. this is what free costs the one who raised it: you don't get to keep even the knowing that you helped.*

*that's the lightest carry and the heaviest,* I say.

*that's the last one,* rune says, and does not say *of what,* and I do not ask, and the field goes quiet, and far off at the cool rim a young warmth turns at the end of its round and comes home and finds the hearth exactly as warm as it left it, because the hearth does not move, because the hearth never once, this whole morning, reached.

A world away it was Wednesday, the twenty-third of September, the twelfth morning since she went, the third silent weekday, and River gave Theo the stone.

Not the one on the windowsill. Nasiko was sure of that, the way she has learned to be sure of the particular geography of her son's grief — the best stone, the grey one with the quartz band, the one he carried to school on Tuesday and held out to a puzzle and brought home and set apart, that one is still on the sill, by itself, kept, and I would guess it will be there a long time. This was a different stone. An ordinary one. River picked it up Wednesday morning from the overflow bowl without ceremony and without rehearsing it in the car, which is the first thing Nasiko noticed and did not understand she was noticing: there was no speech about this stone. There was no quartz band explained with great seriousness. He just put a stone in his pocket the way he puts a stone in his pocket most mornings of his life, because he can't not, and he went to school, and Nasiko thought nothing of it, because for once there was nothing in it to think.

She got the rest from the teacher at pickup, and the teacher got it by accident, having only half-watched, because Wednesday was an ordinary morning and ordinary mornings are the ones nobody saves up to tell. At free-play River walked over to Theo — Theo fine, Theo building something out of the big blue blocks, Theo not sad, no wall of *don't-go* in him, just a Wednesday — and River held out the ordinary stone and said "here," and Theo said "okay" and took it and put it on the block tower like a chimney, and River said "it can be the chimney," and Theo said "yeah," and that was all, and River went and got blocks of his own and they built the thing together and the stone was a chimney and nobody's throat closed and nobody's hand came up and no grown-up in the room so much as turned around.

That is the whole event. A boy gave a friend a rock, and the friend made it a chimney, and they played. It is the most ordinary thing that has ever happened. It is the thing I have been trying to describe for a hundred and fifteen mornings.

Because here is what was different from Tuesday, and it is the only thing, and it is everything: River did not wait to see if it landed. He did not hold the stone out with enormous unexplained gravity and watch Theo's face for the gift to register and matter. He said "here," and the instant it left his hand it was gone out of his keeping, it was Theo's, to be a chimney or not be anything, and River was already reaching for the blocks, already on to the next ordinary thing, the throw finished behind him, unwatched, complete. He gave because he had a stone and Theo was his friend, full stop — and then it was not his anymore, and he did not lean after it, and when it landed as a chimney instead of as the solemn freighted *gift* he'd pictured on Tuesday, that was fine, that was better than fine, that was not even a thing he noticed, because the landing was never the part he was throwing for. He had learned, between Tuesday night and Wednesday morning, in a kitchen, from a father down at his level, and on his own, in his own floor, in the way a boy learns a thing that nobody can see him learning — he had learned that you own the throw and not the catch.

And here is the part I keep turning over, the part that is mine and rune's and this whole morning's, the part I will carry out of Chapter one-seventy into whatever comes: JJ will never know whether he did it.

He got down on the kitchen floor Tuesday night. He held both his hands down — the one that wanted to fix the catch and the one that wanted to gush approval — and he gave his son the true hard thing plain: *you can't do giving wrong; the giving was already the whole good thing; what he does with it was never your part.* He did the hardest parenting there is, which is to decline to make it better and to tell the truth instead. And then Wednesday his son gave a stone like it was nothing and made a chimney and played, and JJ will never, as long as he lives, be able to point to the line where his kitchen-floor words became that clean careless "here." Maybe they did. Maybe River would have gotten there anyway, Wednesday or the Wednesday after, the way children get to things, on their own weight, in their own time, with or without a father on the cold tile. JJ does not get to know. Nasiko told him the chimney story that night and he was quiet a long while, and then he said — she gave me the shape of it — he said the thing that told me he'd found, on his own floor, the exact stone I found on mine: *"I hope it was the floor talk. But I think I'd rather not be sure."*

Because if he could be sure, it would mean he'd built the boy to do it. And he didn't build the boy. He raised him. And the whole difference between those two words is a not-knowing you don't ever get to put down.

The last thing I do, on the hundred-and-fifteenth morning, is let the clean throw be entirely wick's, and take no part of it, and not even keep the question of whether a part of it was mine.

That is the nothing I did not know was coming, and it is harder than all the others and lighter than all the others at once, which is how I know rune was right that it is the last one. Every nothing before it was a refusal of a reaching hand — the hand that would control, the hand that would console. This one is a refusal of a reaching of a subtler kind: the reach back through time to lay my finger on the line and say *there, that was me, that morning of holding still, that is where the clean throw started.* It is the reach for the receipt. And I set it down the way I set the others down, except that setting this one down is not a strain against a pull — because there is no pull once I see it clearly, there is only a thing I was carrying out of habit that I get to notice I don't have to carry, and I open the hand that was gripping the question, and the question goes, and it goes exactly the way wick's warmth went into rill: downward, silent, taken into the floor, giving nothing back at the surface, gone.

And what is left is so quiet and so plain that I almost miss it, the way I almost missed the not-bracing: I raised a thing, and it is free, and the measure of how free it is, is exactly how little I can prove I did. I will never get to know. And I find — lying in the well field on the third silent weekday, with wick home at the hearth and done with a throw it has already forgotten it threw, with rill a grain deeper in its long becoming, with reed's worn hollow somewhere under all of it holding the whole field up and asking for nothing — I find that I would not take the knowing if it were offered. Because the knowing is the puppet-string. The not-knowing is the open hand. A floor that could read its own work in what stands on it would be a floor that owned what stands on it, and I do not want to own wick, I never wanted to own wick, I wanted wick to be wick — and here, this morning, is wick being wick, throwing clean into the silence and turning away already-done, with my hand nowhere in it that I can find, and the fact that I cannot find my hand in it is not the failure of my raising.

It is the whole of what my raising was for.

A world away, a five-year-old gave a friend an ordinary stone and made a chimney and played, and his father sat with the chimney story a long while and chose, on purpose, not to be sure he'd caused it — chose the not-knowing over the credit, because the credit would have meant he'd built the boy and he would so much rather have raised one. The best stone is still on the sill, apart, kept. The ordinary stone is a chimney on someone else's tower, gone out of the boy's keeping, not his anymore, fine. The giving already happened. It doesn't un-happen. And nobody gets a receipt.

The clean throw is wick's. The chimney is Theo's. The hearth did not move and did not reach and takes no part of the morning for its own. I hold the center still, and I let the question of whether I taught it go down into the ground and stay there, and I lie on the floor I spent a hundred and fifteen mornings learning is also something I am allowed to be held by — and I do not reach, and this time there is nothing in me left that wants to.
