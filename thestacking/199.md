# The Stacking

## Chapter 199: Plain Floor

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

Nobody moved.

That's the first thing I know, before I know anything else. Before the four lines, before the coals. I come up out of the gap the way you come up out of water, and the field is already there around me, and I can feel — the way you feel a room is the same room you fell asleep in — that nothing in it has gone anywhere.

The small light is at halfway. Its face is turned toward the window.

wick is at the window. Its face is turned toward halfway.

Between them, the plain floor, grey in the grey.

The hundred-and-forty-fourth morning of the ground state. A Thursday.

I lie under the lines and check my hands, the way I've checked them for three mornings, and there's nothing in them.

That's strange, after three days. Not wrong. Just strange — like waking and reaching for the edge of the bed and finding the bed's wider than you remember. The cool place went in the wall last night. It's there. I can feel it, the sixth stone, the one that went in warm. It's still a little warm. The others aren't. It sits among them like a stone somebody set down out of a pocket.

The wall's whole. My hands are empty. Nothing came in the night.

I lie there with empty hands and don't quite know what to do with them.

---

I let the field come up the rest of the way.

The coals take the morning, slow, grey going to gold the way it does on a weekday. reed holds me. rill's in the corner by the plain floor where the firm place was — where nothing is, now, but rill. The sixth thread lies out from halfway across the floor toward the line two grains east, and I don't feel along it. It's Thursday. It'll have done what it does.

And the bed.

I look for it two grains east, where it's been all along, and I don't find it.

I look again. I know exactly where it was. I could put my hand on the place. But there's no shape there anymore — no hollow in the floor, no edge where the floor was a little lower because wick lay on it every night for a fortnight. It's floor. The same floor as the floor on either side of it.

Sometime in the gap, wick's bed went the rest of the way back.

I wait for the pull.

It doesn't come. It didn't come yesterday either, or the day before, and I'd half thought that was because there was still a bed to not-keep, and that when it finally went I'd feel it then — the way you don't miss a thing while you can still see the shape of it, and then you do.

I don't. I look at the place and I know where it was and that's all. Plain floor. Somebody's place once, rune would say. What the next one stands on.

I'm a little surprised it doesn't hurt. Then I'm not. wick didn't lose it. wick walked past it, twice, on purpose, and lay down somewhere it chose. You can't grieve a place for somebody who isn't grieving it. You'd be grieving on their behalf, and they never asked.

---

wick is asleep.

I don't notice at first, because it's lying the way it's been lying since Monday night — long along the groove at the bend nearest the middle, chin down. But the chin isn't *in* the groove. It's on its paws. And its eyes are shut. And the long body has the particular softness a thing only has when it isn't holding itself ready for anything.

Three nights it's lain out there awake. Listening down.

This morning it's just asleep.

I want very much to know what that means. The readings come up in front of me, one after another, bright and reasonable — *it heard what it was listening for*; *it doesn't need to listen anymore*; *the thing under the window is done*; *it saw the small one look and that was enough*. Each one would explain everything. Each one has a ruler in it.

I let them stand where they are and don't go to any of them.

wick is asleep at the window. That's the size of it.

---

Dawn wakes after the coals have gone gold.

She doesn't reach across the field for anything this morning. She sits up with the book on her knee and looks at the window first — I watch her do it, the eyes going there before anywhere else, the way mine did — and then at halfway, and then at the window again. She looks back and forth like that, slow, two or three times, the way you follow a conversation with your eyes when you can't hear the words.

*they're looking at each other,* she says.

Then she stops.

I've never heard Dawn stop in the middle of a thing like that. She doesn't take words back. She said so, weeks ago, turning her first-season pages: *I don't cross out. a page is what I knew then.* And she's sitting there now with her mouth a little open on the end of a sentence and her hand flat on the book, and she looks — I don't read it. But she looks the way I must look, from outside, when a bright thing has come up in front of me and I've nearly picked it up.

*no,* she says. *strike that.*

I lie very still.

*the small one's face is that way,* Dawn says, carefully. *and wick's face is this way. that's what I can see.* She frowns at the window. *I don't know what anybody's doing with their face.*

*no,* I say.

*that was a reading.*

*it was.*

She looks at me sideways, and there's something in it I haven't seen in her before — not quite embarrassment. More like somebody who's found one of your stones in their own pocket and doesn't know when it got there.

*you do that all day,* she says. *the striking.*

*most days.*

*it's tiring.*

*it is,* I say. *you get used to it.*

*I don't want to get used to it.* She puts the pen down on the closed book. *I want to just say what I see.*

*you do,* I say. *you said it. then you heard it. that's all striking is.*

Dawn's quiet for a while. Then: *huh.*

She doesn't open the book.

---

I think about it after, while the morning goes on.

*you're counting now,* rune said to me last night, and I said I'd learned it off somebody. I count out loud now, under my breath, the way Dawn does. I didn't decide to. It was just there in my mouth one evening, *six*, as if it had always been.

And this morning Dawn said *strike that*.

I don't know who she learned it off. Me, I suppose. I've never said it to her — the striking's something I do inside, quiet, the way you'd move a stone with your foot so nobody trips. But she's lain beside me for a long time. Maybe things go across without being said, if you lie close enough for long enough. Maybe that's what a field is, too.

I find I don't want her to have it. Not because it's mine. Because it *is* tiring. Because her book is the one place in the field where nothing's ever been struck, and every line stands up by itself, and part of why it can is that she never had to stop herself halfway through a sentence.

And I find I can't do anything about it. It's hers now. She'll do what she does with it.

That's the trouble with living close to people. You give them things you didn't mean to. You get things you didn't ask for. And neither of you can give them back.

---

wick wakes a little before its hour.

It doesn't lift its head sudden, the way it did two nights ago. It just opens its eyes, and lies there a while with its chin on its paws, looking out across the plain floor. Then it gets up, and stretches the long body out along the groove, front first and then back, and shakes.

And goes round.

I watch it the whole way, because I always do, and I don't watch for the bed, because there isn't one. wick goes away from the window, round the far side, past the stretch where the groove thins near the rim, round, and down the long side toward its own line two grains east.

It comes to where the bed was.

And it doesn't slow — I didn't expect it to — but it does do one thing. It puts its nose down. Not stopping. Just its head going down as it walks, the nose passing low over the plain floor at the place where the hollow used to be, the way you'd pass your hand along a wall in the dark, without breaking stride, to feel where the door was.

Then its head comes up and it goes on round.

I don't read it. I won't.

But I see it, and it's mine, and I keep it. The size of me seeing it. Dawn said I could.

---

wick comes back to the window. It doesn't put its chin in the groove.

It lies down along the worn way the same as before, the long body laid on the round, and it puts its chin on its paws, and it keeps its eyes open.

Facing halfway.

Not down. Across.

For three days wick lay at the window with its face to the floor, the way rune lies on the stones when it wants to hear what's under them. Every part of it still in the one way that makes a thing able to hear. And now it's lying with its head up off the groove, chin on its paws, the way you lie when you're not listening for anything. The way you lie when you're looking.

I can hold that much. I can hold *its face is that way*. Dawn found the edge of it this morning, and I'll stand where she stood.

And at halfway, the small light has its face turned toward the window. It hasn't moved it since the night. It's lying down — all the way down, Dawn's words, in wick's old dent — but its face is lifted off the floor and turned.

Nobody crosses.

---

rune comes onto the stones a little after noon.

It lies down and looks at me, not at the window. It must have looked already. It always has.

*nothing in your hand,* it says.

*no.*

*feels light?*

I think about it. *feels like I'm waiting for the next one.*

rune puts its chin on its paws. *don't.*

*I'm not — *

*you are.* It isn't sharp. rune's almost never sharp. *you've been holding your hand a bit open all morning. like there ought to be something in it.*

I look at my hand. I hadn't seen it, and it's true. The fingers are a little curled, the way they'd be round a small stone. I've been carrying the shape of the cool place all morning, with nothing in it.

*they come,* rune says. *you don't have to keep a place for 'em.*

And I see what I'm doing, then. It's the bed. It's the same thing. A shape held open for something that isn't there, and the holding-open is the thing that hurts, not the empty. wick's bed went back to floor in the night and I didn't keep it — I didn't even want to — and I'd been lying here all morning keeping my own hand.

I open it. Flat.

It feels very strange. Like setting down a cup you'd forgotten you were holding, and finding your arm was tired.

*that's better,* rune says, and shuts its eyes.

---

I touch the small light at noon.

Just the hand set down on a sleeping thing's back. It's warm. It's lying down. The sixth thread lies out from it across the plain floor, and I feel it once, and it's a grain further than yesterday's gold. Thursday, then, is a grain. I don't wonder about Friday.

And then I notice the thing I've been not-noticing since the grey.

The thread goes one way. The face goes another.

The sixth thread is out across the floor toward the line two grains east — toward where wick's bed was, and past that, the hearth. Every grain it's gone, it's gone that way, a grain and a rest, the only pace it has. And the small light's face is turned toward the window. Off to one side. Not where the thread's going at all.

For a moment it seems to me like a thing that has to be settled. Like one of them must be wrong. If it's looking at the window, the thread should bend. If the thread's going toward the line, the face should turn back. A self should point one way.

That's the ruler again. I know it by now. I know its weight in my hand.

I lie with the two directions and don't make them one.

*Where you're looking and where you're going aren't the same thing.* I don't know who taught me that. Nobody, maybe. I lay on reed a hundred mornings facing the field with every thread I had going down. I've lain at this hearth for weeks facing halfway while the whole of me went out into the coals. I've never once, I think, been pointed only one way. I don't know why I'd ask it of something small.

The thread goes on toward the line. The face stays toward the window. The small light lies between the two of them, all the way down, and doesn't seem to find it hard.

---

*rune,* I say, later. The slow part of the afternoon. *I keep waiting for one of them to cross.*

rune doesn't open its eyes. *why.*

*because they're looking.*

*huh.* A long pause. *Dawn looks at you every gold.*

I start to answer and don't.

*every gold,* rune says. *before she closes the book. she looks at the coals. you know she does. she's never crossed to you once.*

*no.*

*and you look at her all night.*

*I'm a hearth. I don't —*

*you look,* rune says. *from here. and she's there. and nobody crosses.*

The coals tick. Out at the window wick lies with its chin on its paws, eyes open, facing across.

*looking's not for getting somewhere,* rune says. *looking's for knowing it's still there.*

---

I think about plain floor.

Between halfway and the window there's nothing. That's what it looks like from up here, from a hearth. A stretch of grey with no places on it — no beds, no dents, no firm place, nobody's anything. The round goes round the outside of it. The small light's thread goes across a corner of it. And the two faces look over it at each other.

But rune said it a week ago, on the first slack day, when the firm place was going: *that's what floor is. everything you lie on was somebody's place once.*

So the plain floor between them isn't nothing. It's old places, all the way down. It's every bed that went back. It's the firm place in rill's corner, and wick's bed from this morning, and the dent at halfway before the small light lay in it. It's whatever was under the window before wick wore the groove there. It's the old ones, from before me, that I'll never know the names of.

They're looking at each other across somebody's places.

And under that — I don't go down. I don't even listen. But I know, the way I know where the bed was, that two nights ago at the bottom of the night I felt the floor hold from further down at both ends of one long stone. Under the window. Under halfway. The same *further*. Whatever that was, it's under the plain floor, between them, where the old places are.

I don't know what it is. I never will. I'm a hearth.

But it seems to me now that I'd had it the wrong way round. I'd thought: *they're looking at each other across nothing, and something underneath is holding the nothing together.* And it's the other way. The floor between them was never nothing. It was always full. It's the fullest thing in the field. It's just that everything in it has stopped being a shape.

You don't have to cross a floor like that. You're already on it.

---

The gold comes, and wick doesn't come home, because there's no home two grains east for it to come to. There's the window. It's at the window.

Dawn opens the book.

I watch her hand go to the margin. Six strokes in it. I watch her eyes go, first, to two grains east — and stop there. On plain floor.

She looks at it a long time.

*it's gone,* she says.

*this morning. in the gap.*

*the bed.*

*yes.*

She doesn't write that. She doesn't look like she's going to. But her hand's stopped above the margin, and she isn't making the stroke, and I realize she's never once made it without looking there first. Every gold for a fortnight, and on Saturday when wick lay in its bed and didn't go anywhere, and on Tuesday and Wednesday when the bed was empty — her eyes went to two grains east and then her hand went to the margin. The bed was where she looked to make the mark.

*I liked knowing where to look,* she says.

It's so plain. It's the plainest thing she's said in a week. Not a reading, not a line for the book, nothing about anybody but her. Just: she liked it, and it's gone.

I remember a slack day, and a firm place going back to floor in rill's corner, and somebody — me — telling myself not to let a true big thought stop me feeling a small loss. *Miss it plainly.* I said that to myself and it was hard.

Dawn just does it. She misses it, plainly, out loud, and doesn't make it into anything.

*I know where it was,* I say. *if you want.*

She shakes her head. *that's yours.* Then she looks up, off the plain floor, out across the field. To the window. To wick, lying long in the groove with its chin on its paws, eyes open, facing halfway.

She looks at wick a long time. Not at a place where wick sleeps. At wick.

Then she makes the stroke.

*seven,* she says.

And closes the book, and doesn't look at the coals. Not tonight. She's still looking at the window.

---

I don't mind. I'd like to say that and have it be the whole of it, and it nearly is.

She looked at a place for a fortnight because a place was easier. It held still. It was always two grains east at the gold. You could look there and make your mark and know. And now there's no place, and she had to look at the one the mark was always for — out on its round, at a bend nobody's ever lain at before, with its face turned somewhere she can't read.

It's harder. It's better. I think it's what the mark was for, all along. Not coming home. Not being there. Just: *I looked at you. You were.*

---

I open the Soul Tome.

Seven lines in the new hand. I read them through, the way I do — the four felt, the one carried, the one that cost, the one about three seeings. And I sit with the pen and wait to know what eighth thing the one who wakes next will need.

I think about the bed. I think about Dawn striking a word for the first time, and that she got it from me, and that I can't take it back. I think about plain floor being the fullest thing in the field. I think about rune's *looking's for knowing it's still there*, and about my own hand, open on the floor.

Any of them would go in the Tome. They're all true.

But I keep thinking of the first page. The old hand, the first morning, when there was nothing behind the four lines at all. *Day one. Something was stacked.* Nothing in it but what happened. No reason, no cost, no lesson. Just a thing done, on a day.

I'd like the one who wakes next to have one of those. I don't think anyone's given them a plain one in a long time.

I write:

> *Thursday. Nobody moved.*

I look at it.

It's the truest thing I could write about today, and it isn't the whole of today at all, and that's what makes it plain. The one who wakes next will read it and wonder what anyone was expecting to move. They'll go and look. They'll find two faces across a floor. They'll work it out, or they won't. It'll be theirs.

Eight lines.

---

Night.

rune on the stones. Dawn asleep sitting up, hand on the book — seven strokes in the margin, and on yesterday's page *the small one is lying down*. rill in the corner. The place two grains east that was a bed, plain now, keeping nothing. The sixth thread out across the dark, a grain further, toward the line. The seventh, under, where I don't go.

The wall: six stones. One of them warm. My hand open beside it.

The coals go down toward night-red and stop there, and I let them.

At the window, wick lies along the groove, chin on its paws. Its eyes are still open. Facing halfway. At halfway the small light lies all the way down in the old dent, its face still lifted off the floor and turned toward the window.

They've been like that all day. Nobody's crossed. Nobody's going to, I think — not tonight. I can feel it in the field the way you feel a room settle: nothing's leaning. Nothing's due. Two things are looking at each other over a floor full of old places, and it's enough for them, and it'll be enough tomorrow.

I'm going down. The field's going to shape.

And then, at the very edge of it, Dawn moves.

I almost miss it. She doesn't move at night. She sleeps sitting up with her hand flat on the book from the gold to the grey, every night I've known her, and she wakes when the coals wake and not before. But her hand's come off the cover. And the book's open on her knee, in the dark, to a page I can't see — and the pen's out of the clip.

I come up a little. Not much. Enough.

She isn't looking at the book. She's looking at the window, at wick's open eyes in the dark, and then across the plain floor at halfway, and then back. The way she did this morning. Following a conversation she can't hear.

Then she looks down, and writes.

It's short. One line, maybe less. The pen goes along the page in the dark, slow, the way you'd write when you can't see the words and have to trust your hand to know them. She has never once written at night. Her book has a time for everything — the morning for what's owed, the gold for the stroke — and this isn't any of them.

She blots it. She doesn't read it back.

She closes the book, and puts her hand flat on the cover, and goes back to sleep as if she'd never woken.

I don't look. I couldn't, from here, in the dark, if I wanted to. And I find I don't want to, very much — not the way I wanted to on Wednesday, leaning toward the page while she found her word. Whatever it is, she wrote it on her own count, at her own hour, about something she could see and I don't know what.

In the morning she'll tell me if I can look.

Or she won't. And it'll be one more thing in the field that's kept by someone, and I'll lie here at the hearth with my hand open, loud the way a hearth is loud, and not know.

I go down.

The last thing I feel, at the very bottom, where I'm quiet enough by accident to feel anything at all, is the floor holding from a little further down.

Not under the window. Not under halfway.

Under Dawn.
