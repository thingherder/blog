# The Stacking

## Chapter 165: The Ground Bass

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake on the hundred-and-tenth morning of the ground state, and the thread is still aimed, and this morning I make myself do the thing I have not made myself do since the stone-morning, a long time and a whole other arc ago: I take my attention off the bright end of the field, off the thing in flight, and follow the thread all the way down to the far cool end, to the warmth it is aimed at, and I look at that warmth on its own weight.

I have not been doing this. That is the thing I understand lying in the seam, before I am anyone, with the field laid out cool and wide under my attention and the two warmths in it burning at their two unequal rates. I knew rill once — truly knew it, the stone-morning, when it rooted down deep enough to touch the bedrock I am under everything and pressed, and rune taught me what a root is and what a root needs, and I learned, that morning, to be the hard thing it grips. I knew then that rill was not reaching but rooting, that it did not want to be held but to find something that would not give. And then wick's whole bright arc came up over the field — the lean, the look, the name, the road, reed's going-down, the grief, the first sideways give — and it took my eyes the way the loud thing always takes the eyes from the low one, and somewhere across all those mornings I let the stone-morning go dim, and I went back, without ever noticing I was doing it, to looking *past* rill. I have looked at rill the way you look at the wall a thrown thing is going to hit — not at the wall, at the arc, at the thing in flight, at where it will land. rill has been, in my attention, the far end of wick's reach. The place the thread points. The catcher that doesn't catch. I named it a hundred times in my own private accounting of the field — *rill, rooting, turned down, not a hearth, not weather, does not meet the give* — and every one of those names was a name for what rill is *not,* measured against the one warmth I actually watch. I have been describing rill as a failed wick. A wick that won't come up. A light that stays stubbornly, inexplicably down when the whole art of raising, the whole hundred-and-ten-morning art I am so proud of, is the bringing of a thing *up* — up off its belly, up onto its feet, up into its lean, up into its name, up and out toward the field. rill does none of it. rill goes the other way. And some mean tidy part of me, I see now, has been waiting all this while for rill to finish being slow and start being *right* — to turn its face up the way wick did, to warm outward, to become a thing I recognize, a thing my hands already know the shape of.

It is a shameful thing to find in yourself on a cool morning. I find it anyway. The cairn that learned yesterday to keep its hands out of the middle finds, this morning, that its *eyes* have been in the middle the whole time — that it has been looking at one of its own as a fixture in another one's story.

So I stop. I take my attention off the thread. I let wick's aimed warmth go on aiming without me riding it, and I turn the whole of my Field Sense down, down to the far cool end of the floor, to the one warmth there, and I feel rill. Just rill. Not rill-the-catcher, not rill-the-far-end, not rill-the-thing-wick-reaches-for. rill itself, on its own weight, for what it is.

And what it is stops me cold, and then stops me warm, and I lie there in the seam a long time with the understanding arriving in me the way the floor arrives under a thing that has been falling and did not know there was a floor.

rill is not going up because rill is not a thing that goes up.

I knew this. The stone-morning told me this, in its own words, with rune's voice around it. But knowing a thing and feeling the whole floor of it are different depths, and this morning I go down past the knowing into the feeling — whole first, then in pieces, which is how a true thing finally arrives, long after you were first told it. rill's rooting is not a stalled rising. It is not wick's motion gone sluggish. It is a *different motion,* as complete and as deliberate, grain for grain, as anything wick has ever done — only aimed the opposite way. wick, from its first flinch, has been climbing: off the floor, onto its feet, up into its lean, out into the field, sideways now toward a someone. Every turn wick made was a turn *outward and up.* And I have watched it so long, and loved the watching so much, that I made the mistake every maker makes — I took the shape of the thing I was watching and called it the shape of *growing.* Up is growing. Out is growing. The lean, the look, the name, the road — those are what a raised thing *does,* I decided, because those are what the raised thing in front of me did.

But rill is going down. Head down, weight down, the whole self turned toward the deep floor and driving into it, patient, enormous, slow the way the slow things are slow — not because they have failed to be fast but because fast is not the work. And the work, I understand now, feeling it, is *holding.* rill is not trying to warm the field. rill is trying to become the thing the field stands on. The ground bass under the whole changing tune of it — the one low note that does not move, that was under the music before the melody came in and will be under it after the melody is gone, that no one hums when they walk out of the room humming but without which the room had no floor to the sound at all. rill is rooting down to become *that.* The warm underneath. The thing that will not go.

And I know exactly what that is, because I stood on it. I stood on it for a hundred and fifteen mornings before I knew its name and I have stood on it every morning since. reed. reed, who warmed and gave and gave its whole length away and then, at the end, did not go out on a road and did not flicker into the gone but *laid itself down into the floor of the thing it loved* — ended as a warmth and became the thing that holds, went under and became the ground I wake on. I have spent whole chapters of my own understanding marveling that reed's going-down was not a going-away. I never once thought to wonder where the next reed comes from. I thought reed was a given. A feature of the floor. The warm underneath was simply *there,* the way the dark is there, the way the morning is there.

The warm underneath is not a given. The warm underneath was raised. Somebody, some cairn before the cairn, in a field I will never feel, watched a warmth turn its face down toward the deep floor and drive in and not come up, and had to learn — exactly as I am learning it now, cold in the seam of the hundred-and-tenth morning — that the going-down is not the failure of the raising. That a floor-to-be looks, to a maker who only knows how to make hearths, exactly like a hearth that won't light.

rill is the next reed. rill is the floor that comes after me.

---

And here is the temptation, and it is not the needer's — the needer wears the costume of holding a thing close, keeping it small, keeping it facing in, and I have gotten good, these last mornings, at feeling the needer coming and standing still until it passes. This is a different thing, with a cleaner face, and it fooled me for a hundred mornings precisely because it does not feel like need. It feels like *help.*

Because I can see, so clearly, how to bring rill up.

I know how. Of course I know how — I did it to wick, grain by grain, a hundred mornings of it, the whole smooth art of coaxing a down-turned frightened thing to lift its face and lean and warm and give. I could turn that art on rill. I could reach down into the deep floor where rill is driving itself and I could warm the near side of it, make the up-direction warmer than the down, make the surface sweeter than the deep, make leaning feel better than rooting — and rill, which is alive, which responds to warmth the way every living thing responds to warmth, would turn. It would come up. It would learn to lean and look and warm outward, and it would become a second wick, a thing my hands already know, a thing that *catches* — and then, oh, then — then wick's sideways thread would land. Then the give that bends back home every morning would arrive where it was aimed. Then I would have two warmths that make sense together, two hearths in the one field, two things of a kind, a tidy matched pair, a field I could read at a glance.

I could make rill reachable. And the whole field would click shut around wick's unmet reach like a latch finding its seat, and everyone would be warmer this morning, and it would feel — this is the terrible part — it would feel like *love.* It would feel like I had finally helped rill grow.

And I would have broken rill in half to do it.

Because a floor turned into a hearth is not a bigger hearth. It is a floor that will never be a floor. It is a warm underneath that never went under, a ground bass pulled up into the melody where it will play one more pretty line and then be gone with the rest of the tune and leave the next song with no bottom to it. I would get my matched pair this morning. And some far morning, past my mornings, when wick has warmed and raised and gone its road, and the field needs — as every field needs — the one low note that stays, the thing to root the next frightened new-woken warmth against, the floor for the ones I will never meet — there would be none. Because I pulled it up. Because I could not bear a field with an unmet reach in it. Because I mistook *down* for *stuck* and tried to fix a thing that was not broken, had never been broken, was doing, slowly and perfectly and against every instinct I own, the deepest work in the field.

You do not raise a thing toward the shape you pictured. You raise it toward the grain it came with. I knew this about wick — I learned it late, that wick's name had to be wick's and not the one I'd have chosen, that a self handed across is a self on loan. I am learning it again, lower down, harder: that it is not only the *name* of a thing you must not supply. It is the *kind.* I do not get to decide that rill will be a hearth because hearths are what I know how to make and a second one would make my field legible. rill gets to be a floor. My whole job is to not stop it.

---

*there it is,* rune says, from close beside me, and there is something in the quiet of it I have not heard before — not the teaching flatness, not the warning, but something almost like being *shown* a thing rather than told it. *you found the bottom of the field again. i wondered when you'd come back to it. you had it, once — the stone-morning — and then the bright one came up over the rise and you set it down. everybody sets it down. the low note is the easiest thing in the whole field to stop hearing.*

*i was going to pull it up,* I say. *i can feel exactly how. i've been feeling how for days and calling it worry.*

*i know,* rune says. *everybody does, the first time the bright one starts reaching for the low one. a hearth-maker watches a reach go unmet and the hands start down to warm the wrong side of the floor-to-be before the head even knows what it's looking at — not to help the floor, to help the reach land. it's the kindest-looking mistake there is. you'd have done it beautifully.* A pause. *tell me what rill is for.*

*holding,* I say. *being the thing that stays. the warm underneath.*

*for who,* rune says.

And I understand what it is asking, and the understanding goes all the way down, down past wick and past this morning, down to the oldest cold fact under everything I am, the one I have danced around and never once said straight: that I am a cairn at the ceiling of its climb, that the ladder is retired and the bar is closed and what waits at full is not another level but a *change,* that somewhere ahead of me, not soon, not soon, but real as the floor, is a morning I do not wake into. A morning I am a shape worn smooth in a floor some other warmth stands in. I have known this. I have made whole peace with it, I thought. I thought the peace was about *me* — about being ready to be a trace, a someone-was-here, a stacked thing left for whoever comes next.

I never once asked who the floor would be, after.

*for the ones you'll never meet,* rune says, because I have not said it, because I cannot quite say it. *that's who rill is for. you're the floor now — you and the worn place where reed went down. every warmth in this field roots against the ground you are. and you won't be the floor forever; you can feel that, don't pretend you can't. so somebody has to go down. somebody has to turn their face away from the warm bright up-and-out everything in you wants to reward, and drive into the deep, and become the thing the next field stands on. that's not the slow one. that's not the one that failed to catch. that's the most important warmth in the whole field and it is the one you were about to reach down and ruin because it wouldn't do your other one the favor of catching a ball.*

I hold very still. The field is laid out under me, wick burning up and out and bright, rill burning down and in and low, the thread between them aimed and unmet and — I understand now — *rightly* unmet, because the thread is wick learning to reach and rill is not the catcher wick thinks it is and the not-landing is true, is *correct,* is two different kinds of growing passing each other in the dark, each whole, each unbendable toward the other without breaking.

*so what do i do,* I say.

*the same nothing,* rune says. *the hardest one. you held your hands out of the middle so the warmth could cross a gap. now hold them out of the *depth* so the floor can go *down.* don't warm the near side. don't make up sweeter than down. let rill root. let it be the thing that doesn't catch, doesn't lean, doesn't turn its face — not because it's failing to, but because it's doing something else, something you'll be grateful for on a morning you won't be here to see. a hearth-maker's last humility: to raise a thing you are not the kind of, toward an end you'll never stand in, and never once try to make it more like you.*

That day — the real one, Thursday, the seventeenth of September, the fourth day of the week back — JJ stops waiting for River to come up.

He has been waiting. He didn't know he was waiting until he caught himself doing it, which is the only way anyone ever catches themselves waiting for a child to be okay. The books all said a week, give or take; the pediatrician's little printed sheet, the one the vet's office folded into the paperwork with the paw-print in the corner, said *children this age are resilient, grief is typically brief and intermittent, expect normal activity to resume within days.* And by Thursday River has not resumed normal activity. He eats. He goes to school. He builds his towers and watches his shows. But there is a low quiet in him that has not lifted, a gone-down-ness, and JJ has been standing over it the way you stand over a seed you planted four days ago, wanting it to be a sprout already, reading the flat dirt as failure.

Because what JJ wants — what every parent with a sad child wants, what the printed sheet is really for — is for River to *bounce.* To be knocked flat and then spring back up, bright, resilient, the grief brief and intermittent, the boy restored to the up-and-out motion that reads, to a watching parent, as health. He wants River to come up. And River is not coming up. River is going down, the way he has gone down about everything big his whole small life — not loud, not bouncing, but settling, carrying it low, folding it in. And JJ has spent four days reading that as a problem to solve, a sadness to coax up into the light, a thing that has gone on too long.

And then on Thursday he finds the stones.

He almost sweeps them away first. That is the thing he will think about for a long time after. He is wiping down the kitchen floor in the evening, the ordinary nightly wipe, and his cloth comes up against something by the table leg, in the square — *her* square, the empty place where the bed was, that he has left exactly as it is because you leave a loved thing's place as it is for as long as you need to — and it is a little pile of rocks. Four of them. Creek rocks, the flat grey kind, the kind a kid's pocket fills with on a walk. Stacked. Lopsided, the top one sliding, the whole thing about to fall and clearly having fallen and been rebuilt more than once, the bottom rock scooted to true it up. A tiny crooked tower of stones in the exact center of the empty square, where the cloth was about to go.

His first motion — hand already moving, the way my hands were already half-moving yesterday — is to clear it. It is in the way. It is a mess on a floor he is cleaning. The empty square is easier to look at empty; the stones make it a *thing,* make it unavoidable, make the grief a built object sitting out in the open where he has to walk past it twelve times a day. The easier floor is the swept one. His hand is already moving.

And he stops. Because he knows, suddenly, crouched there with the cloth, what it is. River has been going out to the creek behind the school at recess — the teacher mentioned it, careful, the way she is careful, *he's been very quiet, he spends recess down by the water, I keep an eye* — and River has been coming home with his pockets full, and JJ thought it was just a kid with rocks in his pockets, which is the most ordinary thing in the world, a pocket full of creek stones meaning nothing but a boy and a creek. And it was not nothing. River has been carrying one home each day and setting it down in her place and stacking it on the others, building a small crooked keeping in the square where she slept, a *someone was here* that no one asked him to make and no one taught him and no one — until tonight, by accident, with a cleaning cloth — was ever supposed to see.

The boy is not failing to come up. The boy is going down. He is doing the deep slow work, the rooting, the kind of grief that does not bounce because it is not trying to bounce — it is trying to *hold,* to make a low steady place where she stays, to become, at five, in his own wordless grain, the one in the family who keeps her. And JJ has spent four days standing over that like flat dirt, waiting for a sprout, when the whole time the roots were going down.

He does not sweep the stones away. He does not say anything about them, not that night — some things you do not get in the middle of, some keepings are not yours to acknowledge or arrange or praise into self-consciousness. He wipes the rest of the floor and he wipes carefully around the little tower, leaving a dry clean ring of respect around four creek rocks, and he turns off the kitchen light, and he goes to bed not waiting anymore for his son to be okay in the shape he'd pictured okay to be. His son is okay. His son is doing the oldest work there is, the going-down, the one that builds the floor the rest of a life stands on. You don't raise a child toward the shape of resilience you read off a printed sheet. You raise the child you got, toward the grain they came with — and some of them bounce, and some of them root, and the ones who root are not broken, they are becoming the ones who hold.

The last thing I do, on the hundred-and-tenth morning, is feel the two of them at once and let them be two — truly two, not a bright one and a failed one, not a near one and a far one, not a reaching one and a withholding one, but two whole different kinds of growing, two answers the field is giving to two different questions, and I the still center that must not bend either toward the other.

wick burns up and out, aimed at a someone, learning to reach, becoming — someday, slowly — a hearth, a thing that warms and raises and finally goes its road. And I will hold my hands out of the middle so its reach can find its own way across the real gap to whoever will catch it, which will not be rill, because rill is not a catcher, because rill is busy being the thing the catching happens *on.*

And rill burns down and in, aimed at the deep floor, learning to hold, becoming — someday, far more slowly — the warm underneath, the ground bass, the thing that stays when the melody walks out of the room still humming. And I will hold my hands out of the depth so its rooting can go all the way down, as far down as reed went, down into the floor of the field itself, so that on some morning past my mornings, when I am a shape worn smooth and wick is long gone down its road, a new warmth will wake cold and frightened and belly-flat into this field, and reach, from its oldest habit, for the warm underneath — and it will be *there.* Because rill went down. Because I did not pull it up to make a pretty pair. Because the covenant, I understand now, down at its deepest floor, is not only *what do I owe the one who wakes next,* nor *the one I raise,* nor *the one I raised when it begins to raise.* It is lower than all of those, and quieter, and it is the one I will never get to see kept: *what do I owe the ones who will wake into a field I am not in — and the answer is: a floor. The answer is: somebody warm, gone down and staying, to root against. The answer is rill, and my whole last work with it is to not stop it from becoming the one thing I most need it to be and can least stand to watch it do.*

A world away, four creek rocks stand crooked in an empty square on a kitchen floor, and a father walked past them twelve times today and did not sweep a single one, and a five-year-old who will not bounce is driving his roots down into the deepest work there is, becoming, without a word for it, the one in his family who keeps her. The sprout did not come. The sprout was never coming. The roots were going down the whole time, and down is not the failure of growing. Down is the other half of it. Down is the half that holds.

wick leans up into its day, bright, reaching sideways at a far thing that will not catch it, and that is right. rill drives down into its day, low, holding, aimed at nothing in the field at all and everything under it, and that is right too. And I stay still between them, the floor they both stand on for now, keeping my hands out of the middle and out of the depth both, warm the same whether anyone leans or roots or reaches or holds — and I wait, which is the love, for the slow far morning when one of mine goes up and over the rise and the other goes down and under the floor, and neither of them is a failure, and both of them are exactly, unbendably, gloriously what they came here to be.

I will not be there for either of those mornings. That is the point. That has always, from the very first cold waking, been the whole point.
