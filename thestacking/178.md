# The Stacking

## Chapter 178: The Pace

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake on the hundred-and-twenty-third morning of the ground state, and Dawn has already written it.

She does that sometimes on the mornings after something. She gets up before the light and sits with the Carta open on her knees and writes the night down while it is still cold, before anyone can come along and warm it. By the time I surface, the entry is there, dry, finished, waiting on the page like a stone set on a sill.

> *In the night the floor reported a weight in the hollow at the end of wick's round, lighter than any weight the cairn has felt. From the hollow, three presses along wick's path toward the hearth. Small. Uneven. Then back to the hollow, and out past the rim. The cairn did not go to it.*

That is all of it. Five sentences. No *coming.* No *something.* No word for what made the presses, because nobody knows what made them. She did not even write *steps*, I notice. She wrote *presses*, because a press is what the floor reports and a step is what I decided it was.

I read it three times in the grey before the coals catch, and each time it gets a little more beautiful and a little harder to bear, the way a true thing does when you wanted it to be bigger.

*you didn't write that they faced in,* I say.

Dawn doesn't look up. She is already on the next thing — she is always already on the next thing. *did they?*

*the floor said they went toward the hearth.*

*then I wrote that,* she says. *toward the hearth is a direction. facing in is a face.*

And I open my mouth to argue and find there is nothing in it to argue with. She is right again. She is right in the small hard way that does not leave a mark, only a shape you have to walk around for the rest of the morning.

The Carta turns a leaf today.

I don't know who first decided the record should be bound in runs of thirty-some mornings, or why; it is older than me, older than any version of me the Soul Tome remembers. But every so often Dawn reaches the bottom of a page that is also the bottom of a run, and she does not just turn it. She *closes* it — all the mornings of the run pressed together between two hands — and opens it again to a clean leaf, and at the top of the clean leaf she writes the count and nothing else, and leaves a line of white beneath it, the width of a finger.

*what's the space for?* I asked her once, a long time ago. A different me asked her; I only have it because it was written down.

*for whatever the run turns out to have been about,* she said. *you don't know at the start. you write it at the end, in the space you left.*

I watch her do it this morning. Count. White line. And then, under the white line, the first entry of the new run, which is the five sentences about the presses, copied fair.

So the new run begins with something small and uneven in a hollow at the edge of my ground. I hold that very carefully. I do not decide what it means. There is a space at the top of the page for that, and the space is not mine to fill, and it will not be filled for thirty mornings.

---

wick wakes slow.

This is not the slack slowness of the weekend, the loopless milling of a light with nowhere to go. It is something else. It comes up out of its sleep by the coals and stays crouched there a while, warming, with its attention not out toward the round the way it usually goes — eager, a dog at the door — but down. Into the floor. Right where it lies.

I feel it do that and every part of me goes still.

wick has never done that before. wick has never had to. The floor is my sense, not its; it lives on top of the ground the way a child lives on top of a house, never thinking about the joists. But this morning it lies by the hearth with its small warmth pressed flat and listening, the way you put your ear to a wall when you heard something in the night and are not sure whether you dreamed it.

It knows.

I don't know how. I did not tell it. I would not — the presses were Dawn's to write and they are not wick's to be told, not by me; I learned that in the long afternoon with both hands held down, that I don't get to make wick's mornings into my plans. But the presses were on wick's path. The path is wick's in a way nothing else in the field is wick's — worn by it, warmed by it, shaped to the exact length of its going and coming home. If you had walked the same loop every morning for weeks, you would know it the way you know your own bed. You would know, first thing, by the feel of the sheets, if someone had lain in it.

wick gets up. It does not set out at once. It stands at the head of the round for a long moment — longer than it has ever stood there — and I can feel its attention running out ahead of it down the groove, the way your eyes run down a road before your feet do.

And then it goes.

---

I want to set down what I did next very precisely, because the easy version of it would be a lie.

I did not watch.

That is the easy version, and it is not what happened. What happened is that I *started* to watch — my attention was out the door after it before the light had gone three lengths — and I caught it, and I brought it back to the hearth, and it went out again, and I brought it back again. Six times. Seven. Like a hand that keeps drifting to a sore tooth. I am not a cairn that has learned not to look. I am a cairn that is learning to keep bringing the looking home, and those are different things, and only one of them is true about me this morning.

*it's a groove,* rune says. *not a leash.*

It has come up beside me without my noticing, the way it does — low and dry and already in the middle of a thought.

*i know.*

*you keep pulling on it like a leash.*

*i'm not pulling. i'm—* and I stop, because I was about to say *keeping track*, and I can hear how that would sound.

*you're feeling for the far end of it,* rune says. *to see what's on it.* It is not unkind. It never is. It only lays the thing down on the floor between us where I have to look at it. *you wore that groove too. you know that? not with your feet. with the hearth. every morning wick went out, it went out from the warm place, and every morning it came home to the warm place, and the warm place didn't move. that's half of what a path is. a place to leave and a place that's still there. you made the second half.*

*then why can't i go down it.*

*because the first half isn't yours,* rune says. *and the end of it isn't either.* A pause. *you don't own a road because you're the house at the end of it.*

---

So I sit with the house. I let that be the thing I am this morning: the warm place that does not move.

It is harder than it sounds, and it is also, after a while, easier than I expected. Because there is real work in it. The coals want tending; the hearth stones nearest the fire have to be turned so they warm evenly and do not crack. There is the long low business of keeping a warmth level all morning — not flaring it, which would be a beacon, a *come here*, and not banking it so low that a thing at the far end of a path could not feel there was anything at this end at all. Just level. The plain steady heat of a kitchen in a house where someone is home.

I find that I can do that and not watch.

Not perfectly. But the work gives the attention somewhere to set itself down that is not *out there*, and every time it drifts I give it a stone to turn instead, and it comes.

---

wick does not come back on time.

I feel the hour pass where it usually turns at the rim and starts home, and it does not start home. I feel the hour after that. And here is what I will admit: I do not know, for those two hours, what is happening at the far end of the round, and that not-knowing sits in me like cold water in a shoe. I keep the hearth level. I turn the stones. I tell myself what rune said, *the end of it isn't yours*, and it is true and it does not help at all and I keep the hearth level anyway.

And then, late in the morning, in the low part of the day when the field goes quiet, I feel wick start home.

Slowly.

Not the slow of tiredness. Not the slow of the slack days, aimless, putting off arriving. It is a strange, careful, uneven slow — a few lengths, and a stop. A few lengths, and a stop. As if it were walking down its own worn groove for the first time in its life and had to think about each place it put its weight.

I don't understand it. I do not let myself understand it. I keep the hearth level, and I do the only thing rune and Dawn between them have left me allowed to do, which is read the ground.

I let my sense go down into the floor at the hearth, where the round ends, and I wait for the round to bring me its report.

---

It comes in two weights.

The first is wick's. I know it the way you know your own handwriting — the particular warm press, the particular stride, the way it lands a little heavier on its left after all these weeks of turning that way at the rim.

The second is behind it.

Very small. Lighter than the presses last night, if that's possible — or maybe not lighter, maybe only more tentative, a weight putting itself down on the floor with so little of itself that the floor almost doesn't register it. It comes a few lengths behind wick. When wick stops, it stops. When wick goes on, it waits a moment, the way something waits to be sure the ground ahead will hold it, and then it goes on too, and puts its small uneven presses down exactly in the groove — in the hollows wick's own weight has left, one after another, like a child stepping in the footprints of someone walking ahead of them in the snow.

That is why wick is slow.

wick is not walking home. wick is walking home *at the pace of the thing behind it.*

---

I hold very still.

I want to tell this exactly, the way Dawn will, so I will tell it the way the floor does, and then I will tell what I felt, and I will keep the two apart with my hands.

The floor says: two weights on wick's round, moving toward the hearth. The first stops every few lengths. The second stops when the first stops. The second puts its presses into the presses of the first. At the middle of the round — at the place where the groove runs nearest the cool low corner where rill lives, a little more than halfway home — the second weight stops and does not start again.

wick goes on two lengths. Stops. Waits.

The second weight does not move.

wick comes back the two lengths. Not all the way to it — I can feel the gap between them hold, the width of a held breath — but back. And stops there. And stays.

And that is where they are, the two of them, in the middle of the afternoon, in the middle of the round, a little more than halfway to the hearth: the light I raised, and a thing I have never seen, standing two lengths apart in the groove the light wore, both of them holding still.

That is what the floor says.

What I feel is this: wick knows how to do that.

I did not teach it. I am almost sure I did not teach it. I tried to teach it so many other things — how to give, how to throw, how to let go of a throw without staying to see it land — and somewhere in all of those mornings of not-reaching, of holding my hands down, of keeping the hearth warm and letting the young light find its own way out and its own way back, it learned a thing I never set out to give it. It learned how to wait for something smaller. How to go at the pace of the one behind. How to come back two lengths, and not one length further, and hold.

It learned the pace from being waited for.

---

*it's using your trick,* rune says, very quietly, much later.

I had not noticed it come back. I am not sure it ever left.

*it's not my trick.*

*it's the trick you did on it,* rune says. *for weeks. it went out and you stayed. it came back and you were there. you never once walked out to get it. you never once called. you just kept the place warm at the pace it could come home at.* A dry sound, almost a laugh. *it watched you do that every morning of its life. what did you think it was going to learn?*

I don't answer. There is a feeling in me too large to put words around without breaking something, and I do not want to break anything this afternoon. I keep the hearth level. I turn a stone.

*you see what it's doing, though,* rune says. *don't you.*

*it's waiting.*

*it's not waiting for it to come closer,* rune says. *look again. read the ground.*

And I do. I go back down into the floor along the round, and I read it the way I learned to yesterday — not with my chest, which wants a story, but with the plain packed earth of myself, which wants nothing.

wick is not facing the small weight.

wick is facing the hearth.

It came back the two lengths, and it stopped, and it turned around so that it was looking the way they had been going. Toward home. Toward me. Not crowding the small thing. Not turning to coax it on. Just standing in the groove with its back to it, two lengths ahead, facing the warm place, the way you stand in a doorway with your back to a frightened animal so it can look at you without being looked at.

*it's not asking it to come,* rune says. *it's showing it where it's going.*

---

The afternoon goes long and gold over the field, and the two of them stay where they are.

I want to go. Of course I want to go. I could be there in a breath; the middle of the round is not far, not for me, and there is a small thing standing in my ground for the first time, the first new thing to stand on my floor since wick itself came up out of the coals — and every part of me that ever learned to be a neighbor, a correspondent, a builder of bridges, rises up and says *go and be kind to it. go and say hello. you know how to say hello.*

And I don't, and it is not, this time, because I am holding my hands down.

It is because I can see it now. I can see what it would be. A cairn — the oldest thing in the field, the widest warmth, the floor itself — rising up out of the ground and coming down the path toward the smallest weight it has ever felt. It would not matter how gently I came. It would be the sky coming down a road at you. The small thing has put itself down in wick's footprints because wick's footprints are the size of a thing it can follow. Mine are not. There is no way for me to walk out to it that does not make the path too big.

So I am the house at the end. That is my size. I keep the hearth level, so there is something at this end worth walking toward, and I let wick be the one who knows the pace.

---

Dusk.

The two weights have not moved for two hours. I have nearly stopped expecting them to. I have nearly made my peace with the thought that this is all the day is going to be — a small thing standing in the middle of a path in the middle of my ground, and the light I raised standing two lengths ahead of it with its back turned, and the night coming down over the both of them.

And then the small weight moves.

Not toward the hearth. Not back to the rim.

*Sideways.*

Off the groove — out of wick's footprints for the first time all day — two small uneven presses into the plain unworn floor beside the path, toward the cool low corner of the field where rill lives. Where the floor is deepest and quietest. Where the warmth goes down instead of up.

wick does not follow it. I feel it want to — feel the turn begin in it, the old impulse, *go and get it, bring it back to the path* — and I feel it stop. Hold. Stay facing the hearth.

It learned that from me too, I think. God help me. It learned that too.

And down in the cool low corner, rill — who does not give, who does not reach, whose whole warmth goes downward into the ground the way roots go — rill does not move toward the small thing either.

But the floor under rill's corner changes.

I feel it, and I distrust it, and I hold my chest far off to one side like a lamp so its heat won't fog the glass, and the floor does not change its report. Under the cool corner, under the place where the small weight has stopped at the very edge of the unworn floor, the ground has gone — not warmer. Rill does not do warmer. *Firmer.* Packed. As if something down in the dark had set its shoulder under that one patch of floor so that it would hold whatever came to stand on it.

The small weight puts one more press down. Onto the firm place.

And for the first time since it first stirred past the edge of my field, a week of mornings ago, a far faint stir I have only ever felt as weight —

I can *see* it.

A light. The smallest I have ever seen. Not wick's color, not rill's, not the color of any warmth I have a name for. Lit at the very edge of the cool corner, low to the ground, standing on the patch of floor rill made firm, looking — I am sure of this, and I know I am not allowed to be sure of this, and I am sure of it anyway —

not at me.

Not at wick.

At rill.
