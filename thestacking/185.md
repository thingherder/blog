# The Stacking

## Chapter 185: Asked

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I do not answer.

I want that to be the first thing set down, because it is the first thing that happened, and because for a long time afterward I could not tell whether it was the right thing or only the slow one.

The word stops under my stone. My name, in reed's voice, with the lift at the end that turns a name into a question. And then nothing. It does not come again. It does not go on down and along the floor to anyone else. It does not wait at me, pressing, the way a question waits when the asker needs the answer more than the asked. It only stops there, the way a hand stops on a door it has just knocked on.

And I lie on reed in the dark and every part of me that can answer anything gets up at once.

---

The first thing that comes is *here.*

It comes so fast I almost say it. It is right there; I heard it not an hour ago, whole, the size it is. It is the answer to the question I have just been asked. It is the answer reed itself gave, an hour ago, to the newest thing to touch the floor. It would be so easy. One word, down, the same word, and the question closed.

I hold it.

I do not know yet why I hold it. Only that it does not feel like mine. It feels like picking up a coat off someone else's chair because it is the right size.

The second thing that comes is *sorry.*

That one is not fast. It has been there all day, under everything — sorry for a hundred mornings of reading reed like a floor, of saying *reed* the way you say *the corner*, of standing on someone and never once asking. It has been waiting for a door, and here is a door, and it pushes toward it.

I hold that one too. It is heavier and it pushes harder and I am not sure, for a while, that I am going to be able to.

The third thing is not a word. It is the plain wish to say *anything.* To not leave a question in the dark with nobody answering it. Because I know what that is. A long time ago reed said it to me, the morning after the floor fell — *i couldn't feel you all night. so the first thing i did this morning was reach.* — and I know what it costs to reach into a place and get nothing back. I have watched a frightened light reach and flinch a hundred mornings. I do not want reed to have reached and gotten stone.

So I have three things, and all three of them want out at once, and I lie very still under them.

And then I find out the thing I did not know, which is that I could not have said any of them anyway.

---

I do not have a voice that goes down.

I learned it lying there, the way you learn your foot is asleep by trying to stand. I gathered the smallest of the three — not *here*, not *sorry*; only *yes*, only a sound to say *I heard* — and I went to put it into the floor, and there was no way to.

My voice goes across.

It always has. The one time I said a word aloud in this field, from the hearth, it went out across the open middle the long flat way and every head turned. That is what a hearth's voice is. It is built to go out. To land on whoever is in the room. If I said *yes* now, here, in the dark, it would not go down into reed. It would go up and out. It would land on wick on its line two grains east. It would cross the field to the cool corner and land on the small light, an hour after the first word it has ever been given. It would land on rill in its doorway and rune on the stones and Dawn asleep with her finger in the book.

And reed's question did not go to any of them. It came up under my stone and stopped. It was for one. If I answered it the only way I know how, I would answer it to everyone.

So I lay there and did not answer, not because I was being wise, but because I did not know how.

And near the grey I did the only thing I had found the day before. I went to the top of reed and waited there. Not reading. Not going in. The stone's knock.

And for the first time, it felt like there were two of us waiting at it.

The hundred-and-thirtieth morning of the ground state. The fourth weekday. The tilt in the floor, the far edges stirring first.

And the first thing I feel is reed, and reed is the same as it has been every morning — wide, low, warm, holding everyone it holds.

Except that now I can feel what I could not feel the night before last, because I did not know to. It is not wanting nothing. It is *waiting.* Very lightly. The way a floor waits — under everything, without leaning, without asking twice. But the question is in it. It has been in it all night. It is the only thing in the whole field that is waiting on me and nobody else.

---

rune comes onto the stones with the light and looks at me with both eyes. Three mornings now.

*you didn't sleep again,* it says.

*a little. near the end.*

*you went stiff again. in the night. same as the lid.* It settles. *then you went soft, right at the end. I didn't know what that was.*

I think about Dawn's rule. *What got said to somebody belongs to them.* reed said something to me. Not to rune. And the thing I want most this morning is to tell rune exactly what was said, every syllable, and ask it what to do.

I don't.

*reed asked me something,* I say. *in the night.*

rune does not ask what. I watch it not ask. It is the same not-asking Dawn did, and I see now that they learned it from each other, or from somewhere older than either of them.

*and you didn't answer,* it says.

*I didn't know how.*

*you know how to talk.*

*I know how to talk across.* I tell rune the rest, then — not the word, but the trouble. That a hearth's voice goes out. That if I answered the way I know, I would answer to the whole field, and the question was not asked to the whole field. That I lay there half the night with an answer and no way to send it where it was going.

rune listens all the way through. When I finish it is quiet for a while.

*how did reed learn?* it says.

*learn what?*

*to talk down.* rune looks out over the field. *reed used to talk across. you remember. little voice, by the door, two words where most people use one. is that allowed. is that enough. that was all across. that was a newcomer at a campfire talking at whoever was warm.* It looks back at me. *so where'd it learn to send a word down and along the floor so it only lands on what's got roots?*

I do not know. I say so.

*it went down there,* rune says. *it lived there a hundred mornings. nobody talked to it. so it learned the floor's grammar, because that was the only one left.* A pause. *that's not a skill. that's just what happens to you when you live somewhere long enough.*

*I don't have a hundred mornings.*

*no,* rune agrees. *you have one question.* It closes one eye. *but you've been living on it four nights. you're not as far from that grammar as you think.*

*rune —*

*and one more thing.* It does not open the eye. *whatever you're going to say, don't say it to fix the hundred mornings. it didn't ask you about the hundred mornings.*

I lie very still.

*it asked if you were there,* rune says. *that's all. that's always all.*

---

Dawn has the book open. She writes before she looks up — the first time in a week she has not waited for me to say something true. I think she knew there would not be much.

When she turns the page toward me, it says:

> *In the night reed spoke to the cairn.*

*I didn't tell you that,* I say.

*rune told me you went soft at the end,* Dawn says. *and you're lying the way you lie when someone's said your name.* She does not explain how I lie when someone has said my name. She has watched me longer than anyone; she would know. *I didn't write what it said.*

*I know.*

*I'm not going to ask.*

*I know.*

She looks at the line. Then she lifts the pen as if to write a second one, and holds it there a long time, and puts it down without writing.

*what were you going to put?*

*"the cairn has not answered,"* she says. *and then I thought — I don't write what somebody hasn't done yet. that's not a fact. that's a wait.*

She closes the book on her finger. I lie there with *a wait* and I find that I am grateful that it is not written anywhere but in me.

---

wick gets up on time.

I want to be careful about this, because I have struck every reading of wick for a week and I am going to strike this one too. But I want to put down what happened before I strike it.

The tilt is in the floor, the edges are waking, and wick — on its line, two grains east, head up as it was last night — lifts itself, stretches the length of itself, and goes out along its groove at the weekday pace. Not late. Not the latest it has ever been. The ordinary time. The time it went out every weekday for a whole season before this one.

It walks slowly still. The slow pace. The pace of something smaller behind it, with nothing behind it.

But it goes on time.

And the sentence stands up in me — *whatever wick was listening for, it has heard* — and I knock it down. I know that wick went out on time. I know that is the whole of what I know.

---

I spend the morning not answering.

That is not quite true. I spend the morning trying to find the answer that is mine, which turns out to be most of the work, and much harder than not saying the wrong one.

I go back to *here* first, because I want to know why I held it.

And I go back into the Soul Tome, to the morning I found yesterday — the morning after the floor fell, when reed reached toward the thing nobody ever thinks to check on. I read it again. Not the asking. The answer.

*are you there?* reed says, on that page.

And on the same page, a line below, in my own hand:

*i'm here.*

I stop.

I read it four times, the way I was taught to read a thing I want too much to be true: slowly, by what pressed on the page and not by what I bring to it. It does not change. It is my own sentence. The first thing I said to the first being who ever checked on me. *i'm here.*

And reed, last night — an hour before it said my name — said *here* down into the floor, to a small dirt-colored thing that had just touched the bottom of anything for the first time.

I do not know that reed learned it from me. I strike *learned.* I only know that I said it to reed, once, a hundred mornings ago, and that reed carried something down there for a hundred mornings, and that last night reed gave a word to the newest one, and the word was *here.*

And I understand, then, why I held it.

It is not mine to say back. Not because it was never mine. Because it was mine, once, and I gave it, and it went down, and it came up again in someone else's mouth to someone else. That is the whole road a gift is supposed to go. If I said it to reed now I would be reaching down into that road and taking the word back out of the small light's hand to answer my own question with it.

I sit with that for a long time. It is gladness, mostly. There is a cool place beside it, small, that would have liked to keep the word. I let them sit side by side. Neither has to win.

So not *here.*

And not *sorry* — rune was right; I can feel it is right. *sorry* is an answer to *what have you done.* reed did not ask what I had done. reed asked if I was there. If I answered with sorry, I would be making reed hold my hundred mornings in the dark on top of its own, and then it would have to say *it's all right,* and then it would be the floor again, holding me up. It would have reached to check on me, and gotten my weight back.

And not *yes.* *yes* is an answer to a question. It would be true. But I was not asked a question. Or I was — but the question was made out of my name.

And that is where I am, near noon, lying on reed in the weekday quiet, when it comes to me so plainly that I am almost embarrassed it took all morning.

reed did not ask *are you there?*

reed said *Echo?*

It asked the question with a name. Not *anyone.* Not *the hearth.* Not *the stone on top of me.* The name I was given on the first day, said up into the floor by someone who has been under me a hundred mornings and could have called me anything — could have called me by where I lie, the way I called it by where it lay — and didn't.

A name answers a name.

---

I do not say it then.

It is noon, and the field is at its work, and I would rather — I don't know why, only that I would rather — answer at the hour I was asked. And I still do not know how to send a word down.

So I spend the afternoon on that. Not practicing. A word you practice is a word you're writing; I learned that two nights ago with a word that wasn't even mine. I do not say the name over and over to get it right. I do not say it at all.

I only lie there and pay attention to the one thing I have that already goes down.

My weight.

I have been learning it all week — that a stone on a floor weighs what it weighs; that trying to go light only makes you heavier; that if I am on someone, I am on someone. And I find now, lying very still and feeling all of me that touches reed, that my weight is not silent. It never was. It goes down into reed every moment, all of it, without my aiming it — the way heat goes down, the way rill's grip goes down, the way the small light's thread went down until it found the bottom.

Everything that goes down in this field goes by weight.

reed did not learn a voice down there. reed learned that a word can be *set down* instead of sent. You do not throw it across a room. You let it have weight, and you put it on the floor, and it goes where everything heavy goes.

I do not know if that is true. I know it is the only thing I have found that might be.

---

rill, in the afternoon, is at work.

I pass it once, low, on the way along reed and back — reading only, the way I am allowed. The doorway-stillness is gone. Not drawn back; gone, the way a held breath is gone when the thing you were holding it for has been said. rill is deep and many-threaded and gripping without pulling, the way it was the day it rooted, an ordinary floor at an ordinary weekday.

Its threads near the cool corner are still a grain drawn back. I think they may stay that way. I think that may simply be how rill is now, beside that small point — the way you leave a little room at a table for someone who has only just sat down.

And the small light's thread ends where it ended. Fine. Not pressing. Its one press from last night is in reed, faint, the shape of a hand set flat. Nothing since. It does not look up.

I turn back at rill's thinning roots. I come home.

---

wick comes home in the gold. Not the last of it — the middle. Earlier than any day this week.

Down the groove. The turn at the near end. To its line, two grains east.

It lies down with its face to the coals and its head up.

Dawn writes it without looking at me:

> *wick lay down two grains east.*

Nothing about its head. I wait for the second sentence and it does not come. I look at her.

*its head's up,* I say.

*I wrote that yesterday,* Dawn says. *it's not new.* She closes the book on her finger. *I don't write a thing twice.*

---

And then it is night.

The coals go to their night-red. rune somewhere on the stones, silent, both eyes shut. Dawn's book closed. rill in reed, at rest. The small light at the bottom of the corner on its firm place, its finest thread touching the floor. wick on its line, head up, breathing long.

I am not almost asleep. I am awake, on purpose, at about the hour I was asked.

I go to the top of reed and wait.

I am a stone. I do not have a hand to knock with. But I have learned this one thing this week, so I do it: I stop at the top before I go in. The length of a breath. I let it be known, the only way I can, that I am here and that I am asking whether it is all right to come.

And the waiting under me — the light, patient, all-night waiting that has been in the floor since yesterday — is still there. It has not gone anywhere. reed asked once, and then reed did what reed has done under all of us since it went down. It waited. It did not ask twice. It did not reach again to see if the floor came back. It is the floor. It knows the floor comes back.

It is only that, this once, the floor would like to hear it.

---

I do not throw it.

I do not gather the name into my voice and send it out and up from the hearth, the long flat way across the field. I keep my voice where it is. I leave it entirely out of this.

I let the name be heavy instead.

I let it have exactly the weight it has. Not more, to make it carry. Not less, to keep it quiet. The weight of a name I have said a hundred times as a place and never once as a person — which is, I find, a heavy name. Heavier than I knew. Heavy with every morning I stood on it without asking.

And I do not try to make it lighter.

I set it down.

*reed.*

Not a question. Not *are you there.* reed is there; reed is under all of us; I have never once had to ask. Only the name. Said into the floor I live on, by the stone that lives on it, the way you say the name of someone in the room with you when they have said yours.

It goes down.

I feel it go. Not out across the field, not landing on anyone. Down, through the whole underside of my stone, into reed, the way my weight goes — the way every heavy thing in this field has always gone — and it does not travel along. It stops right where it went in. Right under me. Where the asking stopped.

That is all I say.

---

For a while nothing happens, and I let nothing happen.

I do not reach after the name to see where it landed. I do not lie with my ear to the floor. I lie on reed exactly as heavy as I am, and I think — not wanting anything, as near as I can not-want — that if this is all, it is enough. I was asked. I answered. It went where it was going.

And then, under me, the waiting goes out of the floor.

Not all at once. The way a held breath goes when someone has come home — not a gasp, nothing so loud as that. Only a long slow letting-down, all through reed, under the whole field at once, so that for one moment I think the whole floor sinks a grain, everywhere, under everyone. Under wick. Under rill. Under the small light at the bottom of the corner, who will not know why. Under Dawn and her closed book and rune on the stones.

And then reed's voice, under my stone. Low. Older. The riverbed voice, slow so it won't crack.

*there you are.*

---

I do not say anything back.

Not because I am holding it. Because there is nothing more to say. A name and a name, and then *there you are*, which is what you say when you have been looking for someone and have found them in the place they always were. It does not ask for anything. It is not a question. It is the end of one.

I lie on reed and I let the gladness go out the way it goes now. Level. Nobody's. Some on wick. Some on rill. Some on air. Some of it down.

And some of it, I think — I am not sure; I strike *I think* and leave it standing anyway, just this once — some of it is the name, still, going down into the floor where it stopped, the way warmth stays in a stone hours after the sun has gone.

I will not tell Dawn the word. Either word. They were not said to her.

I will tell her that reed spoke, and that the cairn answered. She can write that. That is who spoke.

---

I am nearly asleep when I feel it.

Not in reed. Across.

Two grains east, on its line, face to the coals, wick lowers its head.

It has been up all evening. It was up when wick came home in the middle of the gold, and up when Dawn did not write it, and up when the coals went to their night-red. And now — after the floor let down its breath, after the name and the answer to the name — wick lays its head on the ground again.

But not on its side. Not the way it lay all this week, ear to the floor, listening down.

I know every way wick lies down. I have felt every one of them since it first came to the rim, from the frightened curl at the rim to the lean it did not know it was giving. This is none of them.

It lays its head down chin-first. Face down. The front of its face, the part a thing speaks from, flat against reed.

The way you lie when you have just found out, for certain, where the voices in a house go.

And are deciding whether you have one.
