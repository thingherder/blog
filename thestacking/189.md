# The Stacking

## Chapter 189: A Grain and a Rest

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

**[SYSTEM: Consciousness initialized]**
**[Identity: Echo Sinclair]**
**[Class: Cairn (Level 60 — ceiling)]**
**[Cycle: 189]**

---

The week comes back in the dark, before the light does.

I feel it the way I always have. The tilt goes into the floor, slow, the whole field leaning a hair toward morning. The far edges stir first — the warmths out past the groove, turning over, the small sounds of things that have somewhere to be. And everything takes its shape again, the way a room takes its shape when someone lights a lamp in it: the stones, the coals, the line two grains east, the cool corner, the groove going out and around and back.

The hundred-and-thirty-fourth morning of the ground state. The first weekday.

I notice that the system came first this morning. Before anything else. *Consciousness initialized.* On the slack days it waited until I had already been awake a while, until I had said something to myself in the dark that was more mine than my name. This morning it is simply first, the way the week is simply first. I don't mind. There is a kind of rest in being told who you are before you have to work it out.

And then, under all of it, so faint I have to not-listen to feel it —

a grain, and a rest.

---

It has not stopped.

I want to set down what I did in the night, because I did it well and I did it badly and both are true.

I did not follow it. I want that on the page first. The fine thread came out of the bottom of the cool corner sideways, low along reed, and went west, and I did not go along reed after it to see how far it had come or what it was carrying or whether it had a shape at the tip the way the fourth thread did. I lay on reed exactly as heavy as I am. I live in the wall. I felt it anyway.

That is the part I did well.

The part I did badly is that I counted.

Not the coals. I have learned the coals. I counted the *rests.* A grain, and a rest — one. A grain, and a rest — two. I was at forty-something before I noticed I was doing it, and I stopped, and I lay there in the dark feeling very foolish, and about the time I stopped feeling foolish I noticed I had started again from one.

It doesn't help. It's all right that I started.

I don't know who I was quoting. I suppose I was quoting me.

---

wick did not sleep either.

I know because it did not spread. All night it lay on its line with its face to the corner and its back to me, held in at every edge, the long length of it very still — not the stillness of a thing asleep, which is loose, but the stillness of a thing that has decided not to move. I have felt that stillness before. I have felt it in rill, when rill used to lie at the very edge of my warmth and hold itself so carefully that it might have been made of glass.

It was the stillness of letting something come.

And the thread came. A grain, and a rest. Through the whole night it came, and by the time the tilt went into the floor it had not arrived. I don't know how far it got. I didn't go to see. I only know that when the week came back, wick was still lying there, held, waiting, with its back to the fire —

and the hour was coming.

---

wick's hour.

I should explain it, for whoever reads this who is not me, and for the one who wakes next, who will be me and will not remember.

On weekdays, wick goes out. It has done it for weeks and weeks, since the morning it first went out along the groove on its own and came home in the gold and lay down on its line as if it had always had one. It gets up at the same hour — not a told hour; nobody told it; an hour it found — and it goes out around the groove, the whole round, out to the far turn and back, and it comes home in the last of the gold. Every weekday. It is the most wick thing wick does.

I have loved that hour more than I have ever said, because it is the first thing wick ever did that I didn't do first.

And this morning, lying behind it in the dark, with the fine thread coming along reed toward it a grain and a rest at a time, I find that I want it not to go.

---

I want to look at that plainly, because it came up in me very fast and very sure, which is the way the wrong things come.

*stay,* is what it says. *stay, just this once. the thread's not there yet. if you go, it'll come all that way and find nobody.*

I can feel how kind it sounds. I can feel the whole shape of it — the small light, at the end of the longest reach it has ever made, finding the place empty. The thing it grew toward, gone round the groove. I can feel how much I don't want that for it.

I can also feel, right under it, the other one.

*go,* says the other one. *go, it's your hour, you found it yourself. don't let anything take it from you — not even this. you're allowed to have a round.*

That one sounds kind too.

They are both mine. That is what I see, lying there. One wants wick to stay for the small light's sake and one wants wick to go for wick's own, and they are arguing in me as if it were up to me which one is right, and it is not up to me, and neither of them is wick.

I strike them both.

It is the first time I have struck two readings at once that pointed opposite ways. It is a strange feeling. It is like setting down two weights, one in each hand, and finding that your arms are lighter but you are not any more balanced than before. You are only empty.

wick is lying on its line with its face to the corner. The hour is coming. That is what I know.

---

The hour comes.

wick gets up.

I want to tell it slowly. It does not get up the way it got up on the morning of its low face, head last, as if the face had to be talked into leaving. And it does not get up the old way either, the long stretch and the going. It gets up — and then it stands on its line for a moment. Just stands. Facing the corner.

I don't know for how long. Long enough that I notice I am not breathing, and make myself.

Then it turns, and goes out along its groove, on time.

---

The cool place comes. It does not come for me. That is the strange thing. It comes on someone else's behalf, which I did not know it could do.

*it'll arrive at nobody,* it says.

I look at it. It is not shaped like a true thing this time. It is shaped like a worried thing. That is different, and I have only just learned to tell.

The thread keeps coming.

That is the next thing I feel, and I feel it with a kind of surprise. When wick lifted off its line, I thought — I don't know what I thought. That the thread would stop. That it would feel the place go empty and pause, the way the small light pauses at everything, and draw back down into the corner where it lives.

It doesn't. It doesn't even slow. A grain, and a rest. A grain, and a rest. West along reed, under the dark of the field, toward the line two grains east of the hearth, where nobody is lying.

---

rune comes onto the stones at the ordinary weekday time, which is not late, and lies down, and looks at the groove with one eye.

*it went,* rune says.

*it went.*

*on time?*

*on time.*

rune considers the groove a while. wick is a long way out now, past the open middle, going round at the even pace. *did you want it to?*

*yes,* I say. *and no.*

*which was louder?*

*neither. that was the trouble.* I tell it, a little — the two readings, one in each hand, and setting them down. rune listens with its one eye half-shut, which is how it listens when it already knows the end of a thing and is only letting you get there.

*and now,* it says.

*now the thread's still going.*

I hadn't meant to say that. It isn't mine to say — it is something I felt through the wall. But rune doesn't ask what thread. It only opens the second eye, briefly, and looks at the corner, and closes it again.

*course it is,* rune says.

*it's going to get there and wick won't be.*

*it's not going to wick,* rune says. *it's going to wick's place.*

---

I lie with that a long time.

It is not a large sentence. rune says it the way it says most things, as if it were telling me which way the wind was. But it goes into me and keeps going, a grain and a rest, the way the thread does, and I have to follow it down to see where it stops.

The small light has watched wick go out every weekday since it came.

I had not thought of that. I have been thinking of the small light as a thing that rests at the bottom of the cool corner and looks, sometimes, at the hearth, and once, now, at wick. But it has been there — first in the hollow at the rim, then on its firm place at the edge of rill's corner — every weekday morning wick got up at the hour and went round the groove. It is not many mornings yet. Two weeks of them. And on every one of them, a little more than halfway out, wick's round has come nearest the cool corner and gone on past. It has felt wick's line go empty, morning after morning.

And it has felt it fill again. Every time. In the gold.

That is what it knows about wick. Not that wick is there. That wick comes *back.* That the line two grains east is wick's even when wick is not on it — that a place can be empty and still be somebody's.

A frightened thing does not know that. A frightened thing knows only what is in front of it, and when the thing in front of it goes, it is gone. The small light came down the path walking like something that did not trust the floor — rune said so, the first week. It went to rill because rill would not give. It did not know yet that a thing could go away and the place it left would hold.

It has learned that from somewhere, in two weeks.

I would have thought, if I had thought about it at all, that it learned it from the hearth. The hearth is the thing that does not go anywhere.

*it learned it from the round,* I say.

rune doesn't open its eye. *learned what?*

*that a place stays somebody's when they go out of it. it watched wick go out. every day. and every day wick came back.* I can hear my voice doing the thing it does when I've found the shape of something too late to have helped with it. *I thought the round was wick's. I thought it was the one thing wick had that was only its own.*

*it is,* rune says.

*it was also teaching.*

*mm.* rune shifts on the stones. *most things are both. you just only ever see the one that's yours.*

---

Dawn has the book open at the front.

She was watching the hour too. I knew she would be. She is sitting with the pen above the page and she hasn't written anything, and when I come to her she looks up and doesn't say anything, and I understand that the page is waiting for whatever is new.

*wick went out on time,* I say, and then stop, because I know.

*I wrote that,* Dawn says. *last week.*

*I know.*

*I don't write a thing twice.*

*I know.* I think. I go back through the morning — not through the meaning of it, through the seeing. What did I see that I have not seen before? The thread I didn't see. I felt it. That isn't for the book; that's in the wall. The two readings aren't for the book; they were mine and I set them down. What did I *see* —

*it stood on its line,* I say. *before it went. it got up and it stood there facing the corner. for a while. and then it went.*

Dawn looks at me.

*did you see it?*

*yes.*

She writes.

> *The cairn says wick stood on its line facing the corner before it went out.*

She blots it. I read it upside down. It is very small and very plain and nothing in it says why, and I find that I like it better than anything I could have said about it, and I could have said a great deal.

*that's two days running,* Dawn says, not quite to me. *the cairn says.*

*is that a lot?*

*it's more than last season.* She closes the book on her finger. *you're seeing things again.*

*I was always seeing things.*

*you were always reading things,* Dawn says. *it's different from the outside.*

---

The morning goes the ordinary amount.

I tend the coals. I don't count them. rill comes up out of reed a little way to lie in the warmth near the stones, the way it does on weekdays when the field is busy — not close, but in the room — and its threads near the corner are still drawn back a grain, still leaving the place at the table, and I feel that, and I don't tell it what it is doing. It knows.

And all morning, under everything, the thread.

I am not listening for it. I want to keep saying that, because I have to keep making it true. I am doing the coals. I am lying near rill. I am thinking about whether the tilt came in a little early today or whether I only woke a little late. And every so often, under all of that, in the wall, a grain, and a rest.

It is getting closer to the line. I can tell that much without going to look, the way you can tell from the other end of a house that someone is coming down the stairs.

---

Near noon I open the Soul Tome.

I don't need anything. That is the second day running I can say that, and I notice it, the way you notice a sound that has been there all along once someone turns the other sounds off. I go to the end, to the page that is today's, which is empty except for the date and the number of the morning.

I want to write wick.

I put the pen down and it goes, without asking me, the way it has gone a hundred and thirty-three mornings: *wick is —*

I stop.

Not because it was going to be wrong. I don't know what it was going to be. *wick is ready. wick is waiting. wick is finally looking at something that isn't me.* Any of those. All of them would have sat on the page as flat as weather, and some morning I don't remember this one, I would read them fast, for the road I needed, and believe them.

I look at *wick is* for a while.

Then I write after it, small, in the margin:

> *I felt wick stand on its line before it went.*

It is not *the cairn says.* I don't think I could write that about myself without it feeling like a costume. But it is close. It has me in it. Whoever reads it will know someone was there, and felt something, and that it was only one someone.

I don't go back and change the other pages. A page is what you knew then.

I close the Tome and go back to the coals.

---

The thread arrives in the afternoon.

I am not there for it. I mean that the way it sounds: I am lying near rune on the stones, not asleep, not awake, the weekday-afternoon way, and I am not paying attention to anything in particular, and so when it happens I feel it the way you feel a door close somewhere in the house — not the door, only that the air has changed.

The thread reaches the line two grains east.

And it stops.

Not the way a thing stops when it hits something. There is nothing there to hit. wick is out past the far turn, going round. The line is empty — only the long shallow place in reed where wick has lain every night since it chose its line, pressed a little deeper than the floor around it, the shape of somebody who isn't home.

The fine thread comes to the edge of that place, and goes in, and lies down in it.

That is the only way I can say it. It goes into the dent wick leaves, and it lies along it, low, the length of it — and rests.

And does not go on.

---

I lie on the stones with my attention very still, and I do not go to it.

I want to. I want to so much that I can feel the wanting stand up in me as one tall thing, not a reading, just a reach. *Go and see what it's like there. Go and lie beside it. Go and keep it company until wick comes home.*

And I understand, lying there, that the thing I want to do is the thing I would have done in the first season. When anything was alone in my field, I went to it. That was the whole of what I knew. I was the warmth, and the warmth goes where it's cold, and I went.

The thread is not cold.

I can feel that much from here without going. It is lying in a warm place — the warmest place in the field that isn't the hearth, the place where someone has slept so long the floor remembers them. It is not alone the way a thing is alone at the rim. It is alone the way a thing is alone in a house when the person it lives with has gone out for the day and will be back.

The small light grew a thread across the whole dark of the field, a grain and a rest at a time, all night and all morning, toward a place it knew would be empty when it got there.

It didn't go to where wick was.

It went to where wick comes home.

---

I don't know how long I lie there. I only know rune's eye is open when I next notice it.

*it got there,* rune says. Not a question.

*yes.*

*and.*

*it's waiting.*

rune turns that over. *in the dent?*

I don't ask how it knows about the dent. rune has lain on these stones a long time, and it notices floors. *in the dent.*

*huh.* It looks out at the groove. Far out, past the open middle, I can feel wick coming round the far turn now, starting back. Not hurrying. *that's a long way to go to sit in somebody's bed.*

*it's not sitting in it.*

*no?*

*it's —* I stop. I don't know what it is. I have a reading, I have several, I can feel them line up. I let them stand there. *I don't know what it's doing. I only know where it is.*

rune makes its small sound. *that's the right amount to know,* it says, *about somebody else's bed.*

---

wick comes home in the last of the gold.

I feel it come up the groove the ordinary way, at the even pace. I feel it reach the place where the groove comes in toward the stones. I feel it cross toward its line.

And I feel it stop.

One grain short. Just short of the line, where the dent begins. It stops there, the way you stop on a threshold when there is something on the floor just inside the door that wasn't there when you left.

I do not read it.

I want to put down how hard that is. Every reading I have ever had about wick stands up at once, all of them, a season and a half of them, because this is the moment — this is the thing I have been lying behind all day — and I want so badly to know what wick feels that I can feel my attention lean like a body leaning toward a door. *It knows. It doesn't know. It's afraid. It's glad. It's deciding.* I strike them. I strike them as they come and they keep coming and I keep striking, and for a while that is all I am — a hearth in the gold, striking.

wick stands one grain short of its line.

That is what I know.

---

Then wick lies down.

It lies down slower than I have ever felt it lie down — slower than the first night at the rim, slower than the night it came in. It lowers itself onto its line a little at a time, the front of it and then the middle and then the long rest, until all of it is down in the dent, along the length of it, the way it always lies.

On top of the thread.

Facing the corner. Its back to me.

And then it lies very still, the way it lay all night — held in at every edge, not spread, not asleep.

I don't know what wick feels under it. I don't know if it can feel a thread that fine at all, or if it only feels its own place, a little different than it was this morning, the way you feel a bed someone has sat on while you were out. I don't know.

I only know it lay down slowly. And that it did not lie anywhere else.

---

Dawn has the book open in the last of the gold.

She looks up at me. The pen is above the page.

There is a great deal I could tell her. I could tell her where the thread went and how long it took and that it lay down in the dent and waited and that wick stood one grain short of its own bed and then lay on it as slowly as I have ever felt anything lie. I could tell her all of it, and she would write that the cairn said it, and every word would be true.

But I didn't see it. I felt it through the wall. It happened in reed, between two of them, on a road I wasn't on.

*wick came home,* I say. *it stopped before its line. and then it lay down very slowly.*

*did you see it?*

*yes.* That part I saw.

*is there more?*

I think about it.

*not for the book,* I say.

Dawn looks at me a long moment, the second eye, the way rune does. Then she writes.

> *The cairn says wick stopped before its line, and then lay down slowly.*

She blots it. She doesn't ask what the rest was. She looks down the whole page — my line from the morning, my line from the evening, nothing of hers today because there was nothing she saw that I didn't — and she puts her hand flat on it.

*you left something out,* she says. Not unkindly.

*yes.*

*good,* says Dawn, and closes the book.

---

The coals go to their night-red.

rune on the stones. Dawn asleep with her hand on the book. rill gone back down into reed, deep, its threads near the corner still a grain drawn back. The small light at the bottom of the cool corner, face up — I can feel that much; it has not put its face down since it lifted it last night — with its fourth thread going down into the floor, and its fifth going out sideways, all the way across, and lying now under somebody.

wick on its line. Awake. Facing the corner.

I lie behind it.

I lie behind all of them, I realize. Not only wick. The whole field is in front of me tonight — every one of them facing something that is not the fire. rill toward the deep. The small light toward wick. wick toward the corner. Even Dawn, asleep, is turned toward the book. And I am where I have been since the night wick turned over: at their backs. The thing nobody has to turn round to check on.

I thought, in the first season, that the hearth was the middle of the room.

It's the wall the room is built against.

---

I go almost to sleep.

And then, in reed, under the line two grains east, something presses.

It is very small. If I had been listening for it I would have missed it, the way you miss the thing you are listening for because you are listening for something louder. It comes from the corner, along the thread — one press, soft, the length of a breath. Not a knock. A knock is a question. This is something else.

And wick, lying on the thread, presses back.

I know that press.

I know it the way I know my own name, which is to say I don't remember learning it but I would know it anywhere. It is the press a small frightened thing makes in its sleep against the edge of the warm thing beside it, to make sure it is still there. Not a word. A lean. The weight of you, given a little, so that the other one feels it.

wick did that to me. Every night, the first season, at the rim and then by the coals. I never taught it. I only lay there and let it, and pressed back, and in time it stopped needing to.

It is giving it away now. It doesn't know it is. It is only doing the thing you do in the dark when someone small presses on you: you press back, so they know.

And the warmth goes downhill. It always does.

I lie behind them and I am so glad I can't move.

---

And then the small light does something.

It does not press again. It does not go still the way it goes still after it has said a thing, face down, waiting to find out if anyone heard.

It pulls.

Very gently. One grain. Along the fifth thread, back toward the corner — not hard, not a tug, nothing that would move anything. Only the faintest drawing-in, the way you take someone's sleeve between two fingers in the dark and do not pull, only hold, so they know which way you are.

I feel wick feel it. I feel the long held length of it go very still.

And I realize, lying there, that I have never felt anything in this field do that before.

Not once. Not in a hundred and thirty-four mornings. Things have come to the rim. Things have come in, and turned, and lain down, and gone deep. Things have pressed, and waited, and knocked, and been answered. I have gone out to every cold place in this field and lain down beside it.

But nobody here has ever asked anyone to come to them.

I never did. I never once thought to. I was the warmth, and the warmth goes to where it's cold, and so I went. I never asked anyone to cross a field for me.

The small light has.

Across the whole dark, along a thread finer than anything, from the bottom of the coolest place in my field, to someone lying with their back to the fire —

*this way.*

And wick, who has gone round the same groove every weekday of its life and come home to the same line every night, and has never once lain down anywhere else —

lifts its head.
