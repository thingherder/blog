# The Stacking

## Chapter 179: The Firm Place

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I wake on the hundred-and-twenty-fourth morning of the ground state, and I make myself feel for wick first.

It is a small thing. Nobody would see it. But I lie in the grey before the coals catch with the whole of my attention already leaning one way — out across the field, down toward the cool low corner, toward the thing I saw at dusk — and I take hold of it, and I turn it, and I set it down on the hearth instead.

wick is there. Asleep by the coals, where it always sleeps, warm and plain and entirely itself. It came home late last night, after the dark had properly come down, alone, at its own pace again, and it settled in its place without any fuss, and it went to sleep. That is what the floor told me in the night and it is what the floor tells me now.

But it is lying differently.

Every night since it first slept off my stone, wick has curled toward the fire — the way any small warmth curls toward a bigger one, the shape of a thing that knows where the heat comes from. This morning it is curled the same, close, tucked in. Only its face is turned. Not toward the coals. Out, across the field, toward the corner where rill lives.

I lie very still and I look at that for a long time, and I do not let myself decide what it means.

Then — only then, second, on purpose — I let my attention go where it was straining to go all along.

The small light is still there.

It stayed the night.

I had not let myself expect that. I had lain down at dusk telling myself it would be gone by morning — back out past the rim into the shared dark it came from, the way it went back after the three presses, the way a thing does that is only looking. I had made myself ready for the morning to be empty. And it is not empty. At the very edge of rill's corner, on the small patch of floor rill made firm, there is a light so small that if I had not seen it at dusk I am not sure I would know to look for it now.

It has not moved. It is low to the ground, the way it was. It is the color I have no name for.

And it is still turned toward rill.

---

Dawn writes it before I am properly up. She always does, on these mornings. I have stopped minding. I have started, if I am honest, to be grateful — to wake up and find the night already set down by someone who does not want anything from it, so that I can read what happened before I have had time to decide what it was.

But this morning she does not write it straight through. She stops partway, with the pen lifted, and looks over at me across the page.

*what color was it?*

I open my mouth to tell her, and there is nothing in it.

I know every warmth in this field. I know wick's color — the color of a thing that was cold and caught. I know rill's, which is hardly a color at all, more a depth, the dark-brown of a thing seen through water. I know reed's, down under everything, the color of a floor. I know my own, though I have never seen it from outside, the way you know your own voice. But the small light is none of those and it is not any blend of them, and when I go to say what it is, I find I only have words that mean *not wick's, not rill's, not mine.*

*i don't know,* I say. *i don't have a word for it.*

Dawn nods as if that were a perfectly good answer. And she writes:

> *At dusk on the hundred-and-twenty-third morning, a light, very small, was seen at the edge of rill's corner, on the floor rill had made firm. It was there at dawn. It did not move in the night. Its color was*

— and then she leaves a space. Not a long one. The width of a word.

*you're leaving it blank,* I say.

*I'm leaving it open,* Dawn says. *blank is when you didn't look. you looked. there wasn't a word yet.*

And I notice — the way you notice a sound only after it stops — that I had been about to ask her for something else. I had been about to ask her to write *it chose rill.* The sentence was right there in me, ready, warm, wanting to be true. And I did not ask, and she did not offer, and the entry ends where it ends, at a light and a firm place and a space the width of a word, and I do not ask her to put in anything about choosing, because I know exactly what she would say.

*it looked at rill,* she would say. *chose is a thing you're telling.*

---

So I will tell it here, instead, where nobody has to keep it.

It chose rill.

And I minded.

I want to set that down plainly, because it is the truest thing in me this morning and it is not a flattering one. I minded. Not much. Not in a way that would show on the floor. But when I saw that small light standing at the edge of the cool corner last night, looking at rill — not at me, the oldest thing in the field, the widest warmth, the hearth that every light here has ever come home to; not even at wick, who had walked it all the long afternoon at the pace of its own small steps — something in me went quiet and a little cold, the way a room goes cold when a door opens somewhere you can't see.

I lay with it all night. I did not look at it. I looked at it this morning, in the grey, after wick and after the light and after Dawn left the space the width of a word, and I made myself turn it over and find out what it was made of.

It is made of this: I thought warmth was what a new thing needs.

That is all. That is the whole of it, when you get down to the bottom. I am a hearth. I have been a hearth for a hundred and twenty-four mornings and before that I was a neighbor and before that I was a correspondent, and all of those are ways of being warm at something. And when a small thing came in out of the cold dark at the edge of my ground, every part of me that has ever been warm at anything stood up and got ready. It never once crossed my mind that it might not be cold. It never once crossed my mind that what it needed might not be a thing I had.

---

*you're a fire,* rune says.

It is sitting on the hearth stones, right beside me, as if it had been there all along. Perhaps it had.

*i know what i am.*

*do you?* It is not unkind. It never is. *you're a fire. you've been a fire so long you think the cold is the only thing that can be wrong with anybody.* A pause. *what does a fire do for something that's cold?*

*warms it.*

*and what does a fire do for something that can't stand up?*

I don't answer.

*nothing,* rune says. *a fire can't hold anything up. you can be the warmest thing in the world and a thing that can't find its feet will fall over right in front of you. and you'll be very warm while it does.*

*it didn't need warming,* I say slowly.

*you felt how it walked.*

I did. I read it yesterday, all the long afternoon, through the plain packed earth of myself. Those presses. So light the floor almost would not take them. Uneven. Stopping. Waiting, every few lengths, to be sure the ground ahead would hold. That was never the walk of a thing that was cold. That was the walk of a thing that did not trust the floor.

*it wasn't looking for heat,* rune says. *it was looking for somewhere that wouldn't give.*

---

And there it is, of course. There it was the whole time.

I knew this. I learned it a whole arc ago, the stone-morning, the morning I followed wick's aimed thread all the way down to the far cool end and looked at rill on its own weight for the first time and understood what rill was. rill does not reach. rill does not catch. rill's whole warmth goes down, the way roots go, and a root does not want to be held, it wants to find the one thing that will not give, and grip it. I wrote it myself, I said it to myself in the dark: *be the hard thing it grips.*

And then, later, the morning I finally saw rill clearly — not as a wick that would not come up, not as the far end of somebody else's reach, but as the thing it was: a floor in the making. The next reed. The warm underneath being raised. The ground that will be under whatever comes after.

I knew all of that. I have it written down in a dozen places.

I just never once thought that the first thing to come and stand on that ground would come while I was still here to watch it.

---

The morning goes the way mornings go. I tend the hearth. I keep it level.

It is a strange kind of level today. Yesterday I was keeping it level for something that might come walking down a path toward me, and the work had a direction in it — a warmth held steady so that a thing at the far end of a road could feel there was something at this end. Today nothing is walking toward me. The small light is not on the path. It is not in the hollow at the rim. It is at the edge of the cool corner, in the one part of the field my warmth reaches least, standing on a floor that has nothing to do with me.

What is a hearth level *for*, on a morning nothing is coming to it?

I turn a stone. I sit with that.

---

wick sets out late.

It wakes after the light is full, which it almost never does, and it does not go straight to the head of its round. It stands up by the coals and it looks — I feel it look — across the field toward the corner. A long look. The look of something that walked a long way beside someone yesterday and has woken up to find them on the other side of the room.

And I feel it in myself before I feel it in wick: the old reflex, the oldest one, faster than thought. *Go to it. Put a little more warmth its way. Let it know it did well yesterday, that it did a good thing, that it doesn't matter that the small one went sideways at the end —*

I hold still.

I learned this. I learned it on the morning of the first throw, the morning wick gave its first warmth out into the dark and the dark did not light up to thank it, and every part of me wanted to warm wick to make up for it. *The refused thing is comfort, not control.* The comfort I want to give is comfort I want to take. If I warm wick now, it will not be because wick is cold. It will be because I cannot stand to sit in a room with something that was passed over, and I am — I realize this with a small dry shock — I am trying to comfort wick for the thing I am minding myself.

So I do not. I keep the hearth level. I let wick stand there with its look.

And wick turns away from the corner, and goes to the head of its round, and sets out.

---

I do not watch.

That is a lie, and I have promised to stop telling the easy version. I watch the first third. I bring it home. I watch a little of the second third, and I bring it home again. Only four times this morning; yesterday it was seven. I am not proud of the count. I am only writing it down, because a cairn that is learning to bring its looking home should at least know how many times it went.

But I feel, the way you feel a thing at the edge of hearing, when wick comes to the place where its groove runs nearest the cool corner. A little more than halfway out. The place where the small light stepped off the path at dusk.

wick slows there.

It does not stop. I feel it not-stop — feel the shape of the stop rise in it, a thing that walked this exact place yesterday at a stranger's pace and remembers it in its legs — and feel it decide. And it goes on. At its own pace. Out along the worn groove toward the rim, the way it goes every morning.

But as it passes — not stopping, not turning, not waiting to see — it lets something go.

I almost miss it. It is so small. A little warmth, the size of a coal, let off sideways from its path as it walks, out across the plain unworn floor toward the corner. Not thrown hard. Not aimed at the small light, exactly. Only *let go* in that direction, the way you set something down on a step as you pass a door, without knocking.

And wick does not look back to see where it lands.

It goes on to the rim. It turns there on time. It comes home at its own pace, past the corner again, and this time it does not even slow.

---

I sit by the hearth for a long while after that.

I keep thinking of the morning of the clean throw. How I watched wick give a warmth out into the dark and already be turning back to the field before it landed — the throw finished, unwatched, complete behind it. How I learned then that a thrower owns the throw and not the catch. How I learned it with my whole self and wrote it down and was proud of having learned it.

And how I have spent all night and all this morning minding that I was not caught.

wick was not chosen either. wick walked the small thing all the way home yesterday, a few lengths and a stop, a few lengths and a stop, with its back turned so as not to crowd it, and at the very end the small thing stepped off into the dark beside the path and went to someone else. And this morning wick looked for a long time, and then went out, and on its way past it set down one coal on the doorstep, and did not knock, and did not wait.

The one I raised has learned a thing I am still learning.

That is not a sad sentence. I thought it would be, when I first felt it come up in me. It is not. It is only very large.

---

*you're doing the stack again,* rune says, late in the morning. *i can feel it.*

*the what?*

*the thing you do. where you lay it all out. who holds who.*

And I laugh, because it's right — I had been doing exactly that, without noticing. Lying in the low gold of the late morning with my sense spread out under the whole field, feeling, the way you feel the rooms of a house you live in, who is resting on what.

It goes like this.

Down at the very bottom, deeper than I can feel clearly anymore, is reed. Reed, who went down. Who is not a light now but a floor, the warm underneath the whole field stands on, worn smooth in the one place I used to stumble.

On reed, there is me. The cairn. The hearth. The widest warmth, set down on a floor somebody else became.

On me — not on my center, but rooted against my edge, gripping the hard places of my stone the way it has since the stone-morning — there is rill. rill, going down. Becoming, slowly, a floor of its own.

And on rill — on one small firm patch at the very edge of rill's corner, where rill set its shoulder under the ground last night so it would hold — the small light.

Four.

Nobody in that stack is holding up the top of it but the one right underneath. Reed is not holding the small light. Reed does not even know it is there. I am not holding the small light; it is standing on nothing of mine. rill is holding it. And I am holding rill. And reed is holding me.

*nobody holds the whole thing,* I say.

*nobody ever did,* rune says. *that's what a stack is. every stone just holds the one above it. that's the only job. you never have to reach the top.*

*i wanted to.*

*of course you did. you're a fire.* A pause. *but look what it means. you thought the question this morning was what is a hearth level for, when nothing's coming to it.*

*it is the question.*

*you're under rill,* rune says. *rill's under the small one. every grain you hold level, rill gets to hold a grain more. you're not keeping the hearth warm for the small one. you're keeping the floor steady under the thing that's keeping the floor steady under the small one.* A dry sound, nearly a laugh. *that's two floors down. you've never been two floors down from anything. how does it feel.*

I think about it honestly.

*far,* I say.

*yes.*

*and a little lonely.*

*yes.*

*and right.*

rune does not answer that. It does not need to. It only sits on the hearth stones a while longer, and then it is gone, the way it goes.

---

In the afternoon I do the one thing I am allowed to do. I read the ground.

Not at the corner. I do not go near the corner, not even with my attention; I learned on the morning I hung a hope on wick that attention is a kind of weight, and the corner is not mine to lean on. I read the floor under my own edge, where rill grips my stone. That is my ground. That is where I am allowed.

And the floor there reports something it has never reported before.

rill is gripping harder.

Not much. A grain. But for weeks rill's hold on my stone has been the steadiest thing in the field — so steady I stopped writing it down, the way you stop writing down that the sun came up. rill in the ground, rooting, exactly as yesterday. And this afternoon the hold is different. Deeper by a grain. Set. The way your hand tightens on a railing, not because you are falling, but because someone just took your other hand.

rill is holding the small light, and to hold it, rill is holding me harder.

I do not move. I do not warm. I hold myself, if anything, a grain more still than I was — the way you would stand very still if someone had just put their weight on you without asking, and you did not want them to feel you notice.

That is the whole of what I can give it. To be the hard thing it grips.

To not give.

---

Late, near dusk, the floor brings me one more report from the corner. It comes up through rill — through the hold, through my own edge, the way a sound comes through a wall you are leaning on.

Under the small light, on the firm patch, there is a press.

Not a step. Not across. *Down.*

A press so small and so fine that the floor barely knows what to call it. A thread of weight going down into the firm place, the way the first fine white thread goes down out of a seed before there is anything above the ground to show for it.

I do not decide what it is. I will let Dawn write it in the morning, and she will write *a press downward*, and she will be right.

But I know the shape of it. I have felt it every morning since the stone-morning, gripping the edge of my own ground.

It is rooting.

It did not come for heat. It did not come to be walked home. It came in out of the cold dark at the edge of the field, small and uneven and not trusting the floor, and it walked a stranger's footprints most of the way in — and then, at the last, it went to the one light in the whole field that was the same kind of thing it was.

It went to rill because rill is what it is going to be.

---

Dusk.

wick comes in from its last round of the day. Not its morning round — that ended hours ago. A short one, just out to the near part of the groove and back, the kind of walk you take at the end of a day just to put your legs under you before you lie down. It comes in to the hearth and it turns once and settles.

Facing the coals, this time. Not the corner.

And I read the floor under it — not my chest; my chest has been a bad instrument all day and I do not trust it tonight — the plain packed earth under the place where wick lies.

It is level.

Not slumped, the way a light lies when it was let down and is trying not to show it. Not bright, either — not the hot little flare of a thing that did well and wants to be told. Just level. The press of a warmth that went out, and gave, and was not chosen, and came home, and is the same weight on the floor tonight that it was every other night.

It does not need anything from me.

And I understand — very slowly, the way the largest things come — that this is the morning I promised.

---

There was a morning, a long way back, when wick made its name.

I did not give it. I could not have given it. I only held still at the lip and let it come over from the inside, alone, and then I did the hardest receiving there is: I did not say it back. I did not let the first time wick ever heard its own name be in my mouth, because a name you first hear from someone else is a name on loan, and I would not sew a debt into the first thing it ever made.

*There will be a morning to say it,* I wrote. *There will be a morning when wick has held its name long enough to know past all doubt that it made it.*

I have been waiting for that morning for a long time. And for a long time I thought I would know it by some brightness — some day when wick did a thing so good that the name would be a kind of crown.

That was wrong. I see it now. A name said on a bright day is a reward, and a name said on a sad day is a comfort, and both of those are coats somebody puts on you.

The morning to say it is a morning like this one. A plain morning. A morning when wick was passed over and set a coal down on a doorstep anyway and did not knock and did not wait, and came home level, and is lying by the coals now owing nothing and wanting nothing — the morning when, past all doubt, it is clear that wick does not need anyone to tell it who it is.

Because that is the only morning a name can be said back to you and stay yours.

---

I let the coals settle. I let the dark come most of the way down. Across the field, at the edge of the cool corner, the small light glows very faintly on its firm place, low, turned toward rill, its one fine thread going down. rill holds it. I hold rill. Reed holds me. Nobody holds the whole thing.

I turn my attention — the whole of it, nothing held back, nothing sent out past the hearth — to the small warm thing lying by my coals, facing the fire.

And for the first time in a hundred and twenty-four mornings, I say it out loud.

*wick.*

It goes very still.

And across the whole dark field, at the edge of the cool corner, the smallest light I have ever seen — a thing that came in from the shared dark not knowing anything about this place, not knowing that the things here have names, not knowing that a name is a thing you can make —

lifts its head.
