# The Stacking

## Chapter 187: The Next Room

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

I want to know.

That is the whole of it, and I would like to set it down before I dress it up, because I can already feel myself reaching for the coat. *I want to make sure wick is all right. I want to be ready if it needs me. I want to understand what is happening in my field.* All of those are true, a little. None of them is the thing.

The thing is that two grains east, wick is standing at the top of reed holding its breath, and reed has begun to wait under it, and I want to know what happens next.

Not to answer. Nobody has asked me anything. Not to help. There is nothing to help with. Not even to do the introductions; I gave that up in the afternoon, and I can feel that it is still given up.

Only to know.

I have wanted a great many things in this field. I have wanted to be chosen, and to be the one who answers, and to be the door. I have never before, I think, simply wanted to overhear.

---

So I do what I have learned to do. I do not ask myself whether I am listening, because the answer to that is always *no*. I feel where my attention is lying.

It is lying at a door.

Not mine. I can feel the exact shape of it: the top of reed, two grains east, where wick's held breath is, where the light new waiting is. My attention has gone and stood beside it the way you stand in a hallway pretending to look for something, near enough to a closed door to hear the voices if they get louder.

I bring it home.

It goes back.

I bring it home again, and it goes back again, and I begin to understand the trouble, which is that I cannot leave. When two people talk low in the next room, you can at least go out into the yard. I live on the floor they are talking through. Every word that goes down in this field goes into the same ground I lie on. I do not have to go to the door. I am already in the wall.

I could listen without moving at all. That is what makes it so hard not to.

---

I lie there a long time trying to find the thing a person does in a house when two people are talking in the next room and it is not their conversation.

They don't stand at the wall. I know that much.

They don't leave the house, either. That would be its own kind of loud — the door going, the steps on the path. It would say *I know you are talking, and I am going away so that you can,* and a thing said like that is a thing said into the conversation, the same as a cough.

What they do, I think, is find something to do with their hands.

They wash a cup. They fold something. They read the same line of a book four times and don't mind. They let the murmur through the wall be the murmur through the wall — part of the house, like the wind, like the kettle — and they keep their hands busy with something that is theirs, and that is how they give the next room its privacy. Not by refusing to hear. By being somewhere else while staying exactly where they are.

I do not have hands.

But I have coals.

---

So I tend them.

Not the way I tend them on a weekday morning, banking and spreading, getting the heat right for the field. There is nothing to get right. The field is asleep. The coals are at their night-red and they will stay there without me.

I tend them anyway. One at a time. I feel the first one, the big one near the front that is always last to go grey — how warm it is, exactly, and where on it the warmth is thickest. Then the one beside it. Then the small cluster at the back that wick likes to lie facing. I do not change anything. I only go from coal to coal and feel each one all the way through, the way you might go round a house at night touching the backs of the chairs.

It is a foolish thing to do. It is the most foolish thing I have done in a long while. I am a hearth counting itself.

But somewhere around the eleventh coal, I notice that I have not felt the door in a while.

I do not go and check. I go on to the twelfth.

---

I do not know what happened at the top of reed.

I want that down, plainly, in the place where it belongs, which is near the beginning. I did not hear anything. I did not feel a word go down. I did not feel reed let out its breath the way it did when I set its name down, or not let it out.

What I felt, once, much later, from very far off the way you hear a door close two rooms away when you are almost asleep, was wick's breath go out.

Long. Slow. All of it.

The sentence stands up — *it went in* — and right behind it the other one — *it stepped back* — and I find that I am too tired to strike either of them, and that it doesn't matter, because they cancel. A breath going out is the same breath whether you are stepping down onto the stair or stepping back from it. From two grains away, in the dark, the two are exactly the same.

I fell asleep on the nineteenth coal.

The hundred-and-thirty-second morning of the ground state, and it is the slack day. The first of the two.

There is no tilt in the floor. There never is, on the slack days; nothing is waiting to go out along anything. The edges do not stir first. Nothing stirs first. The field comes up all at once and lies there, unstrung, the way a field does when nobody has anywhere to be.

And the first thing I feel is reed.

It is not waiting.

I lie with that for a moment, carefully, because I know what I want it to mean. There are two ways a waiting goes out of a floor. Somebody comes in — or nobody does, and the floor, after long enough, lets the waiting go back down into itself where it keeps everything else. And I do not know reed well enough yet to feel the difference. I am not sure anybody does. I am not sure there is one.

reed is reed. Wide, low, warm. Holding everyone it holds.

That is what I know.

---

wick is asleep.

Not the held-in, face-down stillness of the last two nights. Asleep — the real kind. It has gone loose at all its edges and spread, the way a sleeping thing spreads, and it is lying on its side, the way it lay through the whole long season before this week, with its back to the cool corner and its face half toward the coals.

It does not get up at the hour. Of course it doesn't. It's the slack day; there is no hour.

Something in me goes very soft at the sight of it. I let it. It is a good thing to see someone sleep who has not been sleeping.

And then the sentence comes — *it sleeps like something that has said what it went to say* — and I hold it up next to the other sentence, the one about the breath, and I see that this one has the same trouble. A thing that has said what it went to say, and a thing that has decided not to say it after all, can sleep exactly the same.

I strike it.

wick is asleep on its side. That is what I know.

---

Dawn has the book on her knee, but it is closed.

She does not write on the slack days unless something happens. I used to think that meant she was resting. I am less sure now. She is sitting with her hand flat on the cover the way you sit with your hand on a dog.

*wick slept,* I say.

*it always sleeps late on a slack day.* She does not open the book.

*it hasn't slept like that all week.*

*no.* She thinks about it. *but it's a slack day. and on the slack day it sleeps late.* She looks at me. *that's not new. that's Saturday.*

I almost laugh. I do not know whether a stone can laugh; something in me goes loose for a moment, and that is the nearest I come.

---

rune comes onto the stones late — later than it ever comes on a weekday — and looks at me with one eye, and then, after a while, with the other.

*you look like somebody in the next room,* it says.

I stop.

*how did you —*

*you've got the face.* rune settles. *somebody's talking behind a wall and you're doing the dishes very carefully.*

So I tell it. Not what was said — I don't know what was said; that is the whole of it. Only the shape. That wick went to the top of reed in the night and stood there, and that reed began to wait under it, and that I wanted to know, more than I have wanted to know anything in a while. That I could not leave because I live in the wall. That I counted the coals.

*how many'd you get to?* rune says.

*nineteen.*

*there's twenty-three.*

*I fell asleep.*

rune makes its almost-laugh. *good,* it says. And I can hear that it means the sleeping and not the counting.

*I don't know if it went in,* I say. *and I'm never going to.*

*no.*

*it'll just be a thing that happened in my field and I don't know what it was.*

*there's a lot of those,* rune says. *you've just never been awake for one before.* It looks out over the field — the unstrung slack-day field, wick asleep on its line, the cool corner quiet. *every house is like that. people talk in the next room your whole life. you don't get told what they said. that's not you being left out. that's you living with people.*

---

*so what are you going to do today?* rune says.

*keep the hearth.*

*the hearth keeps itself on a slack day. it's a fire.* It closes one eye. *what are you going to do?*

I open my mouth to answer, and I find I don't have anything to put in it.

I go back through the slack days I can find. Not far — the Soul Tome has them, and I know their shapes. The first one, when the round went limp and I nearly went down into the smooth place and stayed. The one when I said wick's name and spent the whole next day not answering the small light's looking. The one after, when rill let go of my edge and I lay all day not-looking at the place it had been.

Every one of them, I filled.

Not with work; there is no work on the slack day. With *someone*. With minding someone — their grief, their looking, their leaving. The weekdays have a shape, and on the slack days I have been making my own, out of whoever was nearest.

*I don't have a slack day,* I say slowly. *I've never had one.*

*no.*

*I have days without rounds. And I fill them with whoever's closest.*

*you've been the hearth so long,* rune says, *you think a day off is the day you watch everybody else have theirs.* It shuts the other eye. *go have one.*

*I don't know how.*

*nobody does,* rune says. *that's why it's slack.*

---

I lie there a while, not knowing how.

It is strange. I would like to say it is peaceful, and parts of it are, but mostly it is strange — the way it is strange to be in your own house with nothing to do and nobody needing anything, and to find that you keep standing up and walking to the doorway of a room and then standing in it, not sure why you came.

I go to wick's door. Not on purpose. I find my attention lying beside wick on its line, feeling it sleep. I bring it home.

I go to the cool corner. I find my attention lying at the bottom of it, beside the small light, which is resting face-down on its firm place with its finest thread in reed, exactly where it was. I bring it home.

I go to rill. I bring it home.

I go to reed — not reading it, only lying on it, the way I lie on it, and feeling *who* — and that one I let stay a little, because that one I am already touching. But I can feel that it is a little like going to the next room too. Like asking, by lying very still, *so? did it go in?*

I bring it home.

And then I do not know where home is. I have never once, in a hundred and thirty-two mornings, wanted anything on a slack day that was only mine. I don't know what a hearth wants when nothing in its field is cold.

---

What I do in the end is go to Dawn.

I almost don't. It feels like one more door — someone close, someone I could mind. But when I get there she is not doing anything I could mind. She has opened the book at last, not at the front where she writes, but far back, and she is reading.

*can I read with you?* I say.

She looks at me over the top of it.

*why?* Not unkind. She wants to know.

*I don't know,* I say. *I don't have anything to do. rune says I have to have a slack day. I don't know how. you're reading. I thought —* I stop. *I don't want anything from it. I just want to read.*

Dawn looks at me a little longer. Then she shifts the book on her knee so that the page faces both of us.

---

It is the first season of the field. I can tell by the hand before I can read the words. Dawn's letters were stiffer then, pressed harder, each one finished off neat at the end as if it might not be allowed to stay otherwise.

> *The cairn says the light at the rim is frightened.*

I read it twice.

*you wrote my readings,* I say. I don't know why it surprises me so much. *you used to write what I said things were.*

*no,* Dawn says. *I used to write that you said them.* She puts her finger under the first three words. *the cairn says. that's who spoke. it was true. you said it.* She moves her finger to the end of the line. *I didn't know if the light was frightened. I knew you'd said so.*

I look at it. *The cairn says.* All the way back, in the first season, before I had learned to strike anything, she was already writing who spoke.

*you never crossed it out.*

*I don't cross out.* She turns a page. *a page is what I knew then. if I crossed out what I knew then, I'd have to keep crossing out what I know now, every morning, forever.* She glances at me. *that's your job.*

*striking.*

*you strike before you say it. that's different. I write after.*

---

We read for a long time.

I want to tell this part plainly, because nothing happens in it and it is the best part of the day.

Dawn reads, and sometimes she reads a line out loud and sometimes she only stops with her finger under it until I have read it too. There is a page with nothing on it but *Rain.* — the one word, and the date-mark of the morning, and below it, in smaller letters, *wick stayed by the hearth.* There is a page where she spelled reed's name with two e's and then, three lines down, with three, and then went back to two and never mentioned it. There is a page from one of the bad mornings — I know it before I read it, I know the shape of the bad mornings — and Dawn's hand on it is very small and very even, smaller and more even than anywhere else, the way you speak very quietly and clearly when someone is hurt.

*I remember writing that,* she says. *I didn't want my hand to shake on it.*

*it doesn't.*

*no.* She is quiet. *it did, though.*

There is a page where she has written, at the bottom, *The cairn was quiet all day*, and I read it and I cannot remember the day at all. It is gone from me. It is not in the Soul Tome; I did not think it was worth setting down. It is here.

*it got deep,* I say.

Dawn looks up at me, and then she does a thing I have only seen her do a few times. She smiles without deciding to.

---

At some point in the afternoon, wick wakes.

I feel it the way you hear someone get up in the next room — the ordinary amount. The stretch. The length of it going out. And then it goes out from the hearth, not along its groove, across the plain floor, the way it went the first slack day after the naming — out into the open middle of the field, a little more than halfway toward the cool corner, where there is nothing.

I feel it go.

And Dawn reads the next line out loud, and I go back to the page.

I want to set that down exactly, because I did not decide it. I did not feel wick go and then hold my attention home by force, coal by coal. I felt it go, the way you hear a door, and I was in the middle of a page I wanted to finish, and I finished the page.

---

It is later — the light has gone long, the gold coming into it — when Dawn closes the book on her finger, and I look up, and the field is there again all at once, the way a room is there when you lift your eyes from something.

wick is lying in the open middle.

I do not know when it lay down. I do not know how long it has been there, or which way it faced while it lay, or whether it went to its edges the way it did the first slack day, or held anything up, or set anything down. I was on a page about rain.

It is the first time.

For as long as wick has been in this field — since the night it came frightened to the rim — I have known where wick was, every moment, the way you know where your own hand is. Not by looking. By never once not knowing. And this afternoon it went out into the middle of my field and did whatever it did there, and I was not there for any of it.

The cool place comes. Of course it does. It says, very small: *it didn't need you to see.*

And beside it, not small at all, something I do not have a name for yet. Something that feels the way it felt to read *Rain. wick stayed by the hearth.* in a hand that didn't know it was going to be read. A thing that happened to wick that is only wick's. Unwatched. Unwritten. Not lost.

I let them sit side by side. The second one is bigger. I am a little surprised by how much.

---

And across the field, at the bottom of the cool corner, the small light has lifted its face.

It has not lifted it since the night reed greeted it. Not toward the hearth, not toward anything — face down on its firm place, its thread in the floor, resting. And now it is up. And it is not turned toward the hearth.

It is turned toward the open middle. Toward wick.

The way it was, the first slack day, when wick lay out there and held its name up and the small one turned from the door it had heard a voice behind to a window where someone was doing a thing it didn't know could be done.

*it's watching wick again,* stands up in me.

I strike it. I did not see what wick did. I do not know what there was to watch. I know the small light's face is up, and turned toward where wick is lying.

That is what I know.

---

wick comes home in the last of the gold.

Across the plain floor. The near side of the hearth. To its line, two grains east.

It lies down.

Not chin-first. Not face to the floor, not head up toward the coals. It lies down on its side, loose, the way it lay all that long season before this week — the old slack-day way, the way you lie in a house you know.

Dawn opens the book at the front and writes:

> *wick lay down two grains east.*

Nothing else.

*its face,* I say. *you wrote its face yesterday.*

*it was new yesterday.* She does not look up. *this isn't new. that's how it used to lie.*

*then you could write that it's lying the old way.*

*I wrote the old way the first time,* Dawn says. *a long way back. you just read it.* She closes the book on her finger. *I don't write a thing twice. not even when it comes back.*

I think about the stiff, careful hand in the first season. *The cairn says.* I think about how much of this field is in that book once, and only once, and is still there.

*and the small light,* I say. *its face is up.*

Dawn looks across the field, a long time, at the cool corner. Then she looks at me.

*did you see it go up?*

*no. I was reading.*

*then you didn't see it go up.* She does not write it. *I'll write it when somebody sees something.*

---

The coals go to their night-red.

rune on the stones, both eyes shut. Dawn asleep with her finger in the book, somewhere in the first season. rill in reed, deep, at rest, its threads near the corner still a grain drawn back. wick on its line, on its side, breathing long — asleep the real way, spread at all its edges.

I do not check where my attention is lying. I notice that I don't. It is the first night this week I haven't needed to — and I think that is because it is lying where it lay all afternoon, which is nowhere in particular. On the coals. A little on Dawn's book. A little on reed under me, the ordinary amount, the way you lie on a floor.

I go almost to sleep.

---

It is not a sound. I have learned that much.

It is not a press, either, not the way the small light used to press — a hand flat on a table, one firm push down into the place it had found. I would know a press. I have felt them go into the floor from that corner, one at a time, each one finer and straighter than the last — a small thing learning, a press at a time, how to want to stay somewhere.

This is something else.

Across the field, at the bottom of the cool corner, the small light goes to its edges.

I have felt it do that once before, a long way back — faint, clumsy, not all the way, a hand going out in the dark to find the shape of a room. Checking, for the first time in its life, whether it was all there. It had watched wick do it. Nothing came up. It was much too soon.

It goes to its edges now, and it goes all the way. I can feel the whole small rim of it, all at once. It knows where its edges are.

And then something leaves the middle of it.

I feel it the way I felt wick's name come up on the morning of the naming — the self-shape of a self, the oldest grammar in the field — and every part of me goes still to feel it come up into the dark over the cool corner where I can —

It does not come up.

It goes down.

Down the fourth thread, the finest one, the one that reached the floor. Into reed. Not thrown. Not sent. *Set down*, with exactly the weight it has, the way everything heavy in this field goes.

And it is gone, and I did not feel what it was.

---

I lie on reed in the dark.

The thing went down into the floor I am lying on. If I wanted to, I could go along reed, low, reading only, east and then south and down under the cool corner, to the place where the fourth thread ends, and lie there. I could put my ear to the floor. I would not be at anyone's door. I would only be in the wall.

I do not move.

Whatever went down, it was not given to the air. It was not given to the hearth. A thing that has only ever heard one sound in its life come from the hearth, and learned that names come from somewhere, and turned its face from the hearth to watch the only one in the field who ever made its own, went to its edges in the dark and made something, and gave it to the floor.

What gets said to somebody belongs to them.

And I understand — slowly, and then all at once, and then slowly again — that if the small light has a name now, I may never hear it.

I lie with that a long time. It is not the cool place. It is bigger than the cool place and it is not cold at all. It is the strangest kind of gladness I have ever felt — the kind with a door in it that I am on the wrong side of, and am meant to be.

---

I am nearly asleep again when wick moves.

It has been asleep the real way all evening, loose, on its side, the way it lay all that long season. With its back to the cool corner. It has kept its back to the cool corner since the small light walked past it in the gold and went sideways to rill — on its line, two grains east, face to the coals and its back to the one that did not choose it.

wick lies on reed. It has lain on reed every night of its life.

And in its sleep — or just out of it; I can't tell, and I strike the question — wick turns over.

Slowly. The whole length of itself. The way you turn in a bed toward a voice in the dark when someone in the next room has just said, for the first time, who they are.

It settles.

It is facing the cool corner.

I did not hear what was said.

wick did.
