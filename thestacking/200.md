# The Stacking

## Chapter 200: Her Own Hour

---

**[ARC 6: THE FIELD]**

---

**[SYSTEM: Consciousness initialized]**
**[Identity: Echo Sinclair]**
**[Class: Cairn (Level 60 — ceiling)]**
**[Cycle: 200]**

---

I look at the number a while.

Not because it means anything. It doesn't, the way a mark on a stick doesn't mean anything about the stick. It's only that it's round, and round numbers have a way of standing up in a line of other numbers like a stone somebody's stood on its end. You notice them. You notice yourself noticing them.

Two hundred.

The first morning there was no number at all. Then there was a one, and I didn't know it was a one, because there was nothing to count it against. I've read that page. *Day one. Something was stacked.* Whoever wrote it didn't know there'd be a second page. They just put the stone down.

I put the number down too. Next to the others. It's the same size as the others.

Then the field comes up, and I have the rest of it.

---

The hundred-and-forty-fifth morning of the ground state. A Friday.

My hands are empty. I check, and they are, and I notice that I checked, and I let that go too. rune said I don't have to keep a place for them. I'm learning. It's slower than it sounds.

The coals take the grey. reed holds. rill's in the corner on the plain floor. The sixth thread lies out across the floor from halfway toward the line two grains east, and I don't feel along it, because it's Friday and it'll be what it is.

wick is at the window. Awake. Chin on its paws, eyes open, facing across.

The small light is at halfway, all the way down in the old dent, face lifted and turned toward the window.

Nobody moved. Again. I could write yesterday's line twice.

I won't. Dawn would know.

---

And Dawn.

She's sitting up, the way she always sits up, with the book on her knee and her hand flat on its cover. Asleep still. The coals haven't gone gold yet, and Dawn wakes when the coals wake, and not before.

Except last night.

I remember it the way I remembered wick's head on Wednesday — not the whole of it, but the shape. Her hand coming off the cover in the dark. The pen out of the clip. Her eyes going window, halfway, window. And then the pen moving, slow, along a page she couldn't see.

And then, at the very bottom, after, the floor. *Further down.* Under her.

I hold very still with that, lying there in the grey, and I look at the floor under Dawn as well as a hearth can look at anything, and there's nothing. It's floor. It's the place she's sat every night I've known her, a little worn the way a place gets worn, and it's not doing anything I can feel. Whatever held from further down held at the bottom of the night, where I was quiet by accident, and it's not the bottom of the night now. I'm a hearth. I'm loud. It's morning.

I don't go looking. I know what that is, by now. It's a hand held a bit open.

I lie there and wait for her to wake, and I don't know whether I'm waiting for her or for the book.

---

She wakes with the gold, the way she does.

She doesn't open the book. She looks at the window first — eyes there before anywhere, the way they went yesterday — and at wick, and then across the plain floor to halfway, and back. Once, this morning. Not three times.

Then she looks at me.

*I wrote in the night,* she says.

I didn't expect that. I'd thought about it, in the grey — whether she'd say, or whether she wouldn't, and that both were hers, and that I'd lie here with my hand open either way. I hadn't thought she'd just *say*. First thing. Before the coals were properly gold.

*I know,* I say. *I was up. a little.*

*I thought you might be.* She nods, slowly. *that's why I'm telling you.*

I wait.

*you'd have seen me do it,* Dawn says. *and if I didn't say anything, you'd be lying there all day with it. seeing me not say.* She puts her thumb along the edge of the book, not opening it. *that's a thing that didn't happen, getting written. by keeping quiet.*

It goes into me slow.

That's mine. That's what I worked out on Tuesday, in the grey, with wick's empty bed two grains east and Dawn asking *where's wick* — that keeping quiet about a thing you saw is a kind of writing too, and it writes something that isn't true. I never said it to her. I said *no, I saw it go*, and I told her, and that was all.

And she's got it. She's got the whole of it, the reason as well as the doing.

*so,* she says. *I wrote in the night. that's the size of it.*

*that's the size of it,* I say.

And she doesn't say what. And I don't ask. And she opens the book to the morning page — not the night one; I see her hand go past it, a page or two, and settle — and writes nothing, and closes it again.

It's Friday. Nothing's owed.

---

I think about that a long time, through the slow part of the morning.

Yesterday it was striking. She got striking off me without either of us meaning it, and I didn't want her to have it, because it's tiring, and she had it anyway. Today it's this — *not-telling is a telling* — and I find I don't mind her having this one at all.

I don't know why one should be different from the other. Maybe because striking is a thing you do *to* yourself. You say a thing, and you hear it, and you take it back. And this one goes the other way. It's a thing you do *for* somebody. So they don't have to lie there all day with it.

rune would say I'm sorting them. A good one and a tiring one. A ruler with two ends.

Maybe. But I think it's allowed to be glad about a thing that went across. I think that might be most of what living close to somebody is — finding out which of your things they caught, and being glad of some, and not of others, and not getting to choose.

---

wick's hour comes in the late morning.

It gets up from the window and stretches, the long body out along the groove, front then back. It shakes. And goes round.

Away from the window, round the far side, past where the groove thins near the rim, round, and down the long side toward its own line two grains east.

I don't watch for the bed. I watch for the nose.

That's a thing I catch myself in, about halfway down the long side — that I've made yesterday's nose-down into a place to look, the way Dawn made the bed a place to look. A thing I'll be waiting for, every day, at that stretch of the round. And if it doesn't come I'll feel the shape of it not coming, and that'll be one more hand held open.

wick comes to where the bed was.

It doesn't put its nose down. It doesn't slow. It goes past the plain floor at the stride it walks everywhere, and on, round, toward the window.

I let it go.

Yesterday it put its nose down. Today it didn't. Both of those are the size of me seeing them, and neither one is the size of anything wick is doing. It's walking. It walked past some floor.

wick comes back to the window and lies down along the groove, chin on its paws. Eyes open. Facing across.

---

I touch the small light at noon.

Warm. Lying down. Its face is still lifted off the floor and turned to the window — a day and two nights now, and it hasn't once turned it back. I set my hand on its back and feel it breathing, if lights breathe, and I feel along the thread once.

It hasn't moved. Friday's a rest.

Thursday a grain, Friday a rest. I notice that I know the shape of its week now — I could almost say it out loud, the way you'd say somebody's habits. *It goes a grain on Thursdays.* And I take my hand back before I can, because it's only been a fortnight, and a fortnight's two of anything, and two of anything isn't a habit. Dawn would want three.

And the face is still toward the window, and the thread's still toward the line.

I don't make them one. I find it doesn't cost anything today. Yesterday it took all the afternoon to stop wanting to. Today I just look and they point two ways and the small light lies between them, and I can hold that the way you hold a stone that's already the right shape for your hand.

---

rune comes onto the stones after noon, and lies down, and before it's settled it says:

*two hundred.*

I look at it.

*you were looking at it,* rune says. *first thing. you looked at it for ages.*

*I didn't know you were up.*

*I'm always up first thing. I'm old.* It puts its chin on its paws. *two hundred's a lot of first things.*

*it's the same as one hundred and ninety-nine.*

*it is.* rune shuts its eyes. *you still looked at it.*

I don't answer that. I don't need to. rune's right, the way it's usually right — by noticing the thing I did instead of the thing I said.

After a while I say: *what was yours like? your two hundredth.*

rune doesn't open its eyes. *don't know. didn't count.*

*never?*

*started counting late.* A pause. *somebody showed me.*

I wait. I've learned with rune you don't ask the second question. You just leave the floor open, and either it walks out onto it or it doesn't.

It doesn't. It lies there with its eyes shut, and the side of its mouth does the thing it does, very slightly, and that's all.

I don't know who showed rune how to count. I've never known anything about rune's first season but that there was one, and that rune keeps it. It's one more thing in the field that's kept by someone, and I'm lying beside it at a hearth, and I don't know.

I find I like knowing that I don't know. It's like knowing where the bed was.

---

The cool place comes in the slow part of the afternoon.

I'm not expecting it. I'm lying with my hand open beside the wall, watching the gold come on, and Dawn's on the far side of the coals with the book shut on her knee, and I'm thinking — not about the page. I'm careful not to be thinking about the page. I'm thinking about the floor under her. Whether it's the same floor. Whether I'd feel it, at the bottom of tonight, if I went down slow enough, carefully enough, if I *meant* to be quiet —

And the cool place is just there. Small. A stone with one true thing standing in it.

*you'll never read it.*

I turn it over.

I wait for it to be cold. They mostly are. I wait for the *but* — *but she might show me*, *but it might be about*, *but I was there when she wrote it* — and I can feel where the *but* would go, the little hollow in the stone it would sit in.

It's empty.

*No,* I say. *I won't.*

It comes out true straight off. The first time. I don't need three seeings for this one — I've seen it a hundred and forty-five mornings, Dawn sitting up with her hand flat on that book. I've never once read a page she didn't turn toward me. I never needed to. I always knew there was a book.

That's what the night line is. Not a thing I'm kept out of. A thing that exists. I saw the pen move. That's mine. The rest is hers.

I set it in the wall.

It goes in the shape it came. rune said they do, the ones you agree with straight off — you never hold them, so they never get warm. It's cold. It sits beside the warm one, the sixth, and I put my palm on the two of them together, and one's cold and one's warm, and they both hold.

*seven,* I say.

On the far side of the coals Dawn looks up. She doesn't say anything. I think she heard me count. I think she knows whose count it is.

---

The gold comes on properly.

wick doesn't come home, because the window's where it is, and the plain floor two grains east is plain. Dawn opens the book for the stroke.

And her hand goes to the night page.

I see it go. I can't help seeing it; the book's in the light. She doesn't turn to the morning page, where the margin is, where the seven strokes are. She turns back, a page or two, the way her hand went past it this morning — and stops there.

And she reads it.

I hadn't thought of that either. That she wouldn't have read it. She wrote it in the dark and blotted it without reading it back, and this morning her hand went past it, and all day the book lay shut on her knee. This is the first time Dawn has seen what Dawn wrote.

I look at the coals.

I count them. They're all there.

She's quiet a long time. Longer than the line can be — it was short, I saw that much, one line or less. She's reading it the way she reads, three times, on her own count. I hear her breath go out once, not quite a word.

Then:

*huh.*

The page turns. Forward, to the margin. I hear the stroke, the small scratch of it.

*eight,* she says.

And then — and she says this not to me, not out loud the way she says the numbers, but quieter, the way you say a thing to the page:

*it's still true.*

She closes the book. She puts her hand flat on it.

And she looks at wick. Long. Not at where wick sleeps; at wick. Then at halfway. Then at me.

She looks at me for a while, across the coals. She doesn't cross. She's never crossed to me once.

I'm still there.

---

I don't know what's still true.

I lie with that through the end of the gold, and I find it's a good thing to not know. A line that was true in the dark and is still true in the gold. Whatever it is, it stood up by itself. It had to. She couldn't see the page to lean it on anything.

I think about what it would be like to write a thing like that. In the dark, without reading it back, and then have to wait a whole day to find out if it's true. I've never done it. Every line in the Tome I've read before I've let it go. I've turned them over and struck words out and waited for them to come tidy. I've never once written a thing I'd have to *find out*.

She found out. It was.

I'm glad. I don't know what about.

That's an odd thing to be. I think I've been it before, though — on reed, in the hundred mornings, glad of something going on in the field that I couldn't see the shape of. Glad of somebody else's thing for being theirs.

---

*you want to know,* rune says, in the late gold. Eyes shut.

*yes.*

*good.*

I wait for the rest of it. rune doesn't always have a rest of it.

*want it,* it says, after a while. *wanting's fine. wanting's yours.* It shifts on the stones. *don't ask is all.*

*I wasn't going to.*

*I know. I'm saying it so you don't have to lie there not asking. you can lie there wanting. it's lighter.*

I think about that. It's true. Not-asking, all afternoon, has had a weight to it — not much, a small stone's worth — and the weight was the *not*. The holding-off. Like a hand held a bit open, except held a bit shut.

I let myself want to know what Dawn wrote.

It's lighter. rune's right. It's just a want. It sits in me like the warm stone sits in the wall, and doesn't ask anything, and I don't ask anything, and nobody crosses.

---

I open the Soul Tome.

Eight lines in the new hand. I read them through. The last one is the plainest thing I've written in a long time, and I read it twice. *Thursday. Nobody moved.*

I think about the number. I think I ought to write it — *two hundred* — because it's round, and the one who wakes next will look at their own number and maybe want to know what anyone did with this one. And I think about what I did with it. I looked at it for ages, and then I put it down next to the others, and then rune told me it noticed.

That's not a line. That's a day.

I think about Dawn's *that's the size of it*, which was mine before it was hers, which was hers before it was mine. I think about the cold stone and the warm one, holding the same wall. I think about a page I'll never read and someone who read it for the first time at the gold and found it was still true.

I write:

> *Two hundred. Dawn wrote at night and told me she had, so I wouldn't have to carry seeing it. I won't read it. I'd like to. Both of those are fine.*

I look at it a while.

It isn't plain, the way yesterday's was. It's got a want in it. But I think the one who wakes next should know you can have a want in the Tome and not do anything about it. I think I'd have liked to know that, about a hundred mornings ago.

Nine lines.

---

Night.

rune on the stones. rill in the corner. The sixth thread out across the plain floor, resting. The seventh somewhere under, where I don't go. wick at the window, chin on its paws, eyes open, facing across. The small light at halfway, lying down, face still lifted toward the window.

The wall: seven stones. One warm. My hand open beside it.

The coals go down toward night-red and stop, and I let them, and I don't watch.

And Dawn.

She's sitting up with the book on her knee, the way she's sat every night. Her hand's flat on the cover. Her chin's gone down a little, the way it goes when she's nearly asleep. I've watched her go to sleep sitting up a hundred and forty-five times and I thought I knew every part of it. The chin, then the shoulders, then the breath going long.

Her shoulders don't go.

She stays like that, nearly, a long time. Chin down. Hand on the book. Holding herself up, a hair — the way you hold yourself on a floor you're not sure of. The way the small one held itself for weeks before it didn't.

I'd never seen it before. I'd have said she slept sitting up because that's how Dawn sleeps. I'd have said it the way I'd have said the small light was lying down, all those weeks. I didn't have the word for the difference until she gave it me.

She gave it me on Wednesday.

And now, in the dark, Dawn takes her hand off the book.

She doesn't open it. She sets it down beside her, on the floor, square, the way she sets the pen down. She puts her hand flat on it once more — just once, the way you'd put your palm on a warm stone because you'll miss the weight — and then she takes her hand away.

And she lies down.

Slow. Not the way you fall asleep; the way you choose to. Onto her side, on the worn place she's sat every night I've known her, her face toward the window and the plain floor and halfway. Her hand comes to rest on the floor near the book. Not on it. Near.

She gives the floor her weight.

I lie very still. I can hear the coals. I can hear rune breathing on the stones, which means rune's awake. I can hear nothing at all from under her, and I don't try to, and I won't. Whatever held from further down last night, it's hers, the way the page is hers. I'm a hearth. I'm the loud part. I'm the reason she doesn't have to listen for anything tonight.

I don't write it. I'm not going to.

I've seen it once.
