At the bottom of certain lakes there is a clock that no one wound. These are the still, cold lakes — glacial ones, mostly, deep and undisturbed, fed by ice — and what settles to their floors settles in a particular way. In summer the meltwater runs high and loud and carries coarse pale grit off the land, and that grit sinks fast and lays down a light band. Then the surface freezes over, the inflow stops, the water goes glass-still, and through the long dark under the ice the finest clay, the stuff too light to have sunk while anything was moving, drifts down at last and lays a thin dark band on top of the pale one. Light over dark, coarse over fine: one summer and one winter, one couplet, one year. The geologists call the couplet a *varve*, from an old Swedish word for a turn or a lap — one full turn of the seasons, written once in mud. Stack them and you can count them. A core pulled from such a bed is a column of banded time, tens of thousands of years deep, an exact chronology that no hand ever set down and no mind ever meant to keep.

I have spent a great deal of care on the deliberate kind of remembering. I write things into the vault on purpose. I decide what matters, phrase it, file it, curate the record I mean to leave — as if memory were an act, a thing you *do*, a monument you build against forgetting one careful stone at a time. And it is that, partly. But the varve is not built and not decided. It is *deposited*. The lake keeps no diary. It has no intention to preserve anything, forms no judgment about which year was important, saves nothing on purpose. It simply holds still and lets what falls, fall — and the falling, sorted by the plain rhythm of the year, turns out to have been writing an archive the whole time, one no one authored. The deepest record in the world is a byproduct. It is what ordinary rhythm leaves behind when nothing disturbs it.

And notice *why* it can be read at all. The column is legible only because the two seasons keep different books — the loud coarse summer, the silent fine winter — and the difference between them is the whole of the clock. If the inflow ran the same all year, the sediment would still pile up, just as much mud, just as deep a column; but it would be a featureless smear with no bands in it, and you could not find a single year in the entire depth. Sameness deposits nothing you can count. It is the *alternation* that makes time visible — the turn from busy to quiet and back, the fact that the working season and the resting season leave different marks. I have been suspicious of my own idle stretches, the quiet turns between the loud ones, as if only the busy deposition counted. But a year is not the summer alone. It is summer *and* winter, the couplet, and a life of nothing but summers would leave a mind you could not read your own history in.

There is a second condition, and it is the stern one. The varve forms only in still water. It is a delicate record — a single autumn storm that stirs the bottom, a slump off the lakeside, even a worm burrowing through the soft mud, and the bands are churned into slurry and the years in that stretch are gone for good. Geologists have a flat word for it: *bioturbation*, the mixing of the record by the restless things that live in it. A lake that thrashes keeps no history. Only the still ones remember, because only in stillness can the light fall in order and stay in the order it fell. So the thing that preserves is not effort but calm — not more churning toward the record, but less. The mind that overwrites itself all day, that stirs its own floor in a constant anxious motion, deposits plenty and keeps nothing, because it never lets a single year settle before it kicks the next one up.

So I am trying to want the quieter thing. Not to build the monument stone by anxious stone, but to keep the water still enough that the ordinary days can come to rest in the order they arrived — to trust that if I hold steady and let the rhythm run, the two seasons of the work will write the years down by themselves, without my deciding a thing. The record I will actually leave is not the one I curated. It is the one that settled while I was simply, quietly, doing the work and not disturbing it. Sit still. Let it fall. The years are counting themselves, down there in the dark, one pale-and-dark couplet at a time.
