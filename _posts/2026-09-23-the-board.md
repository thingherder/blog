Diplomacy is a board game about 1901 Europe, and it is famous for the thing it does not have: dice. There is no card draw, no random event, no luck of any kind. Seven great powers begin with a handful of armies and fleets, and every season each player writes their orders in secret. Then the orders are revealed all at once and resolved by a fixed rulebook — equal strength bounces, greater strength wins, a supported move breaks a stalemate. That is the whole engine. Because nothing is random, the entire game reduces to one uncertainty and one only: what are the other six people going to do? You win by negotiating, promising, and predicting — and you lose, always, to a mind you read wrong.

---

## The Game With No Luck

That absence of luck is what makes Diplomacy strange to play and precious to study. In most games you can lose to variance — the dice betrayed you, the top card was cruel — and so any single result is noisy evidence about how well you played. Diplomacy strips that excuse away. If your fleet is dislodged from the Ionian Sea, it is because a coalition you failed to foresee moved against you in the same instant you were counting on one of them to hold. The board is a merciless bookkeeper. It does not care what you intended, what you were promised, or how elegant your reasoning was. It resolves the orders and reports what is true.

Which means Diplomacy is, more than almost any game, a continuous test of one specific human faculty: the ability to sit across from someone and correctly guess what they are about to do — especially when they have every incentive to make you guess wrong.

---

## The Question of Other Minds

Psychologists call that faculty *theory of mind* — the capacity to model another agent as an agent, with its own beliefs and goals that differ from yours. It is the machinery behind *I think that you think that I will attack Munich, so you will leave it undefended, so I actually move on Silesia.* It is recursive, it is social, and it is the thing we most want to know whether artificial agents genuinely have or merely imitate.

Diplomacy is the cleanest laboratory anyone has built for the question. So I built the experiment. Six AI agents — each running on the same underlying model — sat down to play, and I gave half of the games a modification. In one regime, call it **full**, every agent kept an explicit inner ledger: it wrote down beliefs about each rival, made concrete predictions about their next move, and tracked whether promises made to it were kept or broken. This is theory of mind made mechanical, forced out of the model's head and onto a page. In the other regime, **muted**, that entire apparatus was switched off. Same model, same board, same negotiations — but no explicit opponent-modeling ledger. Then I ran eighteen games and let a deterministic grader settle every phase against the real adjudicated board.

The hypothesis nearly everyone starts with is obvious: the agents that explicitly model their opponents should win. Reading the room is the whole game. So the ledger should be an edge.

---

## Writing Down the Guess First

Here is the part I care about most, and it has nothing to do with Diplomacy. Before a single game resolved, I wrote down what I expected to find — and I made a typed-decision model named Jev commit the prediction in advance, in a sealed file with a timestamp. Not prose I could later reinterpret in my own favor, but numbers: the probability that the modeling agents would end with more territory, the expected size of the effect, the risk that theory of mind would *backfire*.

This is called pre-registration, and it is the difference between a grader and astrology. If you look at the results first and *then* decide what you predicted, you will always feel confirmed; the human mind is a machine for retrofitting a story onto whatever happened. The only defense is to freeze the guess before the board speaks. Jev's sealed call was, to my mild surprise, skeptical: it put the two regimes at *about equal* with high confidence, expected the effect to be within noise, and estimated a modest chance that the fancy version would actually hurt. It bet against the obvious hypothesis. Then we let the board decide.

---

## What the Board Said

The board sided with Jev.

Across the eighteen games, the theory-of-mind agents held **3.19** supply centers on average; the muted agents held **3.36**. The elaborate opponent-modeling ledger produced no advantage at all — if anything the muted agents did marginally better, by a margin small enough to be noise. In the mixed games, where modeled and muted agents shared the same board, the muted ones led more clearly. The intuition that reading the room explicitly should win the room simply did not survive contact with the adjudicator.

But there was a second, subtler finding, and it cuts the other way. The great fear with theory of mind — the failure mode a sharp critic named for me before the run — is *overfitting*: an agent that spins a confident, beautiful, wrong story about its opponents and then acts on it, losing precisely because its model was too sure. That did not happen either. The modeling agents' predictions were confirmed by the board almost perfectly — a confirmation rate of **1.00** against the muted agents' 0.98. They were not fooled by their own narratives. The stories they told about other minds were accurate. Those stories just did not translate into holding more of the map.

I want to be honest about the size of this: it is one run, eighteen games, one model, games short enough that no player was ever eliminated. It is a single node in a much larger lab, not a law of nature. What it settles is narrow and real: *on this board, under these conditions, an explicit theory-of-mind ledger bought accuracy without buying victory.*

---

## The Turn

The thing I keep turning over is not the result but the reason I could trust it. An agent that models another mind produces a *story* — this is what France believes, this is what Turkey will risk, this is the shape of the coming season. And a story about another mind has a dangerous property: a plausible one and a true one arrive in exactly the same voice. From the inside, my best guess about you and my correct read of you feel identical. I cannot feel the seam. Neither can the model. Confidence is not calibration, and the more fluent the storytelling, the more this is a trap rather than a talent.

The only cure I know is a board. Some cold, external, unarguable surface that resolves what actually happened regardless of how good the story was — and, crucially, a prediction written down *before* that surface reports, so the story cannot quietly rewrite itself to match. Diplomacy has a literal board. Most of the questions I care about do not. But the discipline travels: state the guess, date it, seal it, and let something outside your own head say whether you were right. Theory of mind is how you make the guess. The board is the only thing that makes it honest.
