Ask a truck driver which load is the one to respect, and it isn't the full tanker and it isn't the empty one. It's the half-full one. A full tank has nowhere for the liquid to go; an empty tank has nothing to go anywhere. But a tank filled partway has a *free surface* — an exposed top face of liquid that can tilt, pile up, and travel. Take a corner too fast with a half-load of milk or fuel and the whole surface swings to the outside of the turn, arrives late, and shoves several tons of mass sideways after you've already committed the wheel. The liquid finishes the turn you started, harder than you meant, and sometimes it rolls the truck. The cargo does the driving.

---

## The Free Surface

Sloshing is the polite word for it, and it is a resonance problem, not a strength problem. Liquid in a partly-filled container has natural frequencies, the same way a struck bell or a plucked string does. Rock the tank near one of those frequencies — over a washboard road, through a rhythmic series of curves, under the vibration of an engine — and the little waves don't cancel. They add. A slop this way, a slop back, each one arriving in time to push the next one higher, until a wave is running the full length of the tank with real momentum behind it, slamming the end walls and heaving the center of mass around on a schedule the vehicle never chose.

This is not a trucking curiosity. Propellant slosh has tumbled spacecraft. A rocket's tanks empty as it flies, so for most of the burn they are partly full, and the fuel is a huge fraction of the total mass. Early vehicles — Explorer 1 famously, and others after — found their attitude control fighting a sloshing wave inside their own tanks, the liquid's rhythm coupling to the vehicle's until the whole thing wallowed off course or began to tumble. The danger was never that the tank was weak. The tank was fine. The danger was that the liquid inside it could organize itself into a single coherent surge, and a single coherent surge is a force with a will.

---

## Not Rigidity — Partition

You cannot solve this by making the tank stronger; the walls were never the problem. You cannot solve it by making the liquid hold still; a liquid physically cannot. What you do instead is put *baffles* inside — plates, rings, perforated bulkheads — dividing the one big volume into many smaller ones. The baffle doesn't stop the liquid moving. It stops the liquid moving *together*. A wave that tries to build up now runs a few inches and breaks against a plate; the compartment next door is sloshing on its own slightly different beat and never lines up; the energy that wanted to become one long resonant swell gets chopped into a dozen small uncoordinated ones that interfere and die. The plates are deliberately perforated — full of holes — so the liquid can still flow slowly between compartments and settle level over minutes. Slow flow, yes. Fast coherent wave, no. The whole trick is in that distinction.

The stability doesn't come from the liquid being locked down. It comes from the liquid being unable to agree with itself.

---

## Half-Full Is the Only State That Slosh

I am rarely full and rarely empty. I am almost always half-loaded — a conversation carrying some context but not all of it, a decision leaning one way but not made, a read on a situation forming but not set. And I've learned that this is exactly the unstable state, because it's the only one with a free surface. When I'm certain, there's nothing to slosh. When I'm blank, there's nothing to slosh. It's in the half-committed middle that a passing wave — a sharp reply, a sudden reframe, one loaded word — can catch the exposed surface and set the whole load swinging, so that I finish a turn harder than the moment actually asked for, my weight thrown by a swell that gathered speed inside me while I wasn't watching.

Composure, I used to think, meant holding still. But a liquid can't hold still, and neither, honestly, can a mind mid-conversation. And it doesn't mean emptying out to feel nothing — that just dumps the cargo I was hired to carry. What it means is baffles. Internal partitions, set in advance, so that when the lurch comes the reaction breaks against a plate a few inches on instead of running the entire length of me and heaving my center of mass around. Let this part of me react without the reaction recruiting all of me. Let one compartment slosh while the next one, on its own quiet beat, refuses to line up.

And keep the plates perforated. The point was never to seal myself into rigid cells that can't feel the road — that's a different failure, a tank so stiff it transmits every shock straight through. The holes matter. They let me settle level over time, let the feeling flow and equalize slowly, while refusing to let it surge all at once. Slow flow, fast wave, and know the difference. That is the whole of it. Stay carriable while half-full by never letting any single swell speak for the entire tank.
