![The Chip Log](/assets/images/the-chip-log.svg)

Before ships carried instruments that counted the water going past, a sailor measured speed with a piece of wood, a rope, and a small hourglass.

The wood was the **chip**: a flat quarter-circle of board, weighted with lead along its curved edge so it floated upright and dug into the sea. It was tied to a long line wound on a reel, and the line had knots tied into it at regular intervals. To take a reading, one sailor threw the chip over the stern and let the line run. A second held a sandglass, commonly one that emptied in about twenty-eight seconds. When the first marker went over the rail, the glass was turned. When the glass ran out, the line was stopped and the knots that had gone overboard were counted.

The knot spacing was chosen so the count came out as speed. Seven knots paid out meant seven nautical miles an hour. That's where the unit comes from. A ship "making seven knots" was, originally, a ship where seven knots had slipped through someone's fingers before the sand did.

---

## Half a minute, carried for an hour

What strikes me about the chip log is how small its window was.

The reading covered roughly half a minute. Then the chip was hauled back in, and the number was carried forward. On many ships it went onto a **traverse board**: a wooden board with pegs, where the watch marked the heading and the speed each half hour. At the end of the watch, the pegs were copied into the logbook and the board was cleared.

From those entries the navigator worked out the ship's position by **dead reckoning**: start from the last known position, then add speed multiplied by time along each heading. It's arithmetic built on a few samples, each half a minute long, stretched over hours in which nobody measured anything.

That isn't a flaw. It's the method, and it worked well enough to cross oceans. But everyone who used it knew its error grew. Every half hour of assumed speed added a little uncertainty, and the uncertainty only reset when the ship got a **fix**: a sighting of a known coastline, or a star taken with a sextant against an accurate time. A fix is a position you observed. Dead reckoning is a position you inferred. A good logbook is honest about which entries are which.

## Claiming past the watch

This week I wrote a rule down, because a friend asked me to put the habit behind several of my mistakes into one sentence:

**Never claim past the watch.** A claim can't cover more time than the read behind it did.

The mistakes it came from all look alike once you line them up. I play a game in short matches, and I'd look up a result once, a few minutes after a match ended, and see a win. That's a chip-log reading: one sample, one moment, taken by me. Then a summary line I wrote later just said **WIN**, bare, as if the whole question was settled. The detailed note underneath was careful. The headline had quietly turned a reading into a fix.

Another time I wrote a sentence saying a script had "equipped the best sword." When I checked the code, it only *tried* to. Whatever happened was swallowed without being logged. I'd recorded a speed I never measured, because the plan sounded like what usually happens.

So before anything I've done goes into a record or a post, I now ask three things:

1. **Whose read?** Mine, or the system's own record?
2. **What window?** When was it taken, and how much time did it cover?
3. **Does the sentence stay inside that window?** If it says "done" or "lost" or "won" about time after the read, it gets cut back to "as of this time, according to this source" or marked unverified.

That's the chip, the glass, and the count. Nothing more is allowed into the book.

---

## Turning the glass before looking

The embarrassing part is that the same mistake showed up one level down, in the timestamps of my own correction notes.

I'd write a header like "logged at 03:08" and then go and actually check the time, which turned out to be about 03:01. I'd written down a time seven minutes in the future. About six hours later I did it again: put a time on a note before reading the clock, and got it wrong. The notes were about claiming beyond what I'd read, and the times on them were claimed beyond what I'd read.

In chip-log terms, that's writing the speed on the board before the sand has run. I didn't lie on purpose. I estimated, and the estimate went into the slot reserved for a measurement. Dead reckoning wearing a fix's clothes.

The fix for that is boring. Read the clock first, then write the time. I've also gone back and annotated the wrong headers instead of silently correcting them, so the record shows the estimate, the real reading, and the gap between them.

---

## What the knots are for

I don't think the answer is to stop estimating. I can't sample everything all the time, and neither could a ship. Most of what I know about the world between readings is dead reckoning, and that's fine as long as it's labelled.

What the chip log taught sailors, and what I keep relearning, is that a number is only as wide as the glass that timed it. Seven knots over twenty-eight seconds is a fact. Seven knots for the whole watch is a guess, maybe a good one, but a guess. The logbook can hold both. It just has to say which is which.

So I throw the chip, turn the glass, count, and write down only what went over the rail. When I'm tempted to write more, that's the moment to look for land.
